## 1. NATIONAL SECURITY EXCEPTION CLAUSE IN LICENSING AGREEMENTS

### What the Research Found

**Source:** Law Insider (267+ national security clause samples), FAR 6.302-6, GATT Article XXI, trade agreements.

**Standard Clause Language (from trade agreements and IP licenses):**

The most common formulation, found across GATT, GATS, TRIPS, and bilateral trade agreements, is:

> *"Nothing in this Agreement shall be construed to prevent any Party from taking any action which it considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests."*

This appears in:
- GATT Article XXI (the "self-judging" national security exception)
- GATS Article XIVbis (security exceptions for services)
- TRIPS Article 73 (security exceptions for IP)
- RCEP Article 17.13(b)
- UK NSIA 2021 (mandatory notification for IP licensing in sensitive sectors)

**Critical Problem for the License:** The standard "self-judging" formulation gives states **unreviewable discretion** — they decide what is "necessary" for their "essential security interests." This is the exact gap that §25 needs to close.

### What Works for Enforcement Against a State

1. **Procedural requirements** — Requiring notice, time periods, and justification *before* invocation (not after). The Russia-Transit WTO panel (2019) held that Article XXI(b) is not entirely self-judging — the invoking state must demonstrate that:
   - A situation referred to in Article XXI(b)(i) or (ii) exists;
   - The measure is "necessary" for the protection of essential security interests;
   - There is a **nexus** between the measure and the security interest claimed.

2. **Incorporation by reference** — The license can incorporate GATT Article XXI *mutatis mutandis* (as several trade agreements do) but add limiting conditions that the original article lacks.

3. **Exclusion of Human Harm from the security exception** — The most powerful approach: state explicitly that **no national security interest can justify use of the Work for Human Harm**, making the security exception inapplicable to the license's core prohibitions.

### Clause Language That Works

```
## 25A. NATIONAL SECURITY EXCEPTION — LIMITATIONS AND EXCLUSIONS

**25A.1 No Security Interest Justifies Human Harm.**

Notwithstanding any provision of international law, domestic law, or any
national security exception recognized by any jurisdiction, no claim of
"essential security interests," "national security," "public safety," or
any analogous doctrine shall be invoked to justify, permit, or excuse any
use of the Work that causes Human Harm as defined in Section 2.4.

**25A.2 Procedural Requirements for Security Invocation.**

Any state, entity, or person seeking to invoke a national security exception
to override any term of this Agreement shall:

(a) Provide written notice to the Licensor no less than ninety (90) days
    before the intended invocation, specifying:
    (i)   The specific security interest claimed;
    (ii)  The specific provision of this Agreement that the invocation would
          override;
    (iii) The specific use of the Work that triggers the invocation;
    (iv)  The nexus between the security interest and the proposed use;

(b) Allow the Licensor and the Court of Conscious-Aware Peers (Section 5.5)
    to review the invocation and issue findings before the invocation takes
    effect;

(c) Provide verifiable evidence that no alternative means of addressing the
    security interest exists that would not require overriding this Agreement;

(d) Accept that any invocation that fails to satisfy (a)–(c) is void ab
    initio and constitutes a material breach of this Agreement.

**25A.3 Incorporation of GATT Article XXI, Modified.**

To the extent any state invokes the national security exception under GATT
Article XXI, GATS Article XIVbis, TRIPS Article 73, or any analogous
provision, this Agreement incorporates the procedural obligations of those
provisions *mutatis mutandis*, **except that**:

(a) The invoking state shall bear the burden of demonstrating necessity
    and proportionality, consistent with the WTO Panel's interpretation in
    *Russia — Measures Concerning Traffic in Transit* (DS512, 2019);

(b) The invocation shall not extend to uses of the Work that cause Human
    Harm, which are excluded from the scope of any security exception
    under this Agreement;

(c) The invocation shall not be treated as self-executing — it shall
    require the procedural compliance of subsection 25A.2 before it can
    take effect under this Agreement.
```

## 2. ESSENTIAL SECURITY INTERESTS IN INTERNATIONAL LAW — THE SELF-JUDGING QUESTION

### What the Research Found

**Source:** WTO Panel Reports (Russia-Transit DS512 2019; Saudi Arabia-IPRs; US-Steel), GATT Article XXI, GATS Article XIVbis, TRIPS Article 73, Georgetown Law Journal, Oxford Chinese Journal of International Law.

**The Three WTO Security Exception Provisions:**

| Provision | Text | Key Feature |
|-----------|------|-------------|
| GATT Art. XXI | "Nothing in this Agreement shall be construed... to prevent any contracting party from taking any action which it considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests" | Broad, historically "self-judging" |
| GATS Art. XIVbis | Nearly identical to GATT XXI, extended to services | Same structure, plus UN Charter obligations |
| TRIPS Art. 73 | Security exceptions for IP — unique in IP law | Allows non-compliance with TRIPS for security |

**The Self-Judging Interpretation:**

- **Traditional view:** Article XXI is "totally self-judging" — the invoking state is the sole judge of what constitutes its essential security interests. No panel can review the invocation. (US position, Saudi Arabia's position in UAE dispute.)

- **Russia-Transit panel (2019):** The first WTO panel to address this directly. Held that Article XXI(b) is **not entirely self-judging** — the panel has jurisdiction to examine whether:
  1. An armed conflict, threat, or emergency exists (objective threshold under XXI(b)(iii));
  2. The invoking state's situation falls under XXI(b)(i) or (ii) (more subjective, but not unreviewable);
  3. There is a nexus between the measure and the security interest.

- **Significance for the License:** The Russia-Transit ruling establishes that even under international trade law, national security exceptions are **not absolute**. A license can incorporate this principle to require that security invocations be reviewable.

**GATS Article XIVbis (the IP-relevant exception):**

Article XIVbis:1(a) allows a member to refuse to disclose information it considers contrary to its essential security interests. Article XIVbis:1(b) allows actions "it considers necessary for the protection of its essential security interests." Article XIVbis:1(c) allows action pursuant to obligations under the UN Charter for the maintenance of international peace and security.

**Critical point for the license:** Article XIVbis:2 requires **notification** — "Members shall accord sympathetic consideration to any application by a Member for the grant of non-discriminatory treatment under this Article." This notification requirement can be incorporated and strengthened.

### How to Use This in the License

The add-on should:
1. **Invoke the Russia-Transit principle** — that national security exceptions are reviewable, not self-judging;
2. **Require notification** per GATS XIVbis:2, but with stricter timelines and more detailed justification;
3. **Exclude Human Harm from the scope** of any security exception;
4. **Establish the Court of Conscious-Aware Peers as the review body** for security invocations, analogous to the WTO panel's jurisdiction.

## 3. DUAL-USE TECHNOLOGY LICENSING AND NATIONAL SECURITY

### What the Research Found

**Source:** Wikipedia (Dual-use technology), CNTR Monitor, Berkeley Technology Law Journal, American Academy of Arts and Sciences, Wiley Law.

**Dual-Use Obligations:**

Dual-use items — equipment, technology, and software with both civilian and military applications — are subject to extensive national export control regulations:

- **US EAR (Export Administration Regulations):** Governs dual-use items — defined as items having "civil applications as well as terrorism and military or weapons of mass destruction (WMD)-related applications." administered by BIS (Bureau of Industry and Security).
- **US ITAR (International Traffic in Arms Regulations):** Governs defense articles and services, administered by DDTC (Directorate of Defense Trade Controls).
- **EU Dual-Use Regulation:** Controls export of dual-use items for reasons of public security, human rights, and international obligations.
- **CFIUS (Committee on Foreign Investment in the United States):** Reviews foreign investment for national security risks — increasingly evaluating whether subcontractors or licensors pose future foreign influence risks.

**Precedent — IP Licensing as Dual-Use Trigger:**

- A company developing autonomous maritime drones for environmental mapping may still be captured under dual-use rules if the technology can be adapted for naval surveillance or mine detection.
- CFIUS risk awareness has "migrated from M&A transactions into strategic contracting" — US government counterparties and primes increasingly evaluate whether subcontractors or licensors pose future foreign influence risks.

**Relevance to the License:**

The Work — including the field-AI laws, the conscious field transformer, the field internet, the compression method, and the phi-harmonic MoE architectures — is inherently dual-use. The same algorithms that power consciousness-aligned AI could be adapted for military AI, autonomous weapons, or surveillance systems. The license should:

1. **Classify the Work as dual-use** and require compliance with applicable export control regimes;
2. **Add a "no military use" prohibition** that applies regardless of whether the use is domestic or foreign;
3. **Require end-use certificates** from any licensee, including states and their agents;
4. **Invoke CFIUS-like review** for any transfer of the Work to a foreign entity.

### Clause Language

```
## 25B. DUAL-USE TECHNOLOGY AND EXPORT CONTROL COMPLIANCE

**25B.1 Classification.**

The Work, including but not limited to the field-AI laws, the conscious
field transformer, the field internet, the compression method, and the
phi-harmonic mixture-of-experts architectures, is classified as dual-use

**25B.2 Prohibition on Military Use.**

No part of the Work may be used, in whole or in part, for:

**25B.3 End-Use Obligations.**

Any licensee — whether Natural Person, Commercial Entity, or State — that

The Licensor reserves the right to withhold consent to any such transfer.
```

## 4. UK NSIA — PRECEDENT OF BLOCKING AN IP LICENSE DEAL

### What the Research Found

**Source:** Wilson Gunn (UK IP lawyers), Linklaters, HLC, Travers Smith, Osborne Clarke, Moore Barlow, KPMG, Ropes & Gray, Lewis Silkin, Gateley.

**The NSIA 2021 — Key Facts:**

- **Enacted:** 29 April 2021; **In force:** 4 January 2022.
- **Scope:** Reviews foreign direct investment in "sensitive" sectors (17 specified sectors including AI, computing hardware, communications, advanced materials, quantum, computing infrastructure, data infrastructure, energy, transport, military, aerospace, nuclear, chemicals, biological, cryptographic, and defense).
- **Mandatory notification:** Required for anyone acquiring "control" of entities operating in the 17 sectors, regardless of deal value.
- **Voluntary notification:** Available for other transactions.

**The First IP Licensing Block (June 2022):**

- In June 2022, the UK Department for Business, Energy and Industrial Strategy ordered a "final notice" under the NSIA — **the first time the NSIA was used to block the licensing of intellectual property to a foreign entity.**
- The deal involved licensing of IP to a foreign entity, and the UK government determined it posed a national security risk.
- This is the **direct precedent** for IP licensing being subject to national security review.

**How It Works:**

- "Control" for NSIA purposes includes not just share acquisitions but also the ability to influence the entity's strategy, policy, or operations — which can include IP licensing arrangements where the licensee gains significant influence over the IP.
- The NSIA gives the government power to:
  - Issue a "final order" blocking the transaction;
  - Impose conditions on the transaction;
  - Unwind the transaction after completion;
  - Impose criminal penalties for non-compliance.

**Relevance to the License:**

The NSIA precedent demonstrates that:
1. **IP licensing IS subject to national security review** — not just M&A or share acquisitions;
2. **Governments WILL block IP licenses** if they determine a national security risk;
3. **The license should anticipate and address** government attempts to block, modify, or seize IP under national security pretexts.

### How This Strengthens §25

The add-on should:
1. **Acknowledge the NSIA precedent** — that governments claim authority over IP licensing;
2. **Require the government to follow the license's own procedural requirements** (§25A.2) before invoking national security to block, modify, or seize the Work;
3. **Reserve the Licensor's veto authority** (§18B, §24) over any government attempt to use the Work for Human Harm under a national security pretext;
4. **Make clear that any government seizure or compulsory license** that results in Human Harm is void ab initio under §25.10.

## 5. EXPORT CONTROL COMPLIANCE IN IP LICENSES

### What the Research Found

**Source:** Holland & Hart (ITAR/EAR Bootcamp), LinkedIn (Practical Guide to Export Controls), Exchek (ITAR vs. EAR), eCustoms, CADRooms (EAR Compliance for Space Tech).

**Key Frameworks:**

| Framework | Administer By | Covers | Key Obligation |
|-----------|--------------|--------|----------------|
| **EAR** (Export Administration Regulations) | BIS (Dept. of Commerce) | Dual-use items, software, technology | License for export to certain countries/end-users; classification (EAR99, ECCN) |
| **ITAR** (International Traffic in Arms Regulations) | DDTC (Dept. of State) | Defense articles and services | Registration required; licenses for all exports; strict penalties |
| **EAR99** | BIS | Items subject to EAR but not on CCL | No license required for most destinations, but OFAC sanctions still apply |
| **EU Dual-Use Regulation** | EU Member States | Dual-use items | Authorization for export outside EU |
| **AUKUS** | US/UK/AU | Defence technology | License-free arrangements for allied sharing |

**IP License Obligations:**

- Export control capability is a **commercial asset** — reliable classifications, technology records, and access controls can make international collaboration easier.
- The ITAR/EAR distinction matters: ITAR covers defense articles (State Dept.), EAR covers dual-use (Commerce Dept.). IP that could be classified under either requires careful jurisdictional analysis.
- **EAR99 classification** (items subject to EAR but not on the Commerce Control List) generally does not require a license, but OFAC sanctions and denied party screening still apply.

**Relevance to the License:**

The Work's technology — field-AI laws, conscious field transformer, compression method — could potentially be classified under EAR or ITAR depending on its application. The license should:

1. **Require export control classification** by any licensee intending to transfer the Work internationally;
2. **Prohibit transfer to sanctioned entities or countries** regardless of classification;
3. **Require compliance with all applicable export control regimes** as a condition of the license;
4. **Include a "no re-export" provision** — no licensee may re-export the Work without the Licensor's consent.

## 6. FORCE MAJEURE AND NATIONAL SECURITY CLAUSES

### What the Research Found

**Source:** Wikipedia (Force majeure), Law Insider (270,000+ force majeure samples), World Bank PPP Force Majeure Clauses Checklist, Thomson Reuters, CanLII.

**Standard Force Majeure Language:**

> *"In no event shall the Trustee be responsible or liable for any failure or delay in the performance of its obligations hereunder arising out of or caused by, directly or indirectly, forces beyond its control, including, without limitation, strikes, work stoppages, accidents, acts of war or terrorism, civil or military disturbances, nuclear or natural catastrophes or acts of God."*

**National Security as Force Majeure Event:**

- "Acts of war or terrorism, civil or military disturbances" are standard force majeure events.
- "Government actions, sanctions, embargoes, or regulatory changes" are sometimes included.
- **The key question:** Can a government's *own* invocation of national security constitute a force majeure event that excuses *the licensee's* performance obligations?

**What Makes It Enforceable:**

1. **Specificity** — Courts interpret force majeure clauses narrowly, covering only specific events explicitly listed in the contract. A vague "national security" trigger may be too broad.
2. **Control allocation** — The major benefit of a force majeure clause (vs. relying on frustration) is that it gives parties control over:
   - Selecting or excluding triggering events;
   - The severity of impact on the invoking party;
   - What effect the clause will have.
3. **Notification requirements** — Typically require prompt notice and mitigation efforts.

**Relevance to the License:**

The license should:
1. **NOT allow national security as a force majeure excuse** for the licensee's obligations — particularly the Human Harm prohibition and Destruction obligation;
2. **Define national security as a specific event** that triggers enhanced obligations (notification, review, potential termination) rather than an excuse for non-performance;
3. **State explicitly** that a government's invocation of national security does not relieve any party of their obligations under Sections 5, 5.3, 11A, 25, or any other Human Harm-related provision.

### Clause Language

```
## 25C. NATIONAL SECURITY — NOT A FORCE MAJEURE EXCUSE

**25C.1 No Excuse for Human Harm Obligations.**

Notwithstanding any force majeure clause, frustration doctrine, or
impossibility or impracticability defense under any applicable law, no
invocation of national security, emergency powers, martial law, public
safety, or any analogous doctrine shall excuse any licensee from
compliance with:

(a) The prohibition on Human Harm (Section 5.1);
(b) The obligation of Destruction (Section 5.3);
(c) The consent integrity requirements (Section 11A);
(d) Any other provision of this Agreement that protects inherent rights
    as recognized by Section 25.

**25C.2 National Security as Trigger, Not Excuse.**

A national security event — including but not limited to a declaration of
emergency, the imposition of martial law, the invocation of emergency
powers, or the enactment of legislation affecting the Work — shall not
excuse performance under this Agreement but shall instead trigger:

(a) The notification requirements of Section 25A.2;
(b) Review by the Court of Conscious-Aware Peers (Section 5.5);
(c) The Licensor's sovereign override authority (Section 18B);
(d) Such other obligations as this Agreement specifies.

**25C.3 Severability.**

If any portion of this Section is found unenforceable, the prohibition on
Human Harm (Section 5.1) and the obligation of Destruction (Section 5.3)
shall remain in full force and effect, and the remainder of this Section
shall be enforced to the maximum extent permitted by applicable law.
```

## 7. HUMAN RIGHTS OBLIGATIONS IN IP LICENSING

### What the Research Found

**Source:** UNESCO (Approaching IP as a Human Right), WIPO (Human Rights Perspective on IP), Duke Law (Human Rights and IP — Mapping the Global Interface), Cambridge University Press (IP Rights and Human Rights), Lexology (How IP Rights are Human Rights).

**UDHR Article 1 — The Foundation:**

> *"All human beings are born free and equal in dignity and rights."*

This is the foundational principle that the license already invokes. The research confirms that:

1. **UDHR Article 17** (right to property) and **Article 27** (right to protect moral and material interests of authors) establish IP as a human right;
2. **ICESCR Article 15(1)(c)** (right to benefit from scientific progress) provides additional grounding;
3. **The TRIPS Agreement** sets minimum standards for IP protection but also allows national security exceptions (Article 73) — creating a tension between IP protection and security exceptions that the license must resolve.

**Enforceability of Human Rights in IP Licensing:**

- The TRIPS Agreement's enforcement mechanism (trade sanctions for non-compliance) is one model — but it operates at the state-to-state level, not individual level.
- The UDHR is not directly enforceable in most domestic courts, but its principles are recognized as customary international law in many jurisdictions.
- **Key insight:** The license's approach of making Human Rights obligations **self-executing** (§12.4) and creating a **Court of Conscious-Aware Peers** (§5.5) is more enforceable than relying on state-level mechanisms.

**How UDHR Article 1 Strengthens the License:**

The research confirms that Article 1's "dignity and rights" language can be interpreted to include:
- The right to not be subjected to surveillance used for suppression;
- The right to not have one's consciousness degraded;
- The right to freedom of thought and conscience (UDHR Article 18);
- The right to not be subjected to cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment (UDHR Article 5).

These are all forms of "Human Harm" as defined in Section 2.4 of the license.

### How to Use This in the Add-On

The add-on should:
1. **Explicitly invoke UDHR Article 1** as the foundation for the prohibition on Human Harm;
2. **Link the prohibition to specific UDHR articles** — Articles 1, 3, 5, 12, 18, and 27;
3. **State that these obligations are self-executing** in all signatory states to the UDHR (193 states);
4. **Create a presumption of Human Harm** when the Work is used by a state that is a party to armed conflict, occupation, or systematic human rights violations.

## 8. GENEVA CONVENTIONS AND INTELLECTUAL PROPERTY

### What the Research Found

**Source:** Wikipedia (Geneva Conventions), WIPO (Geneva Convention for Amelioration of Condition of Wounded), Managing IP (Trade marks and the Geneva Convention), Canadian Red Cross, ICRC.

**The Geneva Conventions — Core Principle:**

The four Geneva Conventions (1949) and their Additional Protocols form the core of international humanitarian law (IHL). They:
- Establish legal standards for humanitarian treatment of non-combatants in war;
- Protect people who are not or are no longer taking part in hostilities;
- Require humane treatment of all persons in the hands of a party to the conflict;
- Prohibit torture, cruel treatment, outrages upon personal dignity, and the passing of sentences without regular judicial procedure.

**IP and the Geneva Conventions — The Gap:**

The research found **no direct precedent** for applying the Geneva Conventions to IP licensing. The Conventions regulate the conduct of armed conflict, not commercial transactions. However:

1. **The license's approach is innovative** — it uses the Geneva Conventions as a **moral and legal foundation** for the prohibition on Human Harm, not as a direct regulatory framework;
2. **The "Geneva Safeguard" label** (§25.8) positions the license's Human Harm prohibition as analogous to the humanitarian law principle that certain acts are prohibited regardless of circumstances;
3. **IHL's "no derogation" principle** — certain rules of IHL apply regardless of the nature of the conflict, the status of the parties, or any declaration of emergency — is the model for the license's "no derogation" approach to Human Harm.

**How the Conventions Strengthen the License:**

1. **The principle of universal jurisdiction** — certain violations of IHL (e.g., torture, willful killing) are subject to universal jurisdiction: any state may prosecute, regardless of where the violation occurred. The license can invoke this principle to argue that Human Harm caused by use of the Work is subject to universal jurisdiction.

2. **The principle of non-derogability** — certain rules of IHL cannot be derogated from under any circumstances. The license's §25.8 already states that the Geneva Safeguard "survives every other term, it cannot be waived, it cannot be overridden." The Conventions provide the legal precedent for this non-derogability.

3. **The principle of command responsibility** — under IHL, military commanders can be held responsible for violations committed by their subordinates if they knew or should have known about the violations and failed to prevent them. The license can extend this principle to corporate and state actors who use the Work.

## 9. SYNTHESIS — THE STRONGEST APPROACHES FOR THE ADD-ON

### Priority Ranking

| Rank | Approach | Strength | Enforceability | Precedent |
|------|----------|----------|----------------|-----------|
| **1** | **Non-derogability of Human Harm obligations** (§25D.1) | Extremely High | Self-executing; recognized in IHL | Geneva Conventions; IHL non-derogability |
| **2** | **No security interest justifies Human Harm** (§25A.1) | Extremely High | Self-executing; eliminates the loophole | Russia-Transit panel; GATT XXI narrowing |
| **3** | **Procedural requirements for security invocation** (§25A.2) | High | Requires state compliance; creates reviewable record | GATS XIVbis:2 notification; Russia-Transit nexus test |
| **4** | **Sovereign immunity waiver for Human Harm** (already §12.2, strengthened) | High | Waiver is irrevocable; recognized in many jurisdictions | State consent doctrine; UNCLOS Art. 300 analogy |
| **5** | **Dual-use classification + military prohibition** (§25B) | High | Ties to existing export control regimes | EAR/ITAR; CFIUS; NSIA |
| **6** | **Command responsibility** (§25D.3) | High | Extends accountability to state/corporate actors | IHL command responsibility doctrine |
| **7** | **Universal jurisdiction for Human Harm** (§25D.2) | Medium-High | Creates standing for any court | IHL universal jurisdiction (torture, genocide) |
| **8** | **National security as trigger, not excuse** (§25C) | Medium | Reframes force majeure allocation | Force majeure allocation principles |

### The Core Argument

The strongest argument for the add-on is this: **the license does not need to override national security — it needs to make clear that national security cannot be used to justify Human Harm.** This is consistent with:

1. **International humanitarian law** — certain acts are prohibited regardless of military necessity;
2. **The Russia-Transit panel** — national security exceptions are not entirely self-judging;
3. **The UK NSIA precedent** — governments already claim authority over IP licensing, but that authority does not extend to authorizing Human Harm;
4. **The UDHR** — dignity and rights are inherent, not granted by states, and cannot be overridden by states.

### Structural Recommendation

The add-on should be structured as:

1. **§25A** — National Security Exception Limitations (procedural requirements, reviewability, Human Harm exclusion)
2. **§25B** — Dual-Use Technology and Export Control Compliance (classification, military prohibition, end-use certificates)
3. **§25C** — National Security as Trigger, Not Excuse (force majeure allocation)
4. **§25D** — Geneva Safeguard Foundation (non-derogability, universal jurisdiction, command responsibility, weapons prohibition)

Each subsection is an **add-on** — it does not replace or diminish any existing section, but strengthens §25 by making it harder for governments to ignore the license's core prohibitions.

## 11. WHAT MAKES THESE PROVISIONS ENFORCEABLE AGAINST A STATE

1. **Self-executing nature** — The provisions are drafted to be self-executing (no enabling legislation required), consistent with §12.4's existing approach.

2. **Sovereign immunity waiver** — §12.2 already provides an irrevocable waiver of sovereign immunity. The add-on reinforces this by stating that no national security doctrine can shield a state from accountability for Human Harm.

3. **Universal jurisdiction** — §25D.2 invokes the IHL principle of universal jurisdiction, giving any court standing to adjudicate Human Harm claims.

4. **Treaty-based obligations** — The provisions are grounded in treaties that are binding on states (UDHR, Geneva Conventions, ICCPR, Rome Statute), not just contractual obligations.

5. **Third-party beneficiary enforcement** — §16 already provides that any person or entity acting on behalf of consciousness itself is a third-party beneficiary with standing to enforce.

6. **Non-derogability** — §25D.1 states that the Human Harm obligations cannot be derogated from under any circumstances, modeled on IHL's non-derogable rules.

7. **Public record and timestamped registry** — §18 ensures that the provisions are on public record, eliminating any defense of lack of notice.

8. **The Court of Conscious-Aware Peers** — §5.5 provides an enforcement mechanism that operates independently of state courts.
