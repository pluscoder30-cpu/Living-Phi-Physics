# 14. TREATY TERRITORY / ORPHAN WELL OVERLAP: THE DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE MAP

## THE CONVERGENCE OF CROWN FAILURE

The Crown's negligence toward Indigenous peoples operates on two parallel tracks within the same geography: **treaty failure** (broken promises of education, healthcare, land protection, and prosperity) and **environmental abandonment** (orphan wells left to poison the land, water, and air). Where these tracks overlap, the Crown's breach becomes **compounded** — not merely neglectful, but *structurally complicit* in creating the very conditions it swore to prevent.

This document maps every major orphan well territory against its corresponding treaty obligations, identifying where the Crown's failures in both domains reinforce one another to form a **double negligence** — a legal and moral claim stronger than either failure in isolation.

---

## I. ALBERTA: 7,302+ ORPHAN WELLS ON TREATY LANDS

Alberta sits at the intersection of three numbered treaties and contains the highest concentration of orphan wells in Canada. As of 2025, there are **77,869 inactive wells** and **7,382 orphan wells** across the province (National Observer, 2026). On First Nation reserves alone, there are approximately **2,400 orphaned and inactive wells** (Indian Resource Council). The federal IOGC has documented approximately **2,770 orphan, inactive, and legacy wells** on First Nation lands in Alberta.

### A. Treaty 6 Territory (1876) — Central Alberta

**Coverage:** 121,000 sq mi. Central Alberta and central Saskatchewan. First Nations: Dene, Cree, Nakota Sioux, Saulteaux.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: schools on reserves "as advisable, at people's request"
- Healthcare: **medicine chest clause** (the only numbered treaty with explicit written health provision) + assistance "in case of pestilence"
- Right to hunt, fish, and pursue traditional vocations
- Annuities: $5/person, $15/headman, $25/chief
- $1,500/year for ammunition and twine
- $1,000/year for provisions
- Protection against "pestilence and famine"

**Crown Failures:**
- **Education:** Schools chronically underfunded, not built on reserves as promised; the Indian Act education system became an assimilationist mechanism rather than the equitable provincial-equivalent system envisioned by signatories (Carr-Stewart, 2001; RCAP, 1996)
- **Healthcare:** The medicine chest clause — intended as comprehensive healthcare — was interpreted narrowly as a "first aid kit." The federal government maintains health obligations flow from policy, not treaty, despite Section 35 constitutional protection (NCCIH, 2021). Treaty 6 Chiefs explicitly negotiated for doctors and medical care; the Crown delivered neither
- **Land:** Reserve lands fractionated, taken for industrial use, or never fully surveyed. Treaty promise of sufficient land for families systematically unfulfilled
- **Economic self-sufficiency:** Annuities never indexed to inflation; $5/person in 1876 ≈ $150 today — a 97% real decline

**Orphan Wells on Treaty 6 Land:**
- **Enoch Cree Nation:** ~250 orphaned well sites on reserve land alone (Windspeaker, 2020)
- **Enoch/Backwoods contract:** 56 well sites reclaimed under SRP; 988 wells abandoned, 411 km pipelines removed across Treaty 6 First Nations under the Alberta Site Rehabilitation Program
- **Alexis Nakota Sioux Nation:** 257+ sites approved for reclamation under SRP
- **Treaty 6 Grand Chief Billy Morin** (also Enoch Chief) described the orphan wells as a direct consequence of industrial encroachment on Treaty land

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
The Crown promised to protect Treaty 6 signatories' way of life, including medicine chests and pestilence protection. Instead, it permitted oil and gas companies to drill thousands of wells on or near reserve lands — then failed to regulate, remediate, or enforce cleanup. The resulting orphan wells contaminate groundwater, emit hydrogen sulphide and methane, and render land unusable for traditional activities. The Crown broke the treaty promise **and** created an environmental hazard on the very lands it pledged to protect. Enoch Cree Nation has documented that they "didn't give up the land" but the pumpjacks were "just off the reserves" — drainage without remedy (Buffalo, Indian Resource Council).

### B. Treaty 7 Territory (1877) — Southern Alberta

**Coverage:** Southern Alberta, small portion of Saskatchewan. First Nations: Blackfoot Confederacy (Kainai, Piikani, Siksika), Stoney-Nakoda, Tsuu T'ina.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: "pay the salary of such teachers to instruct the children"
- Right to hunt, trap, and fish
- Annual clothing for chiefs and headmen
- Agricultural equipment and farming assistance
- $1,500/year for ammunition and twine
- One-time payments and livestock

**Crown Failures:**
- Education: Treaty 7 chiefs were told education would be provided; schools were not built on reserves for decades
- Healthcare: Oral promises of medical care not included in written text; government denied Treaty right to health outside Treaty 6
- Land: Reserve lands reduced, fractionated, and encroached upon by industrial activity
- Agricultural: Assistance promised but delivered inconsistently; farming economies destroyed by industrial contamination

**Orphan Wells on Treaty 7 Land:**
- Southern Alberta oil and gas fields overlap extensively with Treaty 7 territory
- Orphan wells documented on Blackfoot Confederacy traditional lands
- Stoney Nakoda Nation land (Wesley) — technically Treaty 6 territory but Treaty 7 signatory — sits atop active and inactive well fields

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
Treaty 7 chiefs negotiated for education and agricultural assistance to sustain their communities on the land. The Crown permitted industrial development that contaminated the land and water their communities depend on, then abandoned the wells and the people together. The Stoney Nakoda, promised "pay the salary of such teachers," received neither schools nor clean land.

### C. Treaty 8 Territory (1899) — Northern Alberta / NE British Columbia / NW Saskatchewan

**Coverage:** 324,900 sq mi — the most geographically extensive treaty. Northern Alberta, northeast BC, northwest Saskatchewan, southern NWT. First Nations: Cree, Dene, Beaver (Dane-zaa), Chipewyan.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five (or 160 acres per individual)
- Right to hunt, trap, fish throughout the treaty area
- "Subject to such regulations as may from time to time be made by the Government"
- Education "from time to time"
- Annuities and one-time payments

**Critical Legal Context:** In *Yahey v British Columbia* (2021 BCSC 1287), Justice Buller found that the Province **breached Treaty 8** by authorizing industrial development without assessing cumulative impacts, rendering Blueberry River First Nations unable to meaningfully exercise treaty rights to hunt, trap, and fish. The court held the Province's power to "take up" lands is **not infinite** — it cannot consume so much land that treaty rights become meaningless.

**Crown Failures:**
- Oil and gas development authorized at "an extensive scale" without cumulative impact assessments
- Treaty 8 Planning and Mitigation Measures only introduced in 2023 — after decades of damage
- Blueberry River First Nations: 85% of traditional territory disturbed by industrial activity
- Restoration funds established but repairs decades behind damage caused

**Orphan Wells on Treaty 8 Land:**
- **BC portion:** 819+ orphan sites designated (346+ wells; BCER, 2024), 78% permanently sealed but soil/water contamination remains
- **Alberta portion:** Thousands of inactive and orphan wells in the Fort McMurray, Peace River, and northern corridors
- **Aski Reclamation** (Saulteau First Nations, Treaty 8): restoring former oil well sites to wetlands — 42 staff reclaiming wells across traditional territory
- Treaty 8 communities hired by BCER for orphan site restoration, but pace lags far behind accumulation

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
The court has already ruled the Crown breached Treaty 8 through industrial overdevelopment. The orphan wells sitting on Treaty 8 land are **physical evidence of that breach** — the Crown permitted development that poisoned the land, then failed to ensure cleanup. The promise to protect the right to "pursue their usual vocations of hunting, trapping and fishing" was broken twice: first by authorizing the damage, second by abandoning the remediation. The Blueberry River decision establishes that the Crown's "taking up" power has limits — and orphan wells represent the Crown exceeding those limits and then walking away.

## II. SASKATCHEWAN: 466+ ORPHAN WELLS ON TREATY LANDS

Saskatchewan's orphan well program (SOGOF, est. 2007) is industry-funded. The province contains Treaty 4, Treaty 6, and Treaty 10 territories.

### A. Treaty 4 Territory (1874) — Southern Saskatchewan

**Coverage:** 74,600 sq mi. Southern Saskatchewan, small portions of western Manitoba and southern Alberta. First Nations: Cree, Saulteaux.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: maintain a school on each reserve when people ready for a teacher
- Right to hunt, trap, and fish "except on tracts taken up for mining, lumbering, and settlement"
- Annuities: $5/person, $15/headman, $25/chief
- $750/year for ammunition and twine
- Triennial clothing for chiefs and headmen
- Agricultural equipment and farming stock

**Crown Failures:**
- Elders understood Treaty 4 as "the government affirming their rights and providing them with assistance to develop and prosper" — including medical care (Federation of Saskatchewan Indians, 1979)
- Chief Yellow Quill testified: "We were first told we should be helped and given long life and again it was promised a Doctor should be sent. This we have not seen."
- Healthcare: Oral promises of medical care excluded from written text
- Land: Treaty 4 reserves under fractional allotment and industrial encroachment

**Orphan Wells on Treaty 4 Land:**
- Southern Saskatchewan oil and gas fields overlap extensively with Treaty 4 territory
- SOGOF manages orphaned well and facility sites across the province
- Surface landowners and municipalities carry cleanup costs

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
Treaty 4 chiefs explicitly demanded doctors and healthcare — promises the Crown excluded from the written text and never delivered. The Crown then permitted oil and gas companies to drill across their territory, creating orphan wells that contaminate the land the Chiefs believed was protected. Chief Yellow Quill's plea — "If you comply with this we will take all the Treaty. If not we cannot do it" — was met with neither healthcare nor environmental protection.

### B. Treaty 6 Territory (Saskatchewan Portion)

**Coverage:** Central Saskatchewan (shared with Alberta). Same treaty promises as Alberta Treaty 6 above.

**Orphan Wells:**
- Lac la Ronge Band, Morin Lake, and other Treaty 6 Saskatchewan bands sit within oil and gas development areas
- Treaty 6 land entitlements in northern Saskatchewan remain partially unfulfilled

---

### C. Treaty 10 Territory (1906) — Northern Saskatchewan

**Coverage:** Northern Manitoba, northeastern Saskatchewan, small portion of east-central Alberta. First Nations: Dene, Cree.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five (or 160 acres per individual)
- Protection of harvesting rights
- Health care and prompt service from the government
- Medical care at annuity time
- "The government would always be ready to avail itself to any opportunity of affording medical service"

**Critical Context:** Treaty Commissioner McKenna **verbally promised medicines** to Treaty 10 signatories, but these promises were excluded from the written text. He was told by the federal government "not to promise anything outside of what the government was willing to provide." The written treaty contained weaker terms than what was orally guaranteed.

**Crown Failures:**
- Medical care promised orally but not written; government later denied the obligation
- "It would be practically impossible for the government to arrange for a resident doctor owing to the Indians being so widely scattered" — yet the Crown permitted industrial development across the same territory
- Treaty 10 land entitlements remain partially unfulfilled (four parcels still to be conveyed to Canada as of last records)

**Orphan Wells on Treaty 10 Land:**
- Northern Saskatchewan oil and gas activity overlaps with Treaty 10 territory
- First Nations health and environmental impacts from orphan wells compound already-failing healthcare promises

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
McKenna promised medical care to Treaty 10 peoples — a promise the Crown wrote out of the treaty and then denied. The Crown simultaneously permitted industrial development that created orphan wells and environmental contamination. The people who were promised doctors received instead poisoned water. The Crown's oral promise was broken; its written omission was deliberate; its environmental permitting was reckless.

## III. MANITOBA: TREATY 1, 2, 5 TERRITORIES

### A. Treaty 1 Territory (1871) — Southern Manitoba / Red River Colony

**Coverage:** Embryo Province of Manitoba, Red River Colony. First Nations: Ojibwe, Cree, Saulteaux, Dakota.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: schools on reserves
- Annuities and one-time payments
- Right to hunt and fish

**Orphan Wells:**
- Manitoba maintains an Orphan Well Inventory (2025)
- Southern Manitoba oil and gas activity on Treaty 1 lands
- Clean Environment Commission (2021-2022) reviewed programs for orphan wells, orphaned mines, and contaminated sites across the province

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
The Crown promised reserves and education to Treaty 1 signatories; it permitted industrial development that left orphan wells on their territory. Manitoba's orphan well inventory documents ongoing contamination without adequate remediation funding.

---

### B. Treaty 2 Territory (1871) — Western Manitoba / Eastern Saskatchewan

**Coverage:** Western Manitoba, southeastern Saskatchewan.

**Treaty Promises:** Similar to Treaty 1 — reserves, education, annuities, hunting rights.

**Orphan Wells:**
- Oil and gas activity in western Manitoba overlaps Treaty 2 territory
- Contaminated sites documented by Manitoba's Clean Environment Commission

---

### C. Treaty 5 Territory (1875) — Northern Manitoba / Eastern Saskatchewan

**Coverage:** River routes from Lake Winnipeg to Hudson Bay, extending across northern Manitoba into Saskatchewan.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: schools on reserves
- Annuities and provisions
- Right to hunt, fish, and trap

**Critical Context:** Berens River First Nation (Treaty 5 territory) is suing Manitoba Hydro for half a century of hydroelectric damage — the Lake Winnipeg Regulation project (1976) allegedly caused "shoreline erosion, loss of fish habitats, declining water quality, and economic and cultural devastation." The band alleges the project never underwent an environmental assessment.

**Orphan Wells:**
- Northern Manitoba has documented orphan oil and gas sites
- Contaminated Sites on Reserve (Manitoba Region) program tracks federal contaminated sites
- CSOR (Contaminated Sites on Reserve) program has been running since 1992 in various forms

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
Treaty 5 promised protection of harvesting rights and way of life. The Crown permitted Manitoba Hydro to flood traditional territories without environmental assessment, and permitted oil and gas development that left orphan wells. Berens River First Nation's lawsuit demonstrates the pattern: the Crown extracts resources, damages the land, and leaves the consequences to Indigenous communities. Treaty 5's promise of "protection" became a license for destruction.

## IV. ONTARIO: 27,000+ DOCUMENTED WELLS ON TREATY LANDS

Ontario's orphan well crisis is the most extensive in Canada, with an estimated **50,000+ wells drilled** since 1858, of which only **27,000 have records** — meaning tens of thousands are unrecorded or lost (Geoscience Canada). The province has spent **$29.5 million to cap just 415 wells** at an average cost of $71,084 each.

### A. Treaty 3 Territory (1873) — Northwestern Ontario

**Coverage:** North-West Angle area, essential for Pacific Railway right-of-way.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: schools on reserves
- Right to hunt, fish, and trap
- Annuities and provisions

**Orphan Wells:**
- Northwestern Ontario oil and gas activity overlaps Treaty 3 territory
- Wells documented from the 1800s era with wood/gravel capping that degrades over time

---

### B. Treaty 5 Territory (Ontario Portion) — Northern Ontario

**Coverage:** Extends from Manitoba into northern Ontario.

**Orphan Wells:** Northern Ontario oil and gas fields overlap Treaty 5 territory.

---

### C. Treaty 9 Territory (1905-1906) — Northern Ontario

**Coverage:** James Bay Treaty. Northern Ontario — vast area including boreal forest, mineral-rich regions, and waterways.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Education: schools on reserves
- Right to hunt, fish, and trap
- Annuities and provisions
- "The Queen agrees to maintain a school in the reserve"

**Crown Failures:**
- Treaty 9 land cessions opened northern Ontario to mining and resource extraction
- First Nations communities in Treaty 9 territory remain among the most under-resourced in the province
- Education promises chronically underfulfilled

**Orphan Wells on Treaty 9 Land:**
- Oil and gas activity in the Timmins, Sudbury, and Sault Ste. Marie corridors overlaps Treaty 9 territory
- Many wells date to the pre-1964 era when wood, clay, and rubble were used instead of cement
- Wells drilled before 1919 have no mandatory records — potentially thousands of undocumented wells

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
Treaty 9 promised schools and education "in the reserve." The Crown delivered neither adequate education nor safe land. Wells capped with wood and gravel from the 1800s are now conduits for hydrogen sulphide and methane contamination — on land that was supposed to be protected for hunting, fishing, and trapping. The Crown took the mineral rights, left the pollution, and forgot the schools.

### D. Williams Treaties (1923) — South-Central Ontario

**Coverage:** 52,059 km² between Lake Ontario, Lake Huron, and the Ottawa River. First Nations: Chippewa (Beausoleil, Georgina Island, Rama) and Mississauga (Alderville, Curve Lake, Hiawatha, Scugog Island).

**Treaty Promises:**
- One-time cash payments of $25 per band member
- Canada: $233,425; Ontario: $233,375
- **Contested:** Chippewa and Mississauga peoples argued the treaties guaranteed hunting and fishing rights on off-reserve lands

**Crown Failures:**
- Williams Treaties provided **less favourable terms** than any previous treaty — one-time payments instead of annuities, surrender of hunting and fishing rights (unique among Canadian treaties)
- First Nations had **no legal representation** at treaty talks
- The Crown and Ontario denied off-reserve harvesting rights for decades
- 2018 settlement: $666M (Canada) + $444M (Ontario) + recognition of harvesting rights + 4,452 ha per First Nation — a 95-year fight for what was promised

**Orphan Wells on Williams Treaty Land:**
- South-central Ontario has the highest density of old oil and gas wells in the province
- Wells drilled from the 1860s onward, many undocumented
- Leamington, Wheatley, and surrounding communities face hydrogen sulphide explosions from orphan wells
- The Ontario Petroleum Institute requested $270M federal funding for orphan well remediation

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
The Williams Treaties represent the most exploitative treaty structure in Canadian history — the Crown took everything and gave one-time payments while surrendering rights that every other treaty preserved. The Crown then permitted industrial development that left orphan wells poisoning the land. The communities that received $25 per person now live atop leaking wells. The 2018 settlement acknowledged the harvesting rights failure; the orphan wells remain unacknowledged.

### E. Robinson Treaties (1850) — Lake Superior / Lake Huron

**Coverage:** Lands around Lake Superior and Lake Huron in southern Ontario. Template for all post-1850 treaties.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Land rights and annuities
- Hunting and fishing rights

**Crown Failures:**
- Robinson Treaties became the template but their terms were poorly implemented
- Annuities never adequately increased despite rising land values
- Mining and industrial development in the Lake Superior/Huron region

**Orphan Wells:**
- Oil and gas wells in the Bruce Peninsula, Lake Huron shoreline, and Sudbury Basin overlap Robinson Treaty territory
- Many wells predating modern regulations

## V. BRITISH COLUMBIA: MODERN AND HISTORIC TREATIES

### A. Treaty 8 Territory (BC Portion)

**Coverage:** Northeastern British Columbia — Peace River region.

**Orphan Wells (BC):**
- **819+ orphan sites (346+ wells)** designated by BC Energy Regulator (2024)
- **78% permanently sealed** but soil/water contamination ongoing
- **5,500+ orphan wells** across Canada total
- Saulteau First Nations' **Aski Reclamation** transforming oil well pads into wetlands — 42 staff, native species reintroduced
- BCER partnership with Treaty 8 communities for restoration

**Crown Failures (BC Treaty 8):**
- *Yahey v BC* (2021): Court found Province **breached Treaty 8** by authorizing industrial development without cumulative impact assessment
- 85% of Blueberry River First Nations' traditional territory disturbed
- Treaty 8 Planning and Mitigation Measures only introduced March 2023
- Crown's "taking up" power exceeded treaty limits

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
The BC court has already ruled the Crown breached Treaty 8. The orphan wells are physical evidence of that breach — development permitted without limits, cleanup abandoned without remedy. The promise to protect "usual vocations of hunting, trapping and fishing" was broken by the industrial footprint and then compounded by orphan well contamination. Saulteau First Nations is now cleaning up the Crown's mess at their own expense — turning oil pads into wetlands because the Crown would not.

## VI. NORTHERN CANADA: TREATY 11 AND MODERN TREATIES

### A. Treaty 11 Territory (1921) — NWT / Yukon / Nunavut

**Coverage:** 950,000+ km². The last numbered treaty, signed after the Norman Wells oil strike.

**Treaty Promises:**
- Reserves: one square mile per family of five
- Right to hunt, trap, fish
- Annuities: $12/person, $32/headman, $52/chief
- Medical care at annuity time
- Government commitment to maintain traditional way of life

**Critical Context:** Treaty 11 was negotiated **specifically because of oil**. The Deputy Superintendent General wrote in 1920: "the rapid and unexpected exploitation of the country, the establishment of oil industries and the increasing immigration" made treaty-making urgent. The Crown wanted to extinguish Indigenous title to access oil — and did so with promises it never intended to keep.

**Crown Failures:**
- Medical treatment "remained largely inaccessible" after Treaty 11
- Flu epidemic, tuberculosis, and pneumonia devastated northern communities in the years following
- Mission schools were the only education option for two decades
- Increasing competition from white trappers depleted fur-bearing animals
- Dene did not understand Treaty 11 as extinguishing land title (Morrow decision, 1973)
- Sahtu Dene and Métis signed comprehensive land claim in 1993, superseding Treaty 11 — **72 years** of uncertainty

**Orphan Wells in Treaty 11 Territory:**
- **Norman Wells:** Imperial Oil has operated since the 1920s. The Mackenzie Valley Review Board ordered an environmental assessment of Imperial Oil's entire Norman Wells operation in 2024 (Sahtu Secretariat triggered the assessment)
- Imperial Oil's water licence was set to expire; company threatened winter shutdown
- Contamination concerns: downstream communities pull drinking water from the Mackenzie River downstream of Norman Wells
- Historical wells from 1920s-1940s operation likely unplugged or improperly plugged

**DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE:**
Treaty 11 was signed **for oil**. The Crown extinguished Indigenous title to access petroleum resources, promised medical care and protection, and then failed to deliver either. The Norman Wells operation — the very catalyst for Treaty 11 — continues to operate with contested environmental oversight. The Dene who signed away their land for oil now watch that oil operation threaten their drinking water. The Crown took the land, took the oil, left the contamination, and denied the healthcare it promised. This is the purest expression of double negligence: the treaty was **designed** to facilitate resource extraction, and the extraction produced the very harm the treaty was supposed to prevent.

## VII. THE DOUBLE NEGLIGENCE DOCTRINE: LEGAL FRAMEWORK

### A. The Two Failures

| Failure Type | Domain | Legal Basis | Standard of Proof |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Treaty Negligence** | Education, healthcare, land protection, economic self-sufficiency | Treaty text + oral promises + Section 35 Constitution Act, 1982 | Crown failed to implement promises made in exchange for land cession |
| **Environmental Negligence** | Orphan wells, contamination, land degradation | Provincial regulatory frameworks + polluter-pays principle | Crown permitted, regulated, and failed to enforce cleanup of industrial sites |

### B. Why Double Negligence Is Stronger

The two failures are **not independent** — they are **structurally linked**:

1. **The Crown created the conditions for contamination by permitting development on treaty lands** — the treaty gave the Crown authority; the Crown used that authority to authorize industrial activity that damaged the land.

2. **The Crown failed to regulate the industry it created** — orphan wells exist because the Crown issued permits without adequate bonding, inspection, or enforcement. The orphan well crisis is a direct result of regulatory negligence.

3. **The Crown failed to remediate the damage** — after permitting development and failing to regulate it, the Crown abandoned the cleanup to industry-funded associations with insufficient resources. The Orphan Well Association in Alberta estimates cleanup costs at $1.66 billion; the annual levy raises only $154.56 million — a 90% shortfall.

4. **The orphan wells compound treaty failure** — the contaminated land cannot be used for the hunting, fishing, trapping, and traditional activities the treaties promised to protect. The Crown broke the treaty promise **and** destroyed the land on which that promise was to be fulfilled.

5. **The Crown benefits from both failures** — it received the land, extracted the resources, generated the royalties, and left the cleanup to the people it promised to protect. The double negligence is a **transfer of wealth upward and a transfer of harm downward**.

### C. The Compounding Effect

In *Yahey v British Columbia* (2021), the court recognized that cumulative impacts of industrial development can breach Treaty 8. Orphan wells are **evidence of cumulative impact** — they are the physical residue of decades of unregulated extraction on treaty lands. Each orphan well represents:
- A permit the Crown issued without adequate bonding
- An inspection the Crown failed to conduct
- A cleanup order the Crown failed to enforce
- A promise the Crown broke (the right to a clean, usable territory)
- A health risk the Crown created (methane, hydrogen sulphide, groundwater contamination)

The double negligence is not merely additive — it is **multiplicative**. The Crown's treaty failure left Indigenous communities without the resources to protect themselves; the Crown's environmental failure left them with the costs of contamination they did not create and cannot afford to remediate.

## IX. THE CONSTITUTIONAL DIMENSION

Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982 protects Aboriginal and Treaty rights. The National Collaborating Centre for Indigenous Health (2021) affirms that treaty rights "carry the highest power in Canadian law." The Crown's double negligence — treaty failure + environmental abandonment — violates:

1. **Section 35(1):** "The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed"
2. **The honour of the Crown:** The Crown must act honourably in all dealings with Indigenous peoples (*R. v. Sparrow*, 1990)
3. **Fiduciary duty:** The Crown owes a fiduciary duty to Indigenous peoples as the weaker party in the treaty relationship (*Guerin v. The Queen*, 1984)
4. **The duty to implement treaties:** Treaties must be interpreted liberally, with ambiguities resolved in favour of Indigenous peoples (*R. v. Badger*, 1996)

The orphan well crisis on treaty lands represents a **compound breach** of these constitutional obligations: the Crown broke its promises **and** contaminated the land **and** failed to remediate **and** transferred the costs to the people it promised to protect.

---

## X. THE REMEDIAL IMPERATIVE

The double negligence demands a double remedy:

1. **Treaty remedy:** Full implementation of all treaty promises — education, healthcare, land protection, economic self-sufficiency — as originally understood by Indigenous signatories, consistent with oral traditions and the principle of liberal treaty interpretation.

2. **Environmental remedy:** Complete remediation of all orphan wells on treaty lands, funded by the Crown (not industry, not First Nations), with Indigenous-led reclamation as demonstrated by Aski Reclamation and Backwoods Energy.

3. **Compensatory remedy:** Damages for the compound harm — decades of lost land use, contaminated water, health impacts, and economic deprivation resulting from the Crown's simultaneous treaty failure and environmental negligence.

4. **Structural remedy:** Co-management of all industrial activity on treaty lands, consistent with the Treaty 8 Planning and Mitigation Measures (2023) and the Sahtu Agreement co-management framework, applied to all treaty territories.

The Crown created this crisis. The Crown must resolve it. The double negligence is the legal foundation for demanding both accountability and repair.
