# 11. Treaty Interpretation & the Courts: How Treaties Are Read and What Remedies Exist

## The Canons of Treaty Interpretation

Canadian courts have developed a distinct body of interpretive principles governing how treaties with Indigenous nations are to be read. These principles do not apply to ordinary contracts or commercial agreements — they arise from the unique political, historical, and constitutional character of treaties as solemn nation-to-nation agreements.

### 1. Liberal, Generous, and Large Interpretation

The foundational canon is that treaties must be given "the largest, liberal and generous construction" in favour of the Indigenous signatories. This principle was first articulated in *Taylor and Williams* (1981) and has been affirmed repeatedly by the Supreme Court of Canada.

From *R. v. Nowegijick* [1983] 1 S.C.R. 29:
> "Treaties and statutes relating to Indians should be liberally construed and doubtful expressions resolved in favour of the Indians."

From *R. v. Sioui* [1990] 1 S.C.R. 1025:
> "The words of the treaty must not be interpreted in their strict technical sense nor subjected to rigid modern rules of construction. Rather, they must be interpreted in the sense that they would naturally have been understood by the Indians at the time of the signing."

The rationale is straightforward: the Crown drafted the treaties in English, using common law terminology, while Indigenous signatories — many of whom spoke only oral languages — could not read the written text. The treaties, as written documents, recorded an agreement that had already been reached orally and did not always record the full extent of that oral agreement. Therefore, strict literal interpretation would systematically advantage the party that drafted the document against the party that could not read it.

### 2. Ambiguities Resolved in Favour of Indigenous Peoples

Any ambiguities or doubtful expressions in the wording of a treaty must be resolved in favour of the Indigenous signatories. A corollary principle holds that any limitations which restrict the rights of Indians under treaties must be narrowly construed.

From *R. v. Badger* [1996] 1 S.C.R. 771 (per Cory J.):
> "Third, any ambiguities or doubtful expressions in the wording of the treaty or document must be resolved in favour of the Indians. A corollary to this principle is that any limitations which restrict the rights of Indians under treaties must be narrowly construed."

This is not a vague presumption — it is a binding rule of law that applies whenever a treaty provision is susceptible of more than one meaning.

### 3. The Honour of the Crown

The honour of the Crown is always at stake in its dealings with Indigenous peoples. It is always assumed that the Crown intends to fulfil its promises. No appearance of "sharp dealing" will be sanctioned.

From *R. v. Badger*:
> "The honour of the Crown is always at stake; the Crown must be assumed to intend to fulfil its promises. No appearance of 'sharp dealing' will be sanctioned."

This principle operates both as an interpretive tool (courts lean toward readings that preserve the Crown's integrity) and as an independent source of obligations (the Crown cannot act in a way that would be unconscionable).

### 4. Treaties Understood as the Indigenous Signatories Would Have Understood Them

The words in a treaty must be interpreted in the sense that they would naturally have been understood by the Indigenous signatories at the time of signing — not in their strict technical sense, and not subjected to rigid modern rules of construction.

From *Badger*:
> "The treaties, as written documents, recorded an agreement that had already been reached orally and they did not always record the full extent of the oral agreement... The treaties were drafted in English by representatives of the Canadian government who, it should be assumed, were familiar with common law doctrines. Yet, the treaties were not translated in written form into the languages of the various Indian nations who were signatories."

### 5. Oral Promises and Extrinsic Evidence

Where a treaty was concluded orally and afterwards written up by representatives of the Crown, it would be unconscionable for the Crown to ignore the oral terms while relying on the written ones. Extrinsic evidence — including oral history, historical records, and contextual evidence — is admissible to interpret treaties, even in the absence of ambiguity on the face of the document.

From *R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 S.C.R. 456:
> "Extrinsic evidence of the historical and cultural context of a treaty may be received even if the treaty document purports to contain all of the terms and even absent any ambiguity on the face of the treaty."

This principle was also central to *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* [1997] 3 S.C.R. 1010, where the Supreme Court held that oral history is admissible as evidence of Aboriginal title and rights.

### 6. Onus of Extinguishment Lies on the Crown

The onus of proving that a treaty or Aboriginal right has been extinguished lies upon the Crown. There must be "strict proof of the fact of extinguishment" and evidence of a clear and plain intention on the part of the government to extinguish treaty rights.

From *Badger*:
> "Fourth, the onus of proving that a treaty or aboriginal right has been extinguished lies upon the Crown. There must be 'strict proof of the fact of extinguishment' and evidence of a clear and plain intention on the part of the government to extinguish treaty rights."

### 7. Treaties Are Not Frozen in Time

Treaty rights must not be interpreted in a static or rigid way. They are not frozen at the date of signature. The interpreting court must update treaty rights to provide for their modern exercise — determining what modern practices are reasonably incidental to the core treaty right in its modern context.

From *Marshall* (per McLachlin C.J.):
> "What the treaty protects is not the right to harvest and dispose of particular commodities, but the right to practice a traditional 1760 trading activity in the modern way and modern context."

### 8. The Goal of Reconciliation

The goal of treaty interpretation is to choose from among the various possible interpretations of common intention the one which best reconciles the interests of both parties at the time the treaty was signed.

From *Sioui* (per Lamer J.):
> "Choose from among the various possible interpretations of the common intention the one which best reconciles the interests of both parties."

## Key Court Decisions on Treaty Rights

### *Calder v. Attorney-General of British Columbia* [1973] S.C.R. 313

The Supreme Court of Canada ruled that Aboriginal title existed as a legal right independent of its recognition by the Crown. While the Court split 3-3 on whether the Nisga'a title had been extinguished, the decision established that Aboriginal title predated European sovereignty and continued to exist at common law unless explicitly extinguished. This decision led directly to the federal government's policy of comprehensive land claims negotiation.

**Significance**: Aboriginal title is not a gift from the Crown — it is an inherent right that predates Confederation.

### *R. v. Guerin* [1984] 2 S.C.R. 335

The Supreme Court held that the Crown owes a fiduciary duty to Indigenous peoples arising from its control over reserve lands. The Musqueam Indian Band had leased their lands at below-market rates because the Crown had failed to disclose the true value of the lease. The Court found the Crown had breached its fiduciary duty.

**Significance**: The Crown's relationship with Indigenous peoples is not merely political — it is fiduciary. The Crown must act in the best interests of Indigenous peoples when dealing with their lands and assets.

### *R. v. Sparrow* [1990] 1 S.C.R. 1075

The Supreme Court established the justification test for infringement of Aboriginal rights under section 35(1) of the *Constitution Act, 1982*. The Crown may limit Aboriginal rights only if it can demonstrate: (1) a valid legislative objective; (2) that the infringement is as minimal as possible; and (3) that the Crown has consulted with and accommodated the affected Indigenous group.

**Significance**: Aboriginal rights are constitutionally protected and cannot be infringed without justification. The burden of justification rests entirely on the Crown.

### *R. v. Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* [1997] 3 S.C.R. 1010

The Supreme Court held that oral history is admissible as evidence of Aboriginal title and rights. The Court also clarified the test for Aboriginal title: the claimant must demonstrate sufficient, continuous, and exclusive occupation of the land at the time of assertion of European sovereignty. The Court emphasized that Aboriginal perspectives on land use and occupation must be given equal weight to the common law perspective.

**Significance**: Oral history is evidence. Aboriginal title requires proof of occupation, but the meaning of "occupation" must be understood from the Indigenous perspective.

### *R. v. Badger* [1996] 1 S.C.R. 771

The Supreme Court articulated the four core principles of treaty interpretation discussed above. The case involved three Cree men charged under Alberta's *Wildlife Act* for hunting on private lands within Treaty 8 territory. The Court held that treaty rights to hunt for food could not be practiced where land was visibly in use for a purpose incompatible with hunting, but that the *Wildlife Act* provision could not be justified as a limitation on the treaty right without further evidence.

**Significance**: This case is the leading authority on treaty interpretation. Justice Lamer's statement — "This is not a law society; it is the house of the Constitution" — underscores that treaties are constitutional documents, not mere contracts.

### *R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 S.C.R. 456

The Supreme Court held that the Treaties of 1760-61 conferred on the Mi'kmaq the right to catch and sell fish for a "moderate livelihood," based on the treaty's truckhouse clause. The Court emphasized that treaty rights are not frozen in time and must be interpreted to allow for their modern exercise. The decision also confirmed that extrinsic evidence is admissible to interpret treaties, even in the absence of ambiguity.

**Significance**: Treaty rights evolve. The right to trade for "necessaries" in 1760 translates to a right to a moderate livelihood in the modern context.

### *Haida Nation v. British Columbia* [2004] 1 S.C.R. 511

The Supreme Court held that the honour of the Crown requires the Crown to consult with and accommodate Indigenous peoples where government conduct may affect asserted Aboriginal or treaty rights, even before those rights have been proven. The duty to consult exists as a corollary to the honour of the Crown and the reconciliation framework established by section 35.

**Significance**: The duty to consult is not discretionary — it is a constitutional obligation triggered by government action that may affect Indigenous rights.

### *Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia* [2014] 2 S.C.R. 852

The Supreme Court declared Aboriginal title to a specific tract of land in central British Columbia. This was the first time a Canadian court had declared Aboriginal title to a specific area. The Court held that Aboriginal title includes the right to use, enjoy, and profit from the land, and the right to decide how the land is used and who has access to it. The Court also held that any infringement of Aboriginal title requires justification under the *Sparrow* test.

**Significance**: Aboriginal title is not merely a right to use land — it is a right to govern it.

### *Attorney General v. Restoule* [2024] SCC 27

The Supreme Court held that the Crown had a duty to increase annuities under the Robinson Treaties (1850) when economic conditions made the original fixed annuities inadequate. The Court found that the treaty promise of an "annuity" implicitly required the Crown to increase payments as the wealth generated from the ceded lands grew over time.

**Significance**: Treaty promises are not static. The Crown's obligation to act honourably requires it to revisit and adjust treaty terms when circumstances change.

### *Shot Both Sides v. Canada* [2024] SCC 26

The Supreme Court held that declaratory relief was available to the Piikani Nation despite the passage of time and limitation periods. The Court emphasized that declaratory relief serves a different function than damages — it declares the existence of rights and obligations, and is not subject to the same limitation principles.

**Significance**: The passage of time does not extinguish rights. Courts can still declare the existence of treaty obligations even decades after the breach.

## The Truth and Reconciliation Commission and Its Findings

### Background

The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (TRC) was established in 2008 as part of the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement — the largest class-action settlement in Canadian history. The TRC's mandate was to reveal to Canadians the complex truth about the history and ongoing legacy of the residential school system and to guide a process of truth and healing.

### The Findings

The TRC's six-volume final report was released on December 15, 2015. Its central finding was that the residential school system constituted **cultural genocide**.

From the final report:
> "For over a century, the central goals of Canada's Aboriginal policy were to eliminate Aboriginal governments; ignore Aboriginal rights; terminate the Treaties; and, through a process of assimilation, cause Aboriginal peoples to cease to exist as distinct legal, social, cultural, religious, and racial entities in Canada. The establishment and operation of residential schools were a central element of this policy, which can best be described as 'cultural genocide.'"

The TRC defined cultural genocide as:
> "The destruction of those structures and practices that allow the group to continue as a group. States that engage in cultural genocide set out to destroy the political and social institutions of the targeted group. Land is seized, and populations are forcibly transferred and their movement is restricted. Languages are banned. Spiritual leaders are persecuted, spiritual practices are forbidden, and objects of spiritual value are confiscated and destroyed. And, most significantly to the issue at hand, families are disrupted to prevent the transmission of cultural values and identity from one generation to the next."

### Scale of the System

- Approximately **150,000** First Nations, Métis, and Inuit students passed through the residential school system
- **139** residential schools and residences were identified
- The last federally supported residential school operated until the **late 1990s**
- The system was administered by Roman Catholic, Anglican, United, Methodist, and Presbyterian churches in partnership with the federal government
- At least **3,200 children** died in the schools from malnourishment, tuberculosis, and other diseases caused by poor living conditions — Justice Murray Sinclair argued many more died but were not officially counted

### The 94 Calls to Action

The TRC issued **94 Calls to Action** organized across six themes:

1. **Child Welfare** (Calls to Action 1-5): Reduce the number of Indigenous children in care
2. **Education** (Calls to Action 6-13): Close the education gap
3. **Language and Culture** (Calls to Action 13-17): Preserve and revitalize Indigenous languages
4. **Health** (Calls to Action 18-24): Eliminate health care gaps
5. **Justice** (Calls to Action 25-42): Address over-incarceration and policing
6. **Reconciliation** (Calls to Action 43-46): Adopt UNDRIP as the framework for reconciliation
7. **Royal Proclamation** (Call to Action 45-47): Reaffirm the Royal Proclamation
8. **Church Apologies** (Calls to Action 48-49): Church apologies
9. **Education for Reconciliation** (Calls to Action 62-65): Curriculum development
10. **Public Service** (Calls to Action 66-69): Training for public servants
11. **Media** (Calls to Action 76-80): Media representation
12. **Sport** (Calls to Action 86-87): Sport and reconciliation
13. **Business** (Calls to Action 92-94): Business and reconciliation

Key Calls to Action include:

- **Call to Action 43**: Federal, provincial, territorial, and municipal governments to fully adopt and implement UNDRIP as the framework for reconciliation
- **Call to Action 45**: Repudiation of the Doctrine of Discovery and Terra Nullius
- **Call to Action 92**: Business sector to adopt the UNDRIP as a reconciliation framework

### Implementation Progress

As of 2026, implementation of the Calls to Action has been uneven. The TRC itself noted that reconciliation would require sustained political will and concerted material resources — and that the Commission's work was only the beginning.

## The Specific Claims Process

### How It Works

A specific claim arises when a First Nation alleges that the Crown has failed to fulfil its obligations under a treaty or other agreement, or has unlawfully taken or mismanaged reserve land or assets. The process works as follows:

1. **Research and Development**: The First Nation researches the historical basis for its claim and prepares a fully researched historical report with primary and secondary documents. This phase typically takes 6 months to 2 years per claim.

2. **Submission**: The First Nation submits the claim to the Minister of Crown-Indigenous Relations.

3. **Minimum Standard Review**: Within 6 months, Canada determines whether the claim meets the minimum standard for filing. If not, the claim is returned for revision.

4. **Assessment Phase**: The Minister has up to **3 years** to assess whether Canada has an outstanding lawful obligation. The Specific Claims Branch reviews the claim and the Department of Justice prepares a legal opinion.

5. **Negotiation**: If accepted, the parties enter negotiation. If a settlement is reached within 3 years, it proceeds to implementation. If not, the First Nation may file with the Specific Claims Tribunal.

6. **Adjudication**: The Specific Claims Tribunal (est. 2008) can make binding decisions on validity and compensation up to **$150 million** per claim.

### The Backlog Crisis

The specific claims process has been described as slow, cumbersome, costly, adversarial, and legalistic. The backlog has grown dramatically:

| Metric | 2017 | 2024/25 |
|--------|------|---------|
| Under assessment or negotiation | 399 | 718 |
| Under adjudication | 133 | 74 |
| **Total backlog** | **532** | **792** |

Key facts about the backlog:

- Canada has consistently missed its legislated 3-year deadline to assess claims. A 2019 study found that of 107 claims filed between January 2014 and November 2015, Canada failed to respond to **92 (86%)** within the 3-year deadline.
- As of April 2025, **792 claims** remained in the backlog.
- The Specific Claims Tribunal has settled **62 claims** total since 2011, with **70 claims** still before it.
- In fiscal year 2024-25, **10 new claims** were filed with the Tribunal.
- As of March 31, 2025, **44% of active claims** before the Tribunal were stayed to allow the parties to pursue alternative dispute resolution.

### Funding Crisis

The funding situation has worsened dramatically:

- In 2025-26, First Nations submitted **192 funding applications** totaling **$34.5 million** against a **$12 million** budget, resulting in **98 First Nations** being denied funding.
- As of April 2026, the annual budget was set to fall from $12 million to just **$4 million** — a **75% reduction**.
- Federal loan funding is capped at $15,000 to $427,000 depending on claim size, far below actual costs which can exceed $1 million for the research and submission phase alone.
- The Union of BC Indian Chiefs warned that "underfunding does not contain Canada's liability — it escalates it."

### Structural Conflict of Interest

The specific claims process has been criticized for an inherent structural conflict of interest: the Crown simultaneously acts as defendant, adjudicator, and funder. The Auditor General of Canada has called for major reforms.

The Assembly of First Nations has proposed creating an Independent Centre for the Resolution of Specific Claims (ICRSC), but the federal government has not committed to enacting this proposal.

### Settlement Trends

Under the Trudeau government (2015-2025), the number of settlements and the total value of settlements increased dramatically:

- By 2017/18, the number of settlements had almost tripled
- Total settlement value reached **$7,061 million** in fiscal 2024/25
- Agricultural assistance ("cows and plows") claims became the largest category, with 23 agricultural settlements worth **$3,656 million** in 2024/25 alone

However, the backlog grew from 532 to 792 during this same period, as increased success motivated the filing of more and larger claims.

## UNDRIP Implementation in Canada

### The United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples

UNDRIP was adopted by the UN General Assembly in 2007 and sets out the minimum standards for the survival, dignity, and well-being of Indigenous peoples worldwide. Key rights include:

- The right to self-determination
- The right to autonomy in Indigenous community matters
- The right to maintain and strengthen distinct political, legal, economic, social, and cultural institutions
- The right to free, prior, and informed consent before any actions affecting Indigenous lands or resources
- The right to maintain, control, protect, and develop cultural heritage and traditional knowledge

### The UN Declaration Act (2021)

On June 21, 2021, the *United Nations Declaration on the Rights of Indigenous Peoples Act* received Royal Assent. The Act:

1. Affirms the Declaration as a universal international human rights instrument with application in Canadian law
2. Requires the Government of Canada to ensure all federal laws are consistent with UNDRIP
3. Requires the preparation and implementation of an Action Plan
4. Requires annual progress reports tabled in Parliament

### The Action Plan (2023)

On June 21, 2023, the UN Declaration Act Action Plan was released. It contains **181 measures** to be led by over **25 federal departments and agencies**, covering:

- Addressing injustices and eliminating discrimination
- Free, prior, and informed consent
- Self-determination and self-government
- Land, water, and resource rights
- Health, education, and justice
- Cultural and language rights
- Monitoring, oversight, and accountability

### Implementation Progress (as of 2025)

According to the Fourth Annual Progress Report (April 2024 - March 2025):

- Work has begun on **170 of 181** Action Plan measures
- **6 measures** are complete
- **99 measures** are underway
- **28 measures** are in the planning stage
- **37 measures** are being implemented on an ongoing basis

Key achievements include:

- Incorporation of a non-derogation clause into the federal *Interpretation Act*, ensuring all federal laws are interpreted in a manner that upholds Aboriginal and treaty rights under section 35
- Creation of an Indigenous-led UN Declaration Act Action Plan Advisory Committee
- Co-development of an Indigenous Stewardship Policy with Parks Canada

### Criticisms and Shortcomings

Despite progress, significant concerns remain:

1. **Consultation gaps**: The Crown's consultative approach has been criticized as insufficiently collaborative. Meaningful consultation must be done through Indigenous laws and be collective, deliberate, and relational.

2. **Free, prior, and informed consent**: While the Action Plan discusses FPIC, implementation remains weak. The Impact Assessment Act has been redrafted with UNDRIP in mind, but the regulations required to implement shared decision-making have not been enacted.

3. **Funding cuts**: Federal funding for Indigenous capacity to participate in UNDRIP implementation has been reduced. Funding for governance tables has not been renewed, and specific claims research funding has been cut by 75%.

4. **Section 35 limitations**: The non-derogation clause references section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982*, but courts have significantly limited what falls under recognized section 35 rights. There is no mechanism to ensure section 35 rights align with UNDRIP.

5. **International standards**: The government's approach has been criticized for failing to engage with 40 years of international human rights jurisprudence and studies that provide guidance on implementing UNDRIP standards.

## The Intersection: Treaty Interpretation, TRC, Specific Claims, and UNDRIP

The four systems discussed above are interconnected:

1. **Treaty interpretation principles** establish how courts read treaties — generously, in favour of Indigenous peoples, with attention to oral promises and the honour of the Crown.

2. **The TRC** documented how the Crown systematically violated these treaties through the residential school system and other assimilationist policies, constituting cultural genocide.

3. **The specific claims process** was created to address specific historical breaches of treaties and other obligations — but has become a backlog crisis that delays justice by decades.

4. **UNDRIP implementation** represents the most recent framework for transforming the relationship, but its implementation has been uneven and underfunded.

The fundamental tension remains: the courts have developed increasingly sophisticated principles for interpreting treaties in favour of Indigenous peoples, but the executive branch has created processes — specific claims, UNDRIP implementation — that are chronically underfunded, structurally conflicted, and slow to deliver justice.

As the TRC stated: "Canada must move from apology to action."
