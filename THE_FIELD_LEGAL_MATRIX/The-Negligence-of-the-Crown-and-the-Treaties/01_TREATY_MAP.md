## 1. ROYAL PROCLAMATION OF 1763

**The foundational document of Crown-Indigenous relations in Canada.**

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Date** | October 7, 1763 |
| **Issued by** | King George III |
| **Context** | Following Britain's victory in the Seven Years' War; aftermath of Pontiac's War |
| **Status** | ACTIVE — Referenced in Section 25 of the Constitution Act, 1982 |

### Key Provisions

1. **Indian Territory Reserved**: Defined the land west of the established colonies as "Indian Territories" — a vast reserve where Indigenous peoples "should not be molested or disturbed" by settlers.

2. **Treaty-Making Required**: Established that no land could be purchased from Indigenous peoples except through the Crown. Only the Crown could negotiate for Indigenous lands. This created the constitutional framework for all subsequent treaties.

3. **Indian Department**: Established the Indian Department as the primary liaison between the Crown and First Nations.

4. **Land Prohibition Line**: Drew a boundary along the Appalachian Mountains; all lands west were reserved for Indigenous occupation.

### Legal Significance

- Often called the "Indian Magna Carta" or "Indian Bill of Rights"
- Section 25 of the Constitution Act, 1982 confirms: "Nothing in this Charter abrogates or derogates from any aboriginal, treaty or other rights or freedoms that pertain to the aboriginal peoples of Canada"
- In *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* (1997), the Supreme Court found the Proclamation's basic principles were generally applicable in BC
- The Proclamation does NOT create Indigenous rights — it acknowledges and confirms pre-existing rights

### Legal Status Summary

- **Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982** recognizes and affirms Peace and Friendship Treaty rights
- *R v Simon* (1985): SCC confirmed Treaty of 1752 is a valid, enforceable treaty
- *R v Marshall* (1999): SCC ruled hunting and fishing rights guaranteed in Treaties of 1760–1761 were never terminated; Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, and Passamaquoddy have right to hunt, fish, and gather in pursuit of a "moderate livelihood"

- Treaties provided for reserves to be held by the Crown "for the use and benefit" of Indigenous groups
- The 1827 Chippewas land surrender (2.7 million acres) retained less than 1% as reserves

## 5. ROBINSON TREATIES (1850)

**Two interconnected treaties that secured the Upper Great Lakes for the Crown.**

### Robinson-Superior Treaty

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Date** | September 7, 1850 |
| **Location** | Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario |
| **Crown Representative** | William Benjamin Robinson |
| **Parties** | Crown ↔ Ojibwa (Anishinaabe) Chiefs inhabiting the north shore of Lake Superior |
| **Territory** | Lake Superior shoreline from Batchawana Bay to Pigeon River, inland to height of land (Great Lakes drainage divide) — approximately 400 miles of shoreline |
| **Crown Treaty Number** | 60 |
| **Status** | ACTIVE — Subject of *Ontario (AG) v. Restoule* (SCC 2024); $10 billion settlement for Robinson-Huron |

**Terms**:
- Lump sum: £2,000
- Annual annuity: £500 (originally £1,000 total for both treaties)
- Right to hunt and fish in perpetuity on ceded lands
- Three reserves allocated
- Augmentation clause: annuities to increase if ceded territory produces sufficient revenue

### Robinson-Huron Treaty

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Date** | September 9, 1850 |
| **Location** | Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario |
| **Crown Representative** | William Benjamin Robinson |
| **Parties** | Crown ↔ Ojibwa Chiefs of Lake Huron shore |
| **Territory** | Lake Huron shoreline from Batchawana Bay to Penetanguishene, plus islands, inland to height of land |
| **Crown Treaty Number** | 61 |
| **Status** | ACTIVE — $10 billion settlement announced June 2023 (Canada + Ontario) |

**Terms**:
- Lump sum: £2,000
- Annual annuity: £600
- Right to hunt and fish in perpetuity
- 21 new reserves
- Augmentation clause

### Signatory First Nations (Partial List)

**Robinson Superior**:
Animbiigoo Zaagi'igan Anishinaabek, Biinjitiwabik Zaaging Anishnabek, Bingwi Neyaashi Anishinaabek, Fort William First Nation, Gull Bay First Nation, Long Lake 58 First Nation, Michipicoten First Nation, Pays Plat First Nation, Pic Mobert First Nation, Ojibways of the Pic River First Nation, Red Rock Indian Band

**Robinson Huron**:
Atikameksheng Anishnawbek, Batchewana First Nation, Dokis First Nation, Garden River First Nation, Henvey Inlet First Nation, Magnetawan First Nation, Mississauga First Nation, Nipissing First Nation, Sagamok Anishnawbek, Serpent River First Nation, Shawanaga First Nation, Sheshegwaning First Nation, Temagami First Nation, Thessalon First Nation, Wahnapitae First Nation, Wasauksing First Nation, Whitefish River First Nation

### Key Legal Issue

The annuities have been frozen at $4 per person since 1875. In *Ontario (AG) v. Restoule* (2024), the SCC ruled the Crown must exercise its discretion to increase annuities — the augmentation clause is not merely aspirational.

## 9. NUMBERED TREATIES (1871–1921)

**The most significant series of Crown-Indigenous treaties. Covered all of Alberta, Saskatchewan, Manitoba, parts of Ontario, BC, Yukon, and Northwest Territories.**

### Overview

- **First wave**: Treaties 1–7 (1871–1877) — Prairie settlement and Canadian Pacific Railway
- **Gap**: 1877–1899 (22 years)
- **Second wave**: Treaties 8–11 (1899–1921) — Northern resource extraction

---

| Field | Detail |
|-------|--------|
| **Date Signed** | August 3, 1871 |
| **Location** | Lower Fort Garry (Stone Fort), Manitoba |
| **Crown Commissioners** | Treaty Commissioners (including A.G. Archibald) |
| **Parties** | Crown ↔ Anishinaabe (Ojibwe/Saulteaux) and Swampy Cree |
| **Territory** | Southern Manitoba — approximately 160,000 km² |

### Key Modern Treaty: JBNQA (1975)

**The first modern treaty. Signed in response to Quebec hydroelectric development without Indigenous consultation.**

- $168.8 million for Cree
- $91 million for Inuit
- Land, environmental and social protection system
- Income security program for hunters and trappers
- 24 complementary agreements by 2014
- Established precedent for all subsequent modern treaties

### Key Modern Treaty: Nunavut (1993)

- **Created Canada's newest territory** (April 1, 1999)
- 350,000 km² of land and resource rights
- Percentage of royalties from resource exploitation on Nunavut lands
- The largest Indigenous land claim settlement in Canadian history

### Key Modern Treaty: Nisga'a (2000)

- **First modern treaty in British Columbia**
- First treaty in Canada to incorporate both land claims and constitutionally protected self-government provisions
- 1,991 km² of land in Nass River valley
- $196.1 million capital transfer over 15 years
- Indian Act no longer applies to Nisga'a people
- Nisga'a Lisims Government established with law-making authority

## CRITICAL OBSERVATIONS FOR THE NEGLIGENCE ANALYSIS

### 1. The Oral vs. Written Divide

Every category of treaty has a fundamental problem: **what was promised orally vs. what was recorded in writing.** This is documented across:

- Peace and Friendship Treaties (Mi'kmaq oral tradition vs. Crown text)
- Numbered Treaties (oral negotiations vs. written documents; testimony of Duncan Campbell Scott's diaries, Breynat's 1921 negotiations)
- Douglas Treaties (oral agreements vs. HBC standardized forms filled in later)
- Robinson Treaties (oral negotiations vs. Robinson's prepared documents)

### 2. Unfulfilled Promises

Across all treaty categories, the Crown has systematically failed to deliver:

- **Reserves**: Often too small, wrongly located, or reduced over time
- **Annuities**: Frozen since 1875 (Robinson Treaties at $4/person) or never increased
- **Education**: Schools not built or underfunded
- **Healthcare**: Medicine chests never delivered or inadequate
- **Agricultural assistance**: Not provided or insufficient
- **Hunting/fishing rights**: Extinguished by development without consent
- **Famine assistance**: Clause in Treaty 6 invoked during crises but often unmet

### 3. The Assimilation Agenda

- Treaties 1–7 were vehicles for "existing and future assimilation policies in the Northwest" (Government of Canada)
- Indian Act (1876) imposed control over Indigenous peoples' lives
- 1927: Indian Act made it **illegal** to raise funds for Indigenous rights and land claims
- Residential schools (1831–1996): Treaty obligations for education perverted into forced cultural genocide

### 4. Unextinguished Title

- **British Columbia**: Most of the province remains **unceded by treaty** (only Douglas Treaties + Nisga'a + 3 other modern treaties cover small areas)
- **Peace and Friendship Treaties**: Mi'kmaq maintain they never ceded land
- **Modern treaties**: Explicitly recognize Aboriginal title as the starting point
- The Supreme Court in *Delgamuukw* (1997) and *Haida Nation* (2004) confirmed Aboriginal title exists throughout BC

### 5. The Gap (1877–1899)

A 22-year gap between Treaty 7 (1877) and Treaty 8 (1899) when the Crown suspended treaty-making while it focused on Prairie settlement and railway construction. During this period, First Nations were reviewing treaty terms and organizing across territories — but the Crown was not negotiating.
