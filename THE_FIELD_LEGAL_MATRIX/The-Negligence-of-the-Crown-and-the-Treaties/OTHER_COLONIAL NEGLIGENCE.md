# THE NEGLIGENCE OF EMPIRE: French, Spanish, Portuguese & Dutch Colonial Treaty Violations

> *"The crown is not the only empire that signed and broke. France, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands built identical architectures of extraction, abandonment, and denial across every continent they touched."*

---

## THESIS

The British Crown was not alone in its pattern of colonial negligence. **Four other European powers** — France, Spain, Portugal, and the Netherlands — established the same global architecture: **sign → extract → abandon**. Each empire signed treaties or agreements promising protection, governance, or partnership. Each extracted resources and labor. Each systematically broke its promises. Each departed when extraction became unprofitable or resistance too costly. The legal frameworks these empires created — the Code de l'Indigénat, the Treaty of Tordesillas, the Cultivation System, the encomienda — are the foundations of colonial negligence worldwide. Their consequences persist in orphan wells, stolen lands, broken communities, and unresolved legal obligations across the former colonial world.

## I. FRENCH COLONIAL NEGLIGENCE

### 1. ALGERIA — 132 Years of Genocide, Broken Promises, Land Seizure

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Tafna (1837) between France and Amir Abd al-Qadir; Ordonnance du 22 juillet 1834; French Constitution of 1848 declaring Algeria "an integral part of France."

**What France Promised:**
- The Treaty of Tafna (1837) recognized Abd al-Qadir as sovereign of territories in Oran Province not under French control
- France promised to respect Islamic endowments (waqf) as part of the surrender terms
- The 1848 Constitution promised that as "citizens of France," Algerians would receive the rights of the Republic — liberty, equality, fraternity

**What France Failed to Deliver:**
- The Treaty of Tafna was **unilaterally broken** by France in 1839 when French troops marched through the Iron Gates — Abd al-Qadir claimed breach and renewed jihad
- Abd al-Qadir surrendered in 1841 on a promise he would remain in Algeria — **France exiled him** first to France, then to Damascus
- Islamic endowments were **confiscated and looted** in 1843, violating the surrender terms
- The 1848 Constitution made Algeria "part of France" — but Algerian Muslims were classified as **"French subjects" without the rights of citizens**. To become citizens, they had to renounce Sharia — which virtually none did
- The **Indigenous People Law (1871)** granted European settlers ownership of lands while Algerian workers received only 20% of production
- The **Crémieux Decree (1870)** granted French citizenship to Algerian Jews while excluding Muslims — a deliberate divide-and-rule strategy
- Between 500,000 and 1 million Algerians were killed in the first three decades of conquest (out of 3 million). The total death toll over 132 years is estimated at **5.6 to 10 million**
- By 1960, after 120 years of French rule, only **10% of Algerian Muslims were literate**
- Algeria was not recognized as a war of independence by France until **1999** — 37 years after independence

**How This = Abandonment:**
France invaded Algeria to avoid repaying debts (the Bakri-Busnach affair). It conquered, extracted, and when resistance became too costly, departed — leaving 1.5 million dead in the war alone. France refused reparations. In 2025, Algeria introduced a bill to criminalize French colonial rule. The colonial extraction model — **extract first, remediate never** — created a resource curse that persists.

**Orphan Well Connection:**
Algeria's oil infrastructure, developed under French colonial concessions, produced thousands of wells. The French colonial model — extract wealth, abandon communities, leave contamination — is identical to the orphan well pattern in Alberta.

### 2. SENEGAL — Four Communes, Broken Promises of Citizenship

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaties with local rulers (1850s–1880s); Second Republic decree of April 27, 1848 granting citizenship to inhabitants of the Four Communes.

**What France Promised:**
- Inhabitants of Saint-Louis, Gorée, Rufisque, and Dakar would receive **full French citizenship** — the same rights as citizens in the Metropole
- The policy of **assimilation** — that colonial subjects could become French through education, language, and adoption of French customs
- Political representation in the French National Assembly

**What France Failed to Deliver:**
- Despite legal citizenship, **substantial legal and social barriers** prevented the full exercise of rights, especially by those seen as "full-blooded Africans"
- Those who maintained Islamic or customary law (the *originaires*) were termed *sujets* in practice — citizens in name only
- The parliamentary seat for Senegal was **abolished by Napoleon III in 1852**, reinstated, abolished again in 1875, and only permanently restored in 1879
- The *Code de l'Indigénat* was imposed on Indigenous people throughout French Africa, allowing **imprisonment without trial, forced labor, and summary fines** — while the Four Communes were technically exempt
- Assimilation was **"primarily described in public rhetoric to justify and favorably exaggerate French action"** — the reality was limited intermediary construction, not genuine equality
- Even *évolués* (Africans who renounced Islamic law for full French status) **faced substantial discrimination in Africa and the Metropole alike**
- The rest of Senegal — outside the Four Communes — remained under the brutal *Indigénat* system until the **Lamine Guèye law of 1946**, nearly a century after the promise of assimilation

**How This = Abandonment:**
France promised citizenship and assimilation, then systematically undermined it. The Four Communes were "islands of republican legality in a sea of colonial authoritarianism." The promise was never extended in good faith — it was a tool of control, not genuine partnership.

**Orphan Well Connection:**
Senegal's colonial economy was structured for peanut extraction — cash crops for French benefit, not local food security. The extraction model left behind degraded land and dependent communities.

### 3. IVORY COAST — Assimilation as Fiction

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaties with local rulers (1840s–1890s); incorporation into French West Africa.

**What France Promised:**
- Assimilation policy — that colonial subjects would eventually become French citizens
- Administration in partnership with local rulers
- Development for the benefit of all inhabitants

**What France Failed to Deliver:**
- Ivory Coast was designated a *colonie d'exploitation* — a colony of economic exploitation
- The *Code de l'Indigénat* was imposed, allowing forced labor, summary punishments, and no due process
- Cocoa, coffee, and timber extracted for French benefit
- Local rulers were co-opted as instruments of extraction, then abandoned
- The territory was governed as an **extraction zone**, not a partnership

### 4. VIETNAM / INDOCHINA — Broken Promises, Resource Extraction, Forced Labor

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Saigon (1862); Patenotre Treaty (1884); Treaties of 1862–1884 establishing French control over Vietnam, Laos, Cambodia.

**What France Promised:**
- "Protection" and "civilization" under the protectorate system
- Administration for the benefit of all inhabitants
- The 1946 agreement recognized the Democratic Republic of Vietnam as a "free state"

**What France Failed to Deliver:**
- Indochina was designated a **"colonie d'exploitation"** — colony of economic exploitation
- Governor-General Paul Doumer (1897–1902) established the model: **"the principle of colonization, economic development, has hardly even begun in Indochina"** — the goal was extraction
- Vietnam became a **source of raw materials and a market for tariff-protected French goods**
- The French government established a **near-monopoly on opium, salt, and rice alcohol** — forming 44% of the colonial budget in 1920
- Rice cultivation quadrupled between 1880 and 1930 — but **individual peasant rice consumption decreased**. New lands were sold to the highest bidder, not distributed to the landless
- **Forced labor** was recruited with no protection in mines and rubber plantations
- Rubber plantations expanded massively — but only a fraction of profits was reinvested
- The 1946 agreement promising a "free state" **fell apart in months** through broken promises and disagreements
- The name "Vietnam" was **removed from official use** because the word was associated with self-rule
- The **First Indochina War (1946–1954)** followed: France tried to re-colonize after promising freedom

**How This = Abandonment:**
France extracted rubber, coal, rice, and opium for nearly a century. When Vietnam fought for independence, France returned to re-colonize. The pattern — sign, extract, abandon, return to re-extract — is the most cynical form of colonial negligence.

**Orphan Well Connection:**
Indochina's coal mines, rubber plantations, and oil infrastructure were built for extraction only. When France departed, it left behind degraded land, destroyed communities, and contamination — identical to the orphan well pattern.

### 5. HAITI — Colonial Exploitation Legacy

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaties of Paris (1783); French colonial administration of Saint-Domingue.

**What France Promised:**
- Administration and development of the colony
- The Code Noir (1685) nominally regulated slavery with protections

**What France Failed to Deliver:**
- Saint-Domingue was the **most profitable colony in the world** — built entirely on slave labor
- The Code Noir was systematically violated — working conditions were so brutal that the average enslaved person survived **7–10 years** after arrival
- France extracted **more wealth from Haiti than from all 13 American colonies combined**
- After the Haitian Revolution (1804), France **demanded 150 million francs** (later reduced to 90 million) as "compensation" for lost slaves and property — Haiti was forced to pay this debt until **1947**
- This debt — **reparations paid by the enslaved to the enslavers** — crippled Haiti's economy for two centuries
- France never paid reparations for slavery or colonial exploitation

**How This = Abandonment:**
Haiti's poverty is a direct product of French colonial extraction. France extracted billions, then demanded payment from the formerly enslaved for their own freedom. This is not negligence — it is **theft compounded by extortion**.

### 6. NEW CALEDONIA — Kanak Land Rights, 170 Years of Dispossession

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of 1853 (French annexation); Matignon-Oudinot Accords (1988); Nouméa Accord (1998).

**What France Promised:**
- The Nouméa Accord (1998) acknowledged "the devastation wrought by the early stages of French colonialism and its lasting effects on Kanak identity and well-being"
- The Accord provided for "gradual and irreversible transfer of power" from France to New Caledonia
- Land reform through ADRAF (Rural Development and Land Planning Agency) to redistribute land to Kanak clans

**What France Failed to Deliver:**
- The **Decree of October 5, 1862** declared all unoccupied land as French state property — virtually all Kanak land was seized
- By 1902, Kanak reserves made up **less than one-tenth** of Grande Terre
- Kanak population dropped from **60,000 to 27,000** by 1920 — through disease, massacre, and forced displacement
- The **Code de l'Indigénat** imposed on Kanak people prohibited leaving reserves without permission, allowed imprisonment without trial, and forced head taxes
- Nickel mining polluted Kanak lands and rivers
- The 2021 sovereignty referendum **took place during COVID-19, disregarding Kanak customary mourning** and despite objections of Kanak authorities
- In 2024, the French Parliament adopted a bill **dismantling the Nouméa Accord** by unfreezing the electoral body — UN experts called this a severe undermining of Kanak human rights
- The "Marty Project" threatened to **dismantle Kanak customary institutions, customary law, and land rights**
- Land claims remain **stalled and outstanding** — ADRAF has been slow and underfunded

**How This = Abandonment:**
France seized Kanak land in 1853, reduced the population by more than half, confined survivors to 7% of the main island, and 170 years later is **actively dismantling the protections** it promised. The Nouméa Accord's "irreversible transfer of power" is being reversed by the very power that made the promise.

**Orphan Well Connection:**
New Caledonia's nickel mining industry — built on Kanak land, polluting Kanak water — follows the extraction model: take the land, extract the minerals, abandon the contamination.

## II. SPANISH COLONIAL NEGLIGENCE

### 1. MEXICO — Broken Promises, Land Seizures, Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo (1848); Protocol of Querétaro; Gadsden Treaty (1853).

**What Spain/Promised (via Mexico to the US):**
- Article VIII: Property rights of Mexicans in ceded territory would be **"inviolably respected"**
- Article IX: Mexicans would be **"maintained and protected in the free enjoyment of their liberty and property"**
- The Protocol of Querétaro assured that suppressing Article X did **not intend to annul grants of lands made by Mexico**

**What Was Failed to Deliver:**
- The **US Senate deleted Article X** from the Treaty — removing the guarantee of protection for Mexican land grants
- The Treaty was declared **"not self-executing"** — meaning property rights depended on Congressional legislation, not the Treaty itself
- In California, **27% of land grant claims were rejected**; in New Mexico, **76% were rejected**
- The Court of Private Land Claims (1891) applied **more stringent criteria** than either the Surveyor General or the California Commission
- **5.3 million acres** confirmed to 84 non-Pueblo Indian community grants were **virtually all lost** through transfers, tax foreclosures, and partitioning suits
- The Supreme Court in *United States v. Sandoval* (1897) **transferred all community land titles to the federal government** without due process
- Hispanic ranchers lost access to **water, grazing lands, and communal property** — the economic foundation of their communities
- In 2023, descendants of land grant holders reported ongoing struggles: government policies imposed restrictions on land use for endangered species and environmental concerns, violating the Treaty

**How This = Abandonment:**
The US promised to respect Mexican property rights, then systematically destroyed them through legislation, court decisions, and administrative action. The land seizure that began with the Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo is **the original orphan well** — a broken promise that left communities without resources.

**Orphan Well Connection:**
The Treaty of Guadalupe Hidalgo created the legal framework for land dispossession across the American Southwest. The same extraction model — take the land, extract the value, abandon the people — is the architecture of orphan wells.

### 2. PERU — Colonial Extraction, Encomienda, Forced Labor

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Conquest of the Inca Empire (1533); encomienda system; mita (forced labor) system.

**What Spain Promised:**
- "Protection" of indigenous peoples under the encomienda system
- Christian salvation through conversion
- Administration under the Laws of Burgos (1512) and New Laws (1542), which nominally restricted exploitation

**What Spain Failed to Deliver:**
- The encomienda system was **de facto slavery** — indigenous peoples were forced to work in mines and on estates
- The **mita system** at Potosí forced indigenous men to work in silver mines under lethal conditions — mercury poisoning, cave-ins, and exhaustion killed hundreds of thousands
- The New Laws (1542) nominally abolished the encomienda but were **systematically evaded** by colonists
- **Indigenous tribute** was expanded and intensified over three centuries — a 1734 census reform increased tribute by 60% in some provinces
- Silver from Potosí flowed to Spain — estimated at **$45 billion in today's terms** — while indigenous populations were decimated
- The tribute system was **not abolished until 1854 in Peru, 1857 in Ecuador, and 1874 in Bolivia** — decades after independence
- Corruption and inefficiency plagued colonial administration — one *visitador* in 1683 described officials as "the most blind and careless seen in many centuries"

**How This = Abandonment:**
Spain extracted silver, gold, and labor from Peru for 300 years. The encomienda and mita systems were slavery by another name. When independence came, Spain departed without remediation, leaving behind exhausted mines, depleted populations, and extractive institutions.

### 3. COLOMBIA — Broken Promises, Contraband, Colonial Neglect

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Viceroyalty of New Granada (1717/1739); various colonial administrative reforms.

**What Spain Promised:**
- Administration and development of the territory
- Protection of indigenous peoples
- The Bourbon reforms promised territorial integration and wealth generation

**What Spain Failed to Deliver:**
- Colombia's Caribbean coast was **effectively abandoned** — between 1700 and 1763, **no Spanish merchant ships** arrived at the port of Riohacha. Residents obtained necessities through contraband with Jamaica and Curaçao
- Spain's colonial administration was **unable to prevent British and Dutch smuggling** throughout the Caribbean coast
- The Guajira Peninsula was a **lawless frontier** — indigenous Wayuu people obtained firearms through contraband, making them militarily untamable
- Colonial authorities **complicit in contraband** — local officials profited from illegal trade rather than enforcing Spanish monopoly
- The Viceroyalty of New Granada was created for **geopolitical reasons**, not for the benefit of inhabitants
- Infrastructure development was negligible outside of Bogotá and major mining areas

**How This = Abandonment:**
Spain neglected Colombia's Caribbean coast for centuries, allowing contraband to flourish because it could not be bothered to provide legitimate trade. The colonial model — extract from the interior, ignore the periphery — left regions underdeveloped and dependent on smuggling economies.

### 4. VENEZUELA — Colonial Exploitation, Plantation Economy, Neglect

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Welser concession (1528); Captaincy General of Venezuela (1777); Guipuzcoana Company (1728).

**What Spain Promised:**
- Administration and development through the Captaincy General
- Protection of indigenous peoples
- Economic development through the Guipuzcoana Company

**What Spain Failed to Deliver:**
- The **Welser concession** (German bankers) was a disaster — the Germans pursued gold with "brutal disregard for indigenous communities and little concern for sustainable colonization"
- Venezuela was **"of no economic importance"** to Spain for 250 years — administered from neighboring colonies, lacking political unity
- The **Guipuzcoana Company** (1728) monopolized cacao exports, paying growers low prices while enriching Basque merchants
- The 1749 rebellion led by Juan Francisco de León was **brutally crushed** by troops from Santo Domingo and Spain
- Venezuela's petroleum resources were **noticed as early as 1500 but ignored for four centuries**
- Spain showed **"little interest in Venezuela's agriculture"** — obsessed with precious metals from other territories
- Agricultural surplus was sold to **British, French, and Dutch traders** labeled as smugglers — because Spain refused to trade with its own colony

**How This = Abandonment:**
Spain treated Venezuela as a neglected backyard for 250 years, then imposed monopoly companies when it finally noticed the colony. The colonial pattern — neglect, extract, suppress resistance — created conditions for the first independence declaration in South America (1811).

### 5. ARGENTINA — Land Seizures, Encomienda

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Various colonial administrative arrangements; Viceroyalty of the Río de la Plata (1776).

**What Spain Promised:**
- Administration and development
- Protection of indigenous peoples under the encomienda system

**What Spain Failed to Deliver:**
- The encomienda system imposed forced labor on indigenous populations
- Land was seized for Spanish settlers and ranchers
- The Viceroyalty was created late (1776) primarily as a **geopolitical counterweight** to Portuguese and British expansion — not for the benefit of inhabitants
- Indigenous peoples of the Pampas and Patagonia were **dispossessed through military campaigns** (the *Conquista del Desierto* began under Spanish colonial precedents)

### 6. PHILIPPINES — Colonial Rule, Forced Labor, Resource Extraction

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Paris (1898) ceding Philippines to the US; 333 years of Spanish colonial rule.

**What Spain Promised:**
- Christian salvation and "civilization"
- Administration under the encomienda and later hacienda systems
- The *polo y servicios* (forced labor) system was nominally limited

**What Spain Failed to Deliver:**
- The encomienda system was imposed — indigenous Filipinos forced to work in agriculture and public works
- The **polo y servicios** forced labor system was widespread and abusive
- The Gomburza execution (1872) — three Filipino priests executed on fabricated charges — sparked the independence movement
- José Rizal, the national hero, was executed in 1896 for advocating reform
- Spain **sold the Philippines to the United States** for $20 million in the Treaty of Paris (1898) — treating a colony of 7 million people as a commodity
- The Philippine-American War (1899–1902) killed an estimated **200,000–1 million Filipino civilians**

**How This = Abandonment:**
Spain ruled the Philippines for 333 years, extracted labor and resources, then **sold the colony like property** when it could no longer hold it. The people of the Philippines were not consulted, not compensated, and not freed — they were transferred from one colonial master to another.

## III. PORTUGUESE COLONIAL NEGLIGENCE

### 1. BRAZIL — Colonial Extraction, Slavery, Mercantilism

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Tordesillas (1494); various colonial administrative arrangements; Methuen Treaty (1703).

**What Portugal Promised:**
- Administration and development of the colony
- The Crown maintained "absolute power" but promised to govern for the benefit of the colony

**What Portugal Failed to Deliver:**
- Brazil was the **largest destination of African captives in the Atlantic slave trade** — one in two enslaved Africans were taken to Portuguese America
- The plantation economy was built entirely on **slave labor** — sugar, gold, diamonds extracted for Portuguese and British benefit
- The **metropolitan exclusivity** system gave Portugal monopoly rights over purchase of colonial products and sale of European goods — extracting "superprofits"
- Brazil's wealth **ended up making Britain rich** through the Methuen Treaty (1703) — "Brazil was a colony of Portugal, but its wealth ended up making Britain rich in a complex system of international domination"
- The colonial economy was structured for **extraction, not development** — limited manufacturing was prohibited
- When the Portuguese court fled to Rio (1808), it **increased taxation on Brazil** to fund military conflicts in the Río de la Plata
- Brazil's sugar and cotton producers faced **protectionist measures** from Britain and France — Portugal failed to defend its colony's economic interests
- Independence (1822) came under a **Portuguese-born emperor** (D. Pedro I) who intended to reunite the empire — true independence only came when Brazilians expelled him in 1831

**How This = Abandonment:**
Portugal extracted sugar, gold, diamonds, and slave labor from Brazil for 300 years. The colony's wealth financed Portuguese and British industrialization while Brazil remained dependent. When Portugal finally lost Brazil, it left behind a plantation economy built on slavery and extraction.

### 2. ANGOLA — Slave Trade, Forced Labor, Colonial Exploitation

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Simulambuco (1885); various concession company charters; forced labor (*shibalo*) system.

**What Portugal Promised:**
- "Civilization" and development through the colonial civilizing mission
- Protection of indigenous peoples
- The chartered companies would develop the territories they administered

**What Portugal Failed to Deliver:**
- Angola was **the principal source of enslaved Africans** for the Atlantic slave trade — an estimated **4,650,000 Africans** were transported from Angolan ports
- The colony was founded in 1575 specifically to **facilitate the slave trade**
- The **forced labor system (*shibalo*)** persisted officially until the 1960s — unique in its longevity and brutality
- Chartered companies (Mozambique Company, Niassa Company) were granted **quasi-sovereign powers** but failed to provide development capital
- The **Companhia de Diamantes de Angola (Diamang)** operated as a "corporate state" — employing 250,000 workers under forced labor conditions
- Portugal was **"eager to allow large corporations to use forced labour"** — distinguishing it from other colonizers
- After 400 years of Portuguese rule, Angola was left with **"total political instability which devastated the economy and human losses"**
- The independence wars (1961–1974) consumed Portuguese resources — Portugal held on to Angola **"during the 20th century for a few decades longer than most other European powers"**

**How This = Abandonment:**
Portugal extracted 4.65 million enslaved Africans from Angola over four centuries. When forced labor was nominally abolished, it was replaced by chartered companies that operated as states within states. When Portugal finally departed in 1975, it left behind a country devastated by 400 years of extraction and a brutal civil war.

### 3. MOZAMBIQUE — Chartered Company Rule, Forced Labor, Underdevelopment

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Simulambuco (1885); charter of the Companhia de Moçambique (1891); charter of the Companhia do Niassa (1891).

**What Portugal Promised:**
- The chartered companies would **"pacify and develop"** their territories
- Effective occupation would preserve Portuguese sovereignty
- Development capital would flow into the territories

**What Portugal Failed to Deliver:**
- The companies **"failed to provide the capital essential for the economic development of their territories"**
- The areas under company rule were **"the most economically backward in the colony"** when chartered rule ended
- The Niassa Company's concessionary **"could scarcely obtain the £10,000 required to activate the charter"** — the company was capitalized on paper only
- **"Years before 1919 were years of oppression and exploitation, rebellion and mass emigration"** in the Niassa Company's territory
- The Niassa Company's rule produced **"an enclave of desolation and poverty"**
- Foreigners purchased company shares, **diluting Portuguese influence and leading to denationalization**
- The companies **"facilitated external intervention in Mozambique and the denationalization of Portuguese territory"** — the opposite of their intended purpose
- Cotton cultivation was imposed on **hundreds of thousands of Africans** through a "repressive system"
- Forced labor persisted — Mozambique exported **labor to South African mines** well into the 20th century

**How This = Abandonment:**
Portugal outsourced colonial governance to private companies that had neither the capital nor the incentive to develop the territory. When company rule ended, Mozambique was **more underdeveloped than when it began**. The chartered company model was negligence by design — Portugal delegated its obligations to entities that had no obligation to the people.

### 4. TIMOR-LESTE — Colonial Abandonment, Land Seizure

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Lisbon (1859) dividing Timor between Portugal and Netherlands; various land concession laws.

**What Portugal Promised:**
- Administration and development of the territory
- Protection of indigenous land rights
- The *Carta de Lei* guaranteed property transmission through succession according to local custom

**What Portugal Failed to Deliver:**
- In 1900, land was **seized and privatized**, granted as concessions for plantations to **"Lisbon-based men with plantation holdings elsewhere in the Portuguese Empire"**
- Indigenous land tenure systems were **clashed with liberal land policies**, leading to resistance and social upheaval
- The colonial government created a **"paternalist view"** of Timorese as "prodigal and incapable to navigate modern formal land tenure systems"
- Portugal **abandoned Timor-Leste** when Indonesia invaded in 1975 — Portugal had been the administering power but offered no resistance and no protection
- Indonesia occupied Timor-Leste for 24 years (1975–1999) — during which an estimated **100,000–180,000 Timorese died** from violence, famine, and disease
- Portugal's abandonment was so complete that it took **no responsibility** for the consequences of its departure

**How This = Abandonment:**
Portugal ruled Timor-Leste for 400 years, seized its land, extracted its resources, then **walked away when Indonesia invaded**. The colonial power that promised protection offered none when it mattered most. This is the purest form of colonial negligence — **abandonment without even the pretense of obligation**.

## IV. DUTCH COLONIAL NEGLIGENCE

### 1. INDONESIA — Cultivation System, Forced Labor, Broken Promises

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Various treaties establishing Dutch East India Company (VOC) control; Cultivation System (1830); Agrarian Law (1870); various treaties with local rulers.

**What the Netherlands Promised:**
- "Protection" of local rulers and their territories
- Administration under the VOC and later the colonial state
- The Cultivation System was presented as beneficial — teaching "self-discipline" to the "lazy Javanese"

**What the Netherlands Failed to Deliver:**
- The **Cultivation System (1830–1870)** was one of the most extractive forced labor regimes in history:
  - 20% of village land devoted to government export crops — in reality, **significantly more**
  - Peasants forced to work on plantations for **66 days per year** — often more
  - Up to **1 million farmers** forced to work at peak
  - Sugar production increased from 6,700 tonnes (1831) to **100,000+ tonnes** (1850s) — entirely through forced labor
  - Net transfers to the Netherlands (*batig slot*) amounted to **almost 4% of Dutch GDP** and **over 50% of government revenue** in the 1850s
- The system caused **widespread famines and epidemics in the 1840s** — cash crops grown instead of rice
  - Mortality rates rose by **as much as 30%** during this period
  - **Several tens of thousands** perished from starvation and disease
- The *kultuurprocenten* (cultivation percentages) incentivized colonial officials to **push peasants harder** — the more revenue generated, the larger the officials' payments
- The Nederlandsche Handel-Maatschappij (NHM) held **monopoly rights** over trade — underpaying farmers for cash crops while overcharging for imported food
- The Agrarian Law (1870) declared all uncultivated land **property of the colonial government** — effectively **depriving Javanese of communal lands**
- After the Cultivation System was abolished, a new system of **penal sanctions for labor offenses** persisted — workers sentenced to chain gangs for "inadequate work performance"
- The **Max Havelaar novel (1860)** by Multatuli exposed the hidden brutality of the system — but reforms came slowly
- Indonesia fought a **four-year war of independence (1945–1949)** before the Netherlands recognized sovereignty

**How This = Abandonment:**
The Netherlands built its modern economy on the backs of Javanese forced labor. The Cultivation System extracted wealth equivalent to half the Dutch national budget while causing famines that killed tens of thousands. When Indonesia fought for independence, the Netherlands fought back for four years before reluctantly letting go.

**Orphan Well Connection:**
Indonesia's sugar plantations, coffee estates, and mining operations were built through forced labor and extracted for Dutch benefit. When the Dutch departed, they left behind degraded land, exploited workers, and a plantation economy that took decades to restructure.

### 2. SURINAME — Plantation Economy, Slavery, Indentured Labor

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Breda (1667); Society of Suriname (1683); abolition of slavery (1863).

**What the Netherlands Promised:**
- Administration through the Society of Suriname
- "Sovereign status" for Maroons through peace treaties in the 18th century
- Abolition of slavery (1863)

**What the Netherlands Failed to Deliver:**
- Suriname was **the archetype of plantation slavery** — sugar, coffee, and cotton produced entirely by enslaved labor
- The Society of Suriname was modeled on **French mercantilism** — designed to extract tropical commodities for Dutch benefit
- After abolition (1863), the Dutch imported **Javanese and Indian indentured workers** — people with "few rights" who were "often desperate enough to take their chances"
- The result was **one of the most ethnically diverse countries in the world** — not by choice but by colonial labor policy
- **Bauxite mining** dominated the economy — controlled by multinational corporations, not Surinamese
- Even before independence, **"most of the capital needed for development still came from external sources, in particular the Dutch government and large multinational corporations"**
- Suriname's **"vast interior could hardly be considered well connected"** — infrastructure built for extraction, not development

**How This = Abandonment:**
The Netherlands built Suriname on slavery, then replaced it with indentured labor. The plantation economy extracted wealth for centuries while the colony remained dependent. When Suriname gained independence (1975), the Dutch left behind a country structured for extraction, not self-sufficiency.

### 3. CARIBBEAN ISLANDS — Curaçao, Aruba, Bonaire, Sint Maarten, Saba, Sint Eustatius

**The Treaty/Agreement:** Treaty of Westphalia (1648); Treaty of Paris (1814); various colonial administrative arrangements.

**What the Netherlands Promised:**
- Administration and development
- Protection of inhabitants

**What the Netherlands Failed to Deliver:**
- **Curaçao** was a hub of the **Atlantic slave trade** — enslaved Africans processed and shipped through the island

- All islands experienced **minimal investment** in infrastructure, education, or sustainable development
