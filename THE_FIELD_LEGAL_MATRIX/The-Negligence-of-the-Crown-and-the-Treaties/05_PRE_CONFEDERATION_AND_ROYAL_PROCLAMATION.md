# Pre-Confederation Treaties and the Royal Proclamation of 1763

## Foundation Documents of Crown Obligations

---

# PART I: THE ROYAL PROCLAMATION OF 1763

## 1. Overview

- **Date**: October 7, 1763
- **Issued by**: King George III
- **Purpose**: Establish governance of territories acquired from France via the Treaty of Paris (1763), ending the Seven Years' War
- **Constitutional Status**: Referenced in Section 25 of the *Constitution Act, 1982*; often called the "Indian Magna Carta" or "Indian Bill of Rights"
- **Current Status**: **Still in force** as a constitutional document

## 2. Key Provisions

### 2.1 Recognition of Aboriginal Title

The Proclamation explicitly recognized that Indigenous peoples held rights to their territories:

> *"And whereas it is just and reasonable, and essential to our Interest, and the Security of our Colonies, that the several Nations or Tribes of Indians with whom We are connected, and who live under our Protection, should not be molested or disturbed in the Possession of such Parts of Our Dominions and Territories as, not having been ceded to or purchased by Us, are reserved to them, or any of them, as their Hunting Grounds."*

**Significance**: This is the first formal Crown recognition that Indigenous peoples possess **pre-existing rights** to land that the Crown must respect. The Proclamation did not **create** these rights—it **acknowledged** them.

### 2.2 Reservation of Indigenous Lands

The Proclamation reserved all lands west of the Appalachian Mountains (and east of the Hudson's Bay Company territory) as Indigenous "Hunting Grounds":

> *"And We do further declare it to be Our Royal Will and Pleasure, for the present as aforesaid, to reserve under our Sovereignty, Protection, and Dominion, for the Use of the said Indians, all the Lands and Territories not included within the Limits of Our said Three new Governments, or within the Limits of the Territory granted to the Hudson's Bay Company."*

### 2.3 Prohibition on Private Purchase

The Proclamation strictly prohibited private individuals from purchasing Indigenous lands:

> *"And we do hereby strictly forbid, on Pain of our Displeasure, all our loving Subjects from making any Purchases or Settlements whatever, or taking Possession of any of the Lands above reserved, without our especial Leave and Licence for that Purpose first obtained."*

### 2.4 The Treaty-Making Protocol

The Proclamation established the exclusive Crown authority to negotiate land surrenders:

> *"And whereas great Frauds and Abuses have been committed in purchasing Lands of the Indians, to the great Prejudice of our Interests, and to the great Dissatisfaction of the said Indians: in order therefore to prevent such Irregularities for the future, and to the End that the Indians may be convinced of our Justice, and determined Resolution to remove all reasonable Cause of Discontent, We do, with the Advice of our Privy Council, strictly enjoin and require, that no private Person do presume to make any Purchase from the said Indians of any Lands reserved to the said Indians... but that if, at any Time, any of the said Indians should be inclined to dispose of the said Lands, the same shall be Purchased only for Us, in our Name, at some publick Meeting or Assembly of the said Indians to be held for that Purpose by the Governor or Commander in Chief of our Colony respectively."*

This established the constitutional requirement that:
1. Only the Crown (not private persons) could purchase Indigenous lands
2. Purchases could only occur at **public meetings** with Indigenous peoples
3. Purchases required the **free choice** of Indigenous nations
4. The Governor or Commander in Chief had to conduct the process

### 2.5 Enforcement of Colonial Laws in Indigenous Territory

The Proclamation required officers to apprehend fugitives who fled to Indigenous territories:

> *"And we do further expressly enjoin and require all Officers whatever, as well Military as those Employed in the Management and Direction of Indian Affairs, within the Territories reserved as aforesaid for the use of the said Indians, to seize and apprehend all Persons whatever, who standing charged with Treason, Misprisions of Treason, Murders, or other Felonies or Misdemeanors, shall fly from Justice and take Refuge in the said Territory."*

## 3. What the Proclamation Promised to Indigenous Peoples

1. **Protection of possession**: Indigenous peoples would not be "molested or disturbed" in lands not ceded
2. **Reserved hunting grounds**: All unceded territories were reserved exclusively for Indigenous use
3. **Right to dispose of lands freely**: Indigenous nations could choose to sell lands, but only to the Crown
4. **Public and fair process**: Land transactions had to occur at public meetings with full disclosure
5. **Trade rights**: Trade with Indigenous peoples was to be "free and open to all our Subjects"
6. **Licensed traders only**: Traders required licences from the Governor, ensuring regulated commerce

## 4. How the Proclamation Established the "Duty to Consult"

The Proclamation created the foundational framework for what would evolve into the modern duty to consult:

### 4.1 Original Framework
- The Crown acknowledged that Indigenous peoples possessed **pre-existing rights** to their territories
- The Crown claimed ultimate "Sovereignty, Protection, and Dominion" over these territories but recognized Indigenous **possessory rights**
- The Crown created an **exclusive mechanism** for land acquisition through public meetings
- The Crown assumed a **protective role** over Indigenous lands

### 4.2 Evolution Through Case Law

The duty to consult was not explicitly stated in the Proclamation but has been interpreted as flowing from its principles:

- **Delgamuukw v. British Columbia (1997)**: Established that Aboriginal title exists and the Crown has obligations regarding it
- **Haida Nation v. British Columbia (2004)**: Formally recognized the duty to consult as flowing from the "honour of the Crown," which itself flows from the Proclamation's framework
- **Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia (2014)**: Confirmed the duty to consult extends to unproven claims and established the first declaration of Aboriginal title

### 4.3 Key Principles from Haida Nation

The Supreme Court held:
- The duty to consult is grounded in the **honour of the Crown**
- The duty arises when the Crown has **knowledge, real or constructive**, of the potential existence of Aboriginal rights or title
- The Crown **contemplates conduct** that might adversely affect those rights
- The duty is **proportionate** to the strength of the claim and seriousness of the potential adverse effect
- The duty is **not a veto** but requires a meaningful process of consultation in good faith

## 5. How the Proclamation Recognized Aboriginal Title

The Proclamation's recognition operates on multiple levels:

### 5.1 Acknowledgment of Pre-Existing Rights
The Proclamation did not grant rights—it **acknowledged** that Indigenous peoples already possessed rights to their territories. The language "not having been ceded to or purchased by Us" implies that these rights existed **prior to** and **independent of** the Crown.

### 5.2 The "Hunting Grounds" Concept
The Proclamation defined unceded Indigenous territories as "Hunting Grounds"—a term that recognized Indigenous use and occupancy while simultaneously framing it within colonial categories.

### 5.3 Constitutional Protection
Through Section 25 of the *Constitution Act, 1982*, the rights recognized in the Proclamation are now constitutionally protected:
- Section 25(a): "any rights or freedoms that have been recognized by the Royal Proclamation of October 7, 1763"
- This means Charter rights cannot override these recognized Indigenous rights

### 5.4 Judicial Interpretation

- **Calder v. Attorney-General of British Columbia (1973)**: The SCC split on whether the Proclamation applied to British Columbia. Justice Judson held the Proclamation recognized Aboriginal title as a legal right; Justice Martland dissented, arguing the Proclamation did not apply west of the Rockies.
- **R. v. Sparrow (1990)**: The SCC confirmed that Aboriginal rights exist as independent legal rights, and the Proclamation is one source of their recognition.
- **R. v. Marshall (1999)**: The SCC confirmed that Peace and Friendship Treaties (made under the Proclamation's framework) created ongoing treaty rights.

## 6. Current Legal Status

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 remains in force as a constitutional document:

- **Constitutional entrenchment**: Section 25 of the *Constitution Act, 1982* explicitly references the Proclamation
- **Living document**: Courts continue to interpret and apply its principles
- **Ongoing relevance**: The Proclamation's framework underpins modern treaty negotiations, land claims, and the duty to consult
- **Scope debate**: Whether the Proclamation applies to all of Canada or only parts where the British colonial framework operated remains debated. Justice Hall (1973) suggested it applies generally; the SCC has not definitively ruled on this point.

# PART II: PRE-CONFEDERATION TREATIES

## A. Peace and Friendship Treaties (1725-1779)

### 1. Overview

- **Parties**: British Crown and Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik (Maliseet), Abenaki, Penobscot, and Passamaquoddy peoples (Wabanaki Confederacy)
- **Territory**: Maritime provinces (Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island), Gaspé region of Quebec, and northeastern United States (Maine, New Hampshire)
- **Purpose**: End hostilities and establish trade relations
- **Current Status**: **Still in effect** — constitutionally protected under Section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982*

### 2. Treaty Chronology

| Treaty | Date | Parties | Key Feature |
|--------|------|---------|-------------|
| Treaty of Boston (Dummer's Treaty) | December 15, 1725 | Penobscot, Abenaki, British | Ended Dummer's War |
| Mascarene's Treaty | 1726 | Mi'kmaq, Maliseet, Passamaquoddy, British | Ratification of 1725 terms |
| Treaty of 1749 | August 15, 1749 | Maliseet, Chignecto Mi'kmaq, British | Renewed 1725 treaty |
| Treaty of 1752 | November 22, 1752 | Jean Baptiste Cope (Mi'kmaq), British | Renewed peace; annual presents promised |
| Maliseet-Passamaquoddy Treaty | February 22, 1760 | Maliseet, Passamaquoddy, British | Truck house clause |
| Mi'kmaq Treaties of 1760-61 | 1760-1761 | Various Mi'kmaq communities, British | Multiple local treaties |
| Treaty of 1778 | September 24, 1778 | Maliseet, Mi'kmaq, British | Non-assistance to Americans |
| Treaty of 1779 | September 22, 1779 | Mi'kmaq (Cape Tormentine to Chaleur Bay), British | Hunting/fishing rights guaranteed |

### 3. Key Provisions

**What the treaties promised:**
- Peace and friendship between the parties
- No surrender of land or resources
- Freedom to hunt, fish, and gather
- Establishment of truck houses for trade
- Annual presents (in some treaties)
- Protection from molestation

**What the treaties did NOT include:**
- No land surrender provisions
- No monetary compensation for land
- No reserve creation
- No extinguishment of Aboriginal title

### 4. Current Status and Crown Failures

**Legal status**: The Peace and Friendship Treaties remain in effect. The Supreme Court of Canada confirmed this in *R. v. Marshall* (1999), which:
- Recognized the treaty right to fish and trade for "necessaries" (a "moderate livelihood")
- Affirmed these rights are constitutionally protected under Section 35
- Applied to 34 Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, and Peskotomuhkati First Nations

**Crown failures**:
- **Marshall decision aftermath**: Despite the 1999 ruling, the Crown failed to implement moderate livelihood fishing rights for over two decades
- **2020 lobster fisheries crisis**: Sipekne'katik First Nation launched a self-regulated commercial fishery; faced violence and vandalism from non-Indigenous fishers; federal government threatened to prosecute buyers rather than recognize treaty rights
- **Truck house clause**: The 1752 and 1760 treaties promised truck houses (trading posts) for the exclusive use of Indigenous signatories; these were short-lived and abandoned
- **Annual presents**: The 1752 treaty promised annual presents of blankets, tobacco, powder, and shot; this obligation was not consistently fulfilled
- **Ongoing litigation**: Crown continues to resist recognizing the full scope of treaty rights

## B. Upper Canada Land Surrenders (1764-1862)

### 1. Overview

- **Parties**: British Crown (via Indian Department) and Anishinaabe peoples (Mississauga, Chippewa, Odawa), Huron-Wyandot, Six Nations, and others
- **Territory**: Southern and central Ontario (the "Upper Canadian peninsula")
- **Purpose**: Acquire land for settlement, particularly for Loyalists after the American Revolution
- **Current Status**: Most lands considered surrendered, though some claims remain unresolved

### 2. Treaty Chronology (Selected)

| Treaty | Date | Parties | Territory |
|--------|------|---------|-----------|
| Seneca Treaty (Niagara) | 1764 | Seneca, Crown | 4-mile strip along Niagara River |
| Crawford Purchases | 1783 | Mississauga, Crown | North shore of Lake Ontario |
| Between the Lakes Purchase | 1784 | Mississauga, Crown | Niagara Peninsula, north shore of Lake Erie |
| Toronto Purchase | 1787 | Mississauga, Crown | Toronto area (revised 1805, settled 2010) |
| McKee Treaty | 1790 | Chippewa, Odawa, Crown | Southwestern Ontario |
| Rice Lake Purchase | 1818 | Mississauga, Crown | Rice Lake area |
| Saugeen Tract Purchase | 1836 | Chippewa, Crown | Bruce Peninsula |
| Manitoulin Island Treaty | 1836 | Various, Crown | Manitoulin Island (as reserve) |
| Saugeen Peninsula Treaty | 1854 | Chippewa, Crown | Bruce Peninsula (full surrender) |
| Manitoulin Island Treaty | 1862 | Ottawa, Chippewa, Crown | Manitoulin Island (opening to settlement) |

### 3. Key Features

**Land surrender process:**
- Simple agreements describing the land, parties, and compensation
- Before 1818: one-time payments in goods or money
- After 1818: some treaties included annuities and reserve creation
- No standard format; terms varied significantly

**Compensation patterns:**
- Early treaties: clothing, guns, powder, ball, hats
- Later treaties: cash payments, annuities, reserves
- Some reserves were poorly surveyed and smaller than anticipated

### 4. Crown Failures

- **Toronto Purchase**: Originally negotiated in 1787, the boundaries were disputed for over 200 years. The Mississauga argued the treaty covered unceded territory. A revised treaty was signed in 1805, but a final settlement wasn't reached until **2010** ($145 million settlement).
- **Reserve reduction**: Many reserves were improperly surveyed, resulting in smaller areas than promised
- **Coercive surrender**: Some bands were coerced into surrendering reserve lands under threat of removal
- **Wiikwemkoong**: The 1836 Manitoulin Island Treaty established the island as a reserve for all Indigenous peoples; the 1862 treaty opened most of it to settlement without consent of the eastern peninsula bands
- **Williams Treaties (1923)**: The Crown used these to "clean up" unresolved claims from the Upper Canada period, requiring surrender of all rights including hunting and fishing—departing from the Robinson Treaty model

## C. Robinson Treaties (1850)

### 1. Robinson-Superior Treaty

- **Date**: September 7, 1850
- **Location**: Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario
- **Parties**: Ojibwa Chiefs (Lake Superior) and William Benjamin Robinson (for the Crown)
- **Territory**: North shore of Lake Superior from Batchewana Bay to Pigeon River, inland to height of land
- **Area**: Approximately 16,700 square miles
- **Population**: 1,422 Ojibwa people
- **Compensation**: £2,000 initial payment + £500 annual annuity
- **Reserves**: Multiple reserves specified in schedule

### 2. Robinson-Huron Treaty

- **Date**: September 9, 1850
- **Location**: Sault Ste. Marie, Ontario
- **Parties**: Ojibwa Chiefs (Lake Huron) and William Benjamin Robinson (for the Crown)
- **Territory**: North shore of Lake Huron from Penetanguishene to Batchewana Bay, inland to height of land
- **Area**: Approximately 35,700 square miles
- **Population**: 1,240 Ojibwa people
- **Compensation**: £2,000 initial payment + £600 annual annuity
- **Reserves**: 21 reserves specified

### 3. Key Provisions

**What the treaties promised:**
- **Hunting and fishing rights**: "the full and free privilege to hunt over the territory now ceded by them and to fish in the waters thereof as they have heretofore been in the habit of doing" (except in areas sold/leased to individuals)
- **Reserve lands**: Specific parcels for each community, held in common for residence and cultivation
- **Annual annuity**: £500 (Superior) and £600 (Huron), with an **escalator clause** promising increases as resource revenues grew
- **Resource revenue sharing**: If ceded territories produced enough revenue to increase the annuity without loss to the government, the annuity would be augmented
- **Mineral rights on reserves**: Bands could dispose of mineral or valuable productions on reserves through the Superintendent General

**What the treaties did NOT include:**
- No extinguishment of Aboriginal title beyond the specific cession
- No prohibition on hunting and fishing (except on private property)
- No fixed cap on annuity increases (the escalator clause was open-ended)

### 4. Current Status and Crown Failures

**Legal status**: The Robinson Treaties remain in full force and effect.

**Crown failures**:
- **Annuity never increased beyond $4**: The annuity was raised once—from $1.60 to $4.00 per person in 1874. Despite **billions of dollars** in resource revenues from mining, forestry, and hydroelectric development, the annuity has not changed since.
- **2023 settlement**: 21 First Nations signed a proposed settlement with Canada and Ontario worth **$10 billion** to compensate for underpayment of annuities under the Robinson-Huron Treaty
- **2024 Supreme Court decision**: In July 2024, the SCC ruled that the $4 annuity was a "mockery" of the Crown's treaty promise. The Court did not specify what the annuity should be, stating the Crown must negotiate with the plaintiffs.
- **Reserve reduction**: Many reserves on Lake Huron were improperly surveyed, making them smaller than anticipated
- **Coercive surrender**: Within a decade of the treaty, many bands were coerced into parting with reserve lands
- **Métis exclusion**: Robinson refused to deal with Métis people, telling chiefs he was empowered to treat with Indians only. This effectively prevented the development of Métis communities in Ontario.
- **Broken escalator clause**: The treaty's escalation provision has been violated for 150 years

## D. Douglas Treaties (1850-1854)

### 1. Overview

- **Date**: 1850-1854
- **Parties**: James Douglas (Hudson's Bay Company Chief Factor, later Governor) and various First Nations on Vancouver Island
- **Territory**: Southeastern Vancouver Island—Victoria, Saanich, Sooke, Fort Rupert (Port Hardy), and Nanaimo
- **Area**: Approximately 358 square miles (about 927 km²)
- **Number of treaties**: 14 "deeds of conveyance"
- **Current Status**: **Still in effect** — confirmed as treaties by BC Court of Appeal and SCC in *R. v. White and Bob* (1964)

### 2. Treaty Signatories

The following First Nations are descended from Douglas Treaty signatories:
- Esquimalt, Songhees, Sc'ianew (Becher Bay), T'Sou-ke, Tsawout, Tsartlip, Pauqhachin, Tseycum, Snuneymuxw, Kwakiutl, Malahat, Nanoose, Namgis, K'ómoks, Gwa'sala-'Nakwaxda'xw

### 3. Key Provisions

**What the treaties promised:**
- Surrender of land "entirely and forever"
- Reserved village sites and enclosed fields
- Right to hunt over unoccupied lands
- Right to carry on fisheries "as formerly"
- Compensation in goods (primarily blankets)

**The standard clause** in each treaty:
> "The conditions of our understanding of this sale is this, that our village sites and enclosed fields are to be kept for our own use, for the use of our children, and for those who may follow after us; and the land shall be properly surveyed hereafter. It is understood, however, that the land itself, with these small exceptions, becomes the entire property of the white people for ever; it is also understood that we are at liberty to hunt over the unoccupied lands, and to carry on our fisheries as formerly."

### 4. Critical Issues

**Oral vs. Written Agreements**:
- The written documents do not accurately reflect what was agreed orally
- Douglas entered names on blank paper **nine months** before receiving the standard form from London
- No First Nation saw the written version until decades later
- None could read English
- Recent scholarship suggests the agreements were **sharing treaties**, not cession treaties
- The SCC in *R. v. Morris and Olsen* (2006) acknowledged the treaty "was concluded orally" and "subsequently reduced to writing"

**Compensation**:
- The Songhees sold their title to the District of Victoria for **371 blankets and a cap**
- Douglas favored annuity payments but chiefs preferred lump sums
- The total area covered was approximately 1/40th of Vancouver Island

### 5. Crown Failures

- **Termination of Douglas treaties**: After Douglas's retirement in 1864, Governor Joseph Trutch claimed the treaty payments were "for the purpose of securing friendly relations" and "not in acknowledgment of any general title of the Indians to the land"
- **No further treaties**: BC refused to negotiate treaties with the vast majority of the province's First Nations
- **Reserve reduction**: Trutch reduced many reserves established under Douglas
- **Ongoing litigation**: The Douglas Treaties have been relied upon in court proceedings regarding hunting and fishing rights, and provide the basis of ongoing legal action regarding a village site in Victoria
- **Saanich Treaty (Tsartlip)**: Chief Ivan Morris blocked a public road in 2010 because "the federal government has failed to live up to the terms of the Douglas treaty signed in 1852"

## E. Manitoulin Island Treaties

### 1. Manitoulin Island Treaty of 1836

- **Date**: August 9, 1836
- **Parties**: Sir Francis Bond Head (Lieutenant-Governor of Upper Canada) and Chiefs of Ottawas and Chippewas
- **Territory**: Great Manitoulin Island
- **Purpose**: Establish Manitoulin Island as a reserve for dispossessed First Nations
- **Key provision**: The island was to be "made the property (under their Great Father's control) of all Indians whom he should allow to reside thereon"
- **Outcome**: Many Indigenous peoples did not relocate to the island as intended

### 2. Manitoulin Island Treaty of 1862 (McDougall Treaty / Treaty 94)

- **Date**: October 6, 1862
- **Parties**: William McDougall (Superintendent General of Indian Affairs) and Chiefs of Ottawa, Chippewa, and other Indians
- **Territory**: Great Manitoulin Island and adjacent islands
- **Purpose**: Open the island for European Canadian settlement
- **Key provisions**:
  - Reserves of 100 acres per family, 50 acres per single person over 21
  - Cash payment of $700 (advance against future income)
  - Annual interest from land sales distributed per capita
  - Equal fishing rights to those of whites
  - **Wiikwemkoong exception**: The eastern peninsula was excluded from the surrender

### 3. Wiikwemkoong Unceded Territory

- **Status**: **Unceded** — the only unceded territory in southern Ontario
- **Background**: Wiikwemkoong bands refused to participate in the 1862 treaty process
- **Outcome**: The eastern peninsula remained under the terms of the 1836 treaty
- **Current claims**: Wiikwemkoong submitted a specific land claim in 1997 for unsurrendered islands off the eastern shore; a settlement was reached in 2022
- **Population**: Home to the largest Anishinaabek community on Manitoulin Island

### 4. Crown Failures

- **Exclusion from negotiations**: Wiikwemkoong leadership was purposefully excluded from the 1862 treaty process
- **Alcohol use**: Commissioners made "liberal use of alcohol" to encourage acceptance of government demands
- **Broken promises**: The 1836 treaty promised the island as a collective Indigenous reserve; the 1862 treaty opened most of it to settlement
- **Boundary disputes**: The boundary between Wiikwemkoong's unceded lands and the rest of the island was never properly surveyed
- **Adjacent islands**: Islands surrounding Wiikwemkoong were not surrendered in 1862, creating ongoing jurisdictional disputes
- **1863 fisheries murder**: The murder of Fisheries Commissioner Gibbard in 1863 reflected deep Indigenous opposition to the loss of fishing rights

# PART III: SYNTHESIS — THE CROWN'S NEGLECT OF ITS FOUNDATIONAL OBLIGATIONS

## 1. The Proclamation's Promise vs. Reality

The Royal Proclamation of 1763 established a framework that, if honored, would have created a fundamentally different relationship between the Crown and Indigenous peoples:

| Proclamation Promise | Crown's Actual Conduct |
|---------------------|----------------------|
| Indigenous lands "not to be molested or disturbed" | Systematic dispossession through land surrenders |
| Lands reserved as "Hunting Grounds" | Opening reserved lands to settlement (e.g., Manitoulin 1862) |
| Only Crown could purchase Indigenous lands | Crown agents coerced surrenders; inadequate compensation |
| Public meetings for land transactions | Closed-door negotiations; alcohol used to secure agreements |
| Protection of Indigenous interests | Reserve reduction; reserve surrender under threat |

## 2. Pattern of Treaty Violations

Across all pre-Confederation treaties, a consistent pattern emerges:

1. **Initial promise**: Crown makes commitments regarding land, resources, and rights
2. **Gradual erosion**: Crown interprets treaty terms narrowly; fails to fulfill obligations
3. **Coercive renegotiation**: Crown pressures Indigenous peoples into accepting reduced terms
4. **Legislative override**: Crown passes laws that effectively nullify treaty rights
5. **Judicial recognition (belated)**: Courts eventually recognize the original treaty terms—but often decades or centuries later

## 3. The Escalator Clause Violation (Robinson Treaties)

The Robinson Treaties' escalator clause represents perhaps the most egregious Crown failure:
- The treaties promised annuity increases as resource revenues grew
- Billions of dollars in mining, forestry, and hydroelectric revenue extracted from treaty territory
- Annuity increased **once** (1874) from $1.60 to $4.00 per person
- No further increase in **150 years**
- The SCC in 2024 called the $4 annuity a "mockery" of the Crown's promise

## 4. The Oral vs. Written Treaty Problem

Multiple pre-Confederation treaties suffer from discrepancies between what was agreed orally and what was written:
- **Douglas Treaties**: Written documents prepared months after oral agreements; First Nations unable to read English
- **Peace and Friendship Treaties**: Oral promises regarding trade and hunting rights not fully captured in written texts
- **Upper Canada Land Surrenders**: Boundaries often unclear or disputed
- **Robinson Treaties**: Indigenous leaders later disputed the scope of what they agreed to surrender

## 5. Constitutional Protection and Ongoing Litigation

The pre-Confederation treaties are constitutionally protected under:
- **Section 35** of the *Constitution Act, 1982*: "The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed"
- **Section 25** of the *Charter of Rights and Freedoms*: Protects rights recognized by the Royal Proclamation

Yet litigation continues because:
- The Crown consistently resists recognizing the full scope of treaty rights
- Implementation of treaty terms remains inadequate
- "Moderate livelihood" standards remain undefined (*Marshall*)
- Annuity escalation clauses remain unfulfilled (*Robinson*)
- Reserve lands remain underfunded and underserviced

# PART IV: KEY CASE LAW SUMMARY

## *R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 SCR 456 and [1999] 3 SCR 533

- **Issue**: Mi'kmaq treaty right to fish and sell fish
- **Decision**: The 1760-61 Peace and Friendship Treaties created a treaty right to obtain necessaries through hunting, fishing, and trading for a "moderate livelihood"
- **Significance**: First major SCC recognition of Peace and Friendship Treaty rights; constitutionally protected under s. 35
- **Limitation**: Rights can be regulated for conservation or other compelling public objectives

## *Haida Nation v. British Columbia* [2004] 3 SCR 511

- **Issue**: Duty to consult regarding tree farm licence on Haida Gwaii
- **Decision**: The Crown has a legal duty to consult and accommodate Aboriginal peoples when it has knowledge of potential Aboriginal rights and contemplates conduct that might adversely affect them
- **Significance**: Established the modern duty to consult framework; grounded in the "honour of the Crown"
- **Key principle**: The duty is proportionate to the strength of the claim and seriousness of the potential adverse effect

## *Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia* [2014] 2 SCR 257

- **Issue**: Aboriginal title claim over 2,000 km² in BC
- **Decision**: First declaration of Aboriginal title by the SCC; confirmed the test for Aboriginal title (sufficient, continuous, exclusive occupation at time of sovereignty)
- **Significance**: Where title is established, the Crown must either obtain consent or justify incursions under s. 35; displaced the doctrine of interjurisdictional immunity
- **Breach found**: BC breached its duty to consult by issuing forestry licences without consultation

## *R. v. White and Bob* (1964, BC Court of Appeal)

- **Issue**: Whether the 1854 Nanaimo Douglas Treaty was a valid treaty
- **Decision**: Confirmed the Nanaimo agreement was a treaty "as much an act of state as if it had been entered into by the Sovereign herself"
- **Significance**: All Douglas Treaties confirmed as valid treaties under the Indian Act
