# 10 — Indigenous Land Rights, Aboriginal Title, and the Crown's Fiduciary Duty

> **The legal architecture of Indigenous land rights in Canada is not a matter of political opinion — it is constitutional law, established by the Supreme Court of Canada through decades of decisions. The Crown's obligations are not optional. They are binding, enforceable, and increasingly backed by the full weight of the Constitution.**

---

## 1. Aboriginal Title

### 1.1 What Is Aboriginal Title?

Aboriginal title is a **sui generis** (one-of-a-kind) interest in land that arises from the **prior occupation of Canada by Indigenous peoples** before the assertion of European sovereignty. It is not a gift from the Crown. It is not a privilege granted by statute. It is a pre-existing right that predates Confederation itself.

The Supreme Court of Canada defined Aboriginal title in *Delgamuukw v. British Columbia* [1997] 3 SCR 1010:

> Aboriginal title "encompasses the right to exclusive use and occupation of the land held pursuant to that title for a variety of purposes" (at para. 117), including non-traditional purposes, provided these uses can be reconciled with the communal and ongoing nature of the group's attachment to the land.

Aboriginal title confers **ownership rights similar to those associated with fee simple** (*Tsilhqot'in Nation v. British Columbia*, 2014 SCC 44, at para. 73):

- The right to **decide how the land will be used**
- The right of **enjoyment and occupancy** of the land
- The right to **possess** the land
- The right to the **economic benefits** of the land
- The right to **pro-actively use and manage** the land

### 1.2 How Is Aboriginal Title Proven?

The test for Aboriginal title was established in *Delgamuukw* (1997) and clarified in *Tsilhqot'in* (2014). The claimant group bears the onus. Three elements must be established:

1. **Sufficient occupation** of the land at the time of assertion of European sovereignty
2. **Continuity** of occupation (where present occupation is relied on)
3. **Exclusive** historic occupation

**Sufficiency** is assessed using a **culturally sensitive approach** — looking at the Aboriginal culture and practices, and comparing them with what was required at common law to establish title:

> Occupation sufficient to ground Aboriginal title is not confined to specific sites of settlement but extends to tracts of land that were regularly used for hunting, fishing or otherwise exploiting resources and over which the group exercised effective control at the time of assertion of European sovereignty. (*Tsilhqot'in*, at para. 50)

This means **semi-nomadic and nomadic peoples can establish title** over large territories — not just small villages or cultivated fields. The trial judge in *Tsilhqot'in* found title to 1,750 square kilometres based on seasonal patterns of use by a semi-nomadic people.

**Continuity** does not require proof of an unbroken chain of occupation. The claimant must establish that present occupation is rooted in pre-sovereignty times.

**Exclusivity** requires showing the intention and capacity to retain exclusive control. Other peoples being on the same land does not negate exclusivity — if others were excluded or were on the land by permission, exclusivity can still be established.

### 1.3 The *Tsilhqot'in* Decision (2014) — First Declaration of Aboriginal Title

On June 26, 2014, the Supreme Court of Canada made history by granting the **first-ever declaration of Aboriginal title** in Canadian law:

> "We are Tŝilhqot'in… we have never surrendered our Title or Rights… we have only strengthened them!" — Tsilhqot'in Nation

The Court held:

- The trial judge was correct in finding the Tsilhqot'in had established title
- British Columbia **breached its duty to consult** by issuing forestry licences without consultation
- The **Forest Act did not apply** to Aboriginal title lands (since title lands are not "Crown lands")
- The Crown's infringement was **not justified** under s. 35

### 1.4 What Rights Does Aboriginal Title Confer?

Once Aboriginal title is established:

- The title-holding group has the **exclusive right to decide how the land is used** and the right to benefit from those uses
- Governments and others seeking to use the land **must obtain the consent** of the title holders
- If the Aboriginal group does not consent, the government's only recourse is to establish the proposed incursion is **justified under s. 35** of the *Constitution Act, 1982*
- The Crown must demonstrate: (1) a compelling and substantial governmental objective, (2) that the government action is consistent with the Crown's fiduciary duty, and (3) minimal impairment and proportionality (*Tsilhqot'in*, at paras. 77-88)

### 1.5 Can Aboriginal Title Be Extinguished?

**Before 1982**: Aboriginal rights could be extinguished by:
1. "Surrender to the Crown"
2. "A clear and plain intention" by the Crown to extinguish through legislation or treaty

**After 1982** (section 35): The Crown **can no longer unilaterally extinguish** Aboriginal or treaty rights. The constitutional protection of s. 35 means there is now an obligation on the Crown to justify any infringements according to the *Sparrow* test.

As the Supreme Court stated:

> "Now that section 35 provides constitutional protection to Aboriginal and treaty rights, the Crown may no longer unilaterally extinguish them. But if a right was previously extinguished, neither section 35 nor any other provision of the Constitution Act, 1982 can revive it."

**Critical point for unextinguished title**: In *Delgamuukw*, the Supreme Court held that the **Province of British Columbia had no jurisdiction to extinguish Aboriginal rights** between entering Confederation (1871) and the entrenchment of s. 35 (1982). Section 91(24) of the *Constitution Act, 1867* gave the federal government exclusive jurisdiction. This means that in British Columbia, **most Aboriginal title has never been lawfully extinguished**.

## 2. Treaty Rights

### 2.1 Section 35 of the *Constitution Act, 1982*

Section 35(1) reads:

> "The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed."

Section 35(2):

> "In this Act, 'aboriginal peoples of Canada' includes the Indian, Inuit and Métis peoples of Canada."

Section 35(3):

> "For greater certainty, in subsection (1) 'treaty rights' includes rights that now exist by way of land claims agreements or may be so acquired."

**Section 35 did not create rights** — it affirmed rights that existed, some of which were recognized at law long before 1982 (*R. v. Sparrow*, [1990] 1 SCR 1075).

### 2.2 Treaty Interpretation — Liberal in Favour of Indigenous Peoples

The Supreme Court has established that treaties are to be interpreted according to special principles reflecting their unique nature:

1. A treaty represents an **exchange of solemn promises** between the Crown and the various Indian nations
2. The **honour of the Crown is always at stake** — the Crown must be assumed to intend to fulfil its promises
3. Any **ambiguities or doubtful expressions** must be resolved in favour of the Indians
4. Any **limitations restricting the rights** of Indians under treaties must be **narrowly construed**
5. The **onus of establishing strict proof of extinguishment** lies upon the Crown

These principles were articulated in *R. v. Badger* [1996] 1 SCR 771 and applied across subsequent cases.

### 2.3 Oral Promises vs. Written Text

Treaties are **sui generis** agreements that are not to be interpreted as ordinary contracts. The written text does not capture the full understanding of the parties. Oral promises made during treaty negotiations are legally significant and must be considered in interpretation.

In *R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 SCR 456, the Court interpreted the Peace and Friendship Treaties of 1760-61 by examining the historical context, the understanding of both parties, and the oral promises made — finding a treaty right to fish and trade for "necessaries" even though the written text was sparse.

### 2.4 The "Honour of the Crown" Doctrine

The honour of the Crown is a **constitutional principle** fundamental to Aboriginal law. It is not a written rule but a concept developed by the judiciary:

> "In all its dealings with Aboriginal peoples, from the assertion of sovereignty to the resolution of claims and the implementation of treaties, the Crown must act honourably." (*Haida Nation v. British Columbia*, 2004 SCC 73)

The honour of the Crown gives rise to different obligations in different circumstances:

- **Control Over Aboriginal Interests**: When the Crown assumes discretionary control over a specific Aboriginal interest, it must preserve and protect that interest
- **Constitutional, Treaty and Statutory Interpretation**: The Crown must interpret its constitutional obligations broadly and purposively
- **Duty to Consult**: When the Crown contemplates conduct that may adversely affect a s. 35 right, it has a duty to consult and, where appropriate, accommodate
- **Treaty-making and Implementation**: The Crown has a duty to negotiate honourably and avoid "sharp-dealing"

## 3. The Crown's Fiduciary Duty

### 3.1 *Guerin v. Canada* (1984) — Fiduciary Duty as Express Trust

The landmark *Guerin v. The Queen* [1984] 2 SCR 335 established that the Crown owes a **fiduciary duty** to Indigenous peoples that is enforceable in the courts:

**Facts**: The Musqueam Indian Band surrendered reserve lands to the Crown for lease to a golf club. The terms obtained by the Crown were much less favourable than those approved by the Band at the surrender meeting. The Crown withheld information from both the Band and an appraiser.

**Held**: The Crown breached its fiduciary obligation. The trial judge awarded $10 million in damages.

**Key principles from Dickson J.**:

> "The nature of Indian title and the framework of the statutory scheme established for disposing of Indian land place upon the Crown an equitable obligation, enforceable by the courts, to deal with the land for the benefit of the Indians."

> "Where by statute, by agreement or perhaps by unilateral undertaking, one party has an obligation to act for the benefit of another, and that obligation carries with it a discretionary power, the party thus empowered becomes a fiduciary. Equity will then supervise the relationship by holding him to the fiduciary's strict standard of conduct."

**Key principles from Wilson J.**:

> "The Crown owed a fiduciary duty — not a mere political obligation — to the Band arising from its control over the use to which reserve lands could be put."

> "This fiduciary duty, upon surrender, crystallized into an express trust of the land for the purpose specified."

The fiduciary relationship has **two sources**:
1. The **nature of Indian title** itself (inalienability except to the Crown)
2. The **statutory framework** (Indian Act, Royal Proclamation) that places the Crown between Indigenous peoples and third parties

### 3.2 *R. v. Sparrow* (1990) — Justification for Infringement

*R. v. Sparrow* [1990] 1 SCR 1075 was the Supreme Court's **first section 35 decision** and established the framework for justifying infringements of Aboriginal and treaty rights:

**Key principles**:

> "The Government has the responsibility to act in a fiduciary capacity with respect to aboriginal peoples. The relationship between the Government and aboriginals is trust-like, rather than adversarial."

> "The honour of the Crown is at stake in dealings with aboriginal peoples. The special trust relationship and the responsibility of the government vis-à-vis aboriginals must be the first consideration in determining whether the [infringing] legislation or action in question can be justified."

The **Sparrow test** for justifying infringement:
1. The law must have a **valid, compelling and substantial objective** (e.g., conservation, public safety)
2. The government must demonstrate it is acting in accordance with its **fiduciary obligation** — giving priority to Indigenous claims, taking rights seriously
3. The infringement must be **minimal impairment**
4. **Fair compensation** may be required
5. **Consultation** with the affected group

### 3.3 *R. v. Badger* (1996) — Liberal Interpretation

*R. v. Badger* [1996] 1 SCR 771 confirmed and reinforced the principles of treaty interpretation:

> "Treaties are of a sacred nature and represent an exchange of solemn promises between the Crown and various Indian nations."

The Court held:

> "Any ambiguities or doubtful expressions in the wording of the treaty or document must be resolved in favour of the Indians. A corollary to this principle is that any limitations which restrict the rights of Indians under treaties must be narrowly construed."

> "The onus of establishing strict proof of extinguishment of a treaty or aboriginal right lies upon the Crown."

The Court also confirmed that the Sparrow test applies to treaty rights and that the federal and provincial governments have authority to regulate treaty rights, but only where justified on conservation or other compelling grounds.

### 3.4 *R. v. Marshall* (1999) — Ongoing Rights

*R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 SCR 456 affirmed that treaty rights are **ongoing and alive**:

**Facts**: Donald Marshall Jr., a Mi'kmaq Indian, was charged with selling eels without a licence and fishing during a closed season. He claimed treaty rights under the Peace and Friendship Treaties of 1760-61.

**Held**: The Supreme Court reversed Marshall's convictions, recognizing a **treaty right to catch and sell fish** in pursuit of a moderate livelihood.

**Key principles**:

> "The surviving substance of the treaty is not the literal promise of a truckhouse, but a treaty right to continue to obtain necessaries through hunting and fishing by trading the products of those traditional activities subject to restrictions that can be justified under the *Badger* test."

The Court established:
- Treaty rights are **not frozen in time** — they can evolve
- "Moderate livelihood" means securing the **necessaries** of life
- Treaty rights are held by the **community as a whole**
- Regulations can be imposed if justified on conservation or other compelling grounds
- **35 Mi'kmaq, Maliseet and Passamaquoddy First Nations** in Nova Scotia, New Brunswick, Prince Edward Island, and the Gaspé region of Quebec are potentially affected

### 3.5 *Restoule v. Canada* (2024) — Duty to Increase Annuities

*Ontario (Attorney General) v. Restoule*, 2024 SCC 27, is the most recent landmark decision on treaty obligations:

**Facts**: In 1850, the Anishinaabe of Lake Huron and Lake Superior entered into the Robinson Treaties, ceding their territories. The treaties contained an "Augmentation Clause" promising to increase annuities if the ceded lands produced enough revenue. The annuities were increased to $4 per person in 1875 but have **not been increased since** — nearly 150 years.

**Held**: The Supreme Court unanimously ruled:

1. The Crown has a **duty to consider, from time to time**, whether it can increase the annuities without incurring loss
2. If the Crown can increase the annuities, it **must exercise its discretion** — this discretion is not unfettered
3. The discretion must be exercised **diligently, honourably, liberally, and justly**
4. The Crown's discretion is **subject to review by the courts**
5. The Crown **dishonourably breached** the Robinson Treaties by failing to diligently fulfill the Augmentation Clause
6. The Crown must determine an amount of **honourable compensation** for amounts owed from 1875 to the present

Justice Jamal's words will echo through decades of Crown-Indigenous relations:

> "For well over a century, the Crown has shown itself to be a patently unreliable and untrustworthy treaty partner in relation to the augmentation promise. It has lost the moral authority to simply say 'trust us.'" (at para. 262)

The Court established a **duty of diligent implementation** of treaty promises — flowing directly from the honour of the Crown — that has both procedural and substantive elements. A purely procedural duty (simply "turning its mind" to increases) is insufficient.

## 4. Unextinguished Aboriginal Title

### 4.1 How Much Land Has Aboriginal Title NOT Been Extinguished?

The short answer: **most of Canada**.

**British Columbia**: The vast majority of the province. BC has **very few treaties** — only a handful of Douglas Treaties on Vancouver Island (1850s) and the Nisga'a Treaty (2000). The rest of the province — most of BC's land mass — has never been ceded by treaty. The *Delgamuukw* case (1997) involved a claim to 58,000 square kilometres in northwest BC alone. The *Tsilhqot'in* case (2014) declared title to 1,750 square kilometres.

As the Supreme Court noted in *Delgamuukw*:

> Aboriginal title in British Columbia is a legal interest in the land and is a burden on Crown title. The decision was expected to have significant repercussions on the future negotiation and settlement of comprehensive land claims based on Aboriginal title in those regions of the country where traditional Aboriginal lands have not been ceded by treaty. These include not only **most of British Columbia**, but also, for example, **parts of Quebec and Atlantic Canada**.

**The Territories** (Yukon, NWT, Nunavut): Most of the land has not been ceded. Modern land claims agreements have been completed in some areas (e.g., Nunavut, Nunavik, NWT), but significant areas remain unresolved.

**The Prairies and Ontario**: The numbered treaties (Treaties 1-11, 1871-1921) cover much of these regions, but the scope and interpretation of these treaties remains contested. As *Restoule* (2024) demonstrated, treaty promises made 150+ years ago remain legally enforceable and may require substantial compensation for past breaches.

**Quebec and Atlantic Canada**: Most of this territory was never ceded by treaty. The Supreme Court in *Delgamuukw* specifically identified these regions as areas where unextinguished Aboriginal title may exist.

### 4.2 What Rights Remain in Treaty Areas?

Even where treaties have been signed, significant rights remain:

- **Treaty rights are constitutionally protected** under s. 35 and cannot be extinguished
- **Oral promises** made during treaty negotiations are legally enforceable
- **The honour of the Crown** infuses every treaty obligation
- **Annuities and other benefits** promised in treaties remain enforceable (*Restoule*, 2024)
- **The duty of diligent implementation** requires the Crown to actively fulfil treaty promises
- **Rights evolve** — they are not frozen in time (*Marshall*, 1999)

## 5. The Crown's Obligations

### 5.1 Protect Reserve Lands

The Crown holds reserve lands **for the use and benefit** of the bands for which they were set apart (s. 18(1), *Indian Act*). This creates a fiduciary obligation:

- Reserve lands are **inalienable** except by surrender to the Crown
- The Crown must protect reserve lands from **exploitation** and third-party encroachment
- The Crown must ensure that any use of reserve lands is **for the benefit** of the band
- The Crown cannot use reserve lands for purposes **incompatible with Indian title** without consent

### 5.2 Provide Education and Healthcare

Treaty promises regarding education and healthcare are legally binding:

- **Treaty 6** contains a **medicine chest clause** — an obligation on the Crown to supply medical supplies to Treaty Indians free of charge (*Dreaver et al v. the King*, 1935)
- The **treaty right to health** has been recognized as flowing from the sacred promises made during treaty negotiations
- Modern treaties and self-government agreements increasingly include health and wellness provisions
- The Federal Government has accepted responsibility for First Nations people on reserves through policy, but has yet to fully recognize the Treaty obligation

### 5.3 Pay Annuities

The *Restoule* (2024) decision made clear:

- The Crown has a **duty to consider increasing annuities** when economic circumstances permit
- The Crown's discretion is **not unfettered** — it must be exercised liberally, justly, and in accordance with the honour of the Crown
- The Crown must pay **retroactive compensation** for past breaches
- Treaty claims are **not statute-barred** by provincial limitations legislation
- The duty of diligent implementation has both **procedural and substantive** elements

### 5.4 Consult on Any Land Use

The duty to consult arises when three elements are present:

1. **Crown conduct** is contemplated
2. **Section 35 rights** (Aboriginal or treaty) exist or are credibly asserted
3. The conduct may have an **adverse impact** on those rights

The duty exists on a **sliding scale** (*Haida Nation v. British Columbia*, 2004):

- At the lower end: giving notice, disclosing information, discussing issues
- At the higher end: providing written reasons, working with groups to accommodate concerns
- At the highest end: **consent** may be required where title has been established

The Crown must have **actual or constructive knowledge** of the right. The right need not be proven — a credible, unproven claim suffices.

### 5.5 Accommodate Indigenous Interests

Accommodation measures may include:

- Changes to proposed project design
- Follow-up environmental monitoring
- Co-management arrangements
- Funding for traditional land use
- Restoration of wildlife or habitat
- Participation in economic benefits
- Continuing consultations

The Crown must be **responsive to concerns expressed** during consultation. A purely ceremonial process will not satisfy the constitutional duty.

## 6. Summary of Crown Negligence

The pattern of Crown negligence across these obligations is clear:

| Obligation | Legal Basis | Crown Failure |
|---|---|---|
| Protect reserve lands | *Guerin* (1984), s. 18 Indian Act | Lands surrendered on unfavourable terms; information withheld |
| Provide healthcare | Treaty 6 medicine chest clause, *Dreaver* (1935) | Chronic underfunding; services not equivalent to off-reserve |
| Pay annuities | Robinson Treaties, *Restoule* (2024) | No increase in 150 years despite massive resource revenues |
| Consult on land use | *Haida* (2004), *Tsilhqot'in* (2014) | Forestry licences issued without consultation (*Tsilhqot'in*) |
| Accommodate interests | *Sparrow* (1990), *Badger* (1996) | Inadequate accommodation measures |
| Implement treaties | *Marshall* (1999), *Restoule* (2024) | "Patently unreliable and untrustworthy treaty partner" |

The Supreme Court's own assessment in *Restoule* (2024) is damning:

> "For well over a century, the Crown has shown itself to be a patently unreliable and untrustworthy treaty partner in relation to the augmentation promise. It has lost the moral authority to simply say 'trust us.'"

This is not political rhetoric. It is the judgment of the highest court in the land.
