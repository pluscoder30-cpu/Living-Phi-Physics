# 07 — Treaty Failure and Land Reversion: The Legal Doctrines

## The Central Thesis

When the Crown fails to uphold its treaty obligations, a constellation of legal doctrines—frustration, condition subsequent, fiduciary breach, honour of the Crown, and contractual termination—converge on a single conclusion: **the land reverts**. This is not radical legal theory. It is the logical terminus of principles the Supreme Court of Canada has already articulated.

---

## 1. THE DOCTRINE OF FRUSTRATION

### 1.1 The Doctrine in General Contract Law

Frustration operates where an event occurs—without default of either party and for which the contract makes no sufficient provision—that either renders performance impossible or fundamentally different from what was contemplated. *Davis Contractors Ltd v Fareham Urban District Council* [1956] AC 696.

The doctrine is a **risk allocation procedure**: if it is not reasonably possible to place the risk of non-performance on either party, the contract is frustrated. *Edwinton Commercial Corp v Tsavliris Russ* [2007] EWCA Civ 547.

**Key elements:**
- The frustrating event must be outside the control of both parties
- The event must render performance radically different from what was contemplated
- The contract must not have allocated the risk to one party
- Frustration **automatically terminates** the contract for the future

### 1.2 Application to Crown-Indigenous Treaties

The frustration doctrine has **never been successfully applied** to Crown-Indigenous treaties in Canadian law. This is deliberate.

In *Mikisew Cree First Nation v Canada (Minister of Canadian Heritage)* [2005] SCC 69, the Supreme Court observed that the territory controlled by Indigenous Nations in 1900 "was given up and has never been returned." The Court **presumed** that Indigenous Nations have fulfilled their side of the bargain, based on the power imbalance that existed and continues to exist between the treaty partners.

The Crown cannot invoke frustration because:

1. **The Crown's own breach cannot be the frustrating event.** Frustration requires an event "without default of either party." Where the Crown's own conduct caused the failure, frustration does not apply. *Taylor v Caldwell* (1863) 3 B&S 826 (frustration requires absence of fault).

2. **The treaties allocated risk to the Crown.** The treaties were promises of perpetual obligation. The risk of the Crown's failure to perform was inherent in the Crown's assumption of discretionary control. Frustration only applies where the risk was not contractually allocated.

3. **The Crown's breach is not a supervening event.** Frustration requires an event subsequent to contract formation. The Crown's pattern of neglect and breach is not a single supervening event but a continuing course of conduct—precisely the kind of breach that gives rise to contractual remedies rather than discharge.

### 1.3 What Happens When the Treaty Is Frustrated (Hypothetically)

If frustration were to apply—and it does not—the consequences would be:

- Both parties **discharged from future obligations** under the treaty
- Obligations accruing **before** the frustrating event remain binding
- Under the *Law Reform (Frustrated Contracts) Act 1943* (English law), the court may apportion losses already incurred

**But in the Crown-Indigenous context:** If the Crown's breach were treated as frustration (which it is not), the logical consequence is that **the Indigenous party is released from its cession obligations**, because the treaty's consideration was the Crown's promises. When the Crown's promises fail, the bargain collapses—and the land, which was the subject of the cession, reverts.

This is the reductio ad absurdum that the Crown cannot afford: if the treaty is frustrated, the land returns.

## 2. THE DOCTRINE OF CONDITION SUBSEQUENT

### 2.1 The Doctrine

A condition subsequent is a condition in a grant that, if it occurs, terminates the grantee's estate and revests title in the grantor.

**Classic formulation:** A grant "to have and to hold so long as" a particular condition is maintained. If the condition fails, the estate automatically terminates and the land reverts to the grantor.

**Key principles from U.S. Supreme Court jurisprudence (persuasive in Canadian law):**

In *New York Indians v United States*, 170 U.S. 1 (1898):

> "The provisions of the third article created a **condition subsequent**, upon a breach of which the government might declare a forfeiture... A distinction is drawn by the authorities between the case of a private grantor, who may re-enter in the case of the breach of a condition subsequent, and the government, which can only repossess itself of lands by **legislative or judicial action**."

> "A condition, when relied upon to work a forfeiture, is construed with **great strictness**. The grantor must stand on his legal rights, and any ambiguity in his deed, or defect in the evidence offered to show a breach, will be taken **most strongly against him, and in favor of the grantee**."

### 2.2 Did the Treaties Contain Conditions Subsequent?

The treaties contained explicit conditional language:

**Robinson Treaties (1850):** The Augmentation Clause was a conditional promise: the Crown would increase annuities *if* economic conditions permitted. The Crown's obligation to exercise its discretion was the condition that, if performed, would maintain the treaty relationship. Its non-performance is a breach of the condition.

**Treaty 45½ (Saugeen, 1836):** The Crown promised to protect the Saugeen Peninsula from encroachment by settlers. The condition was ongoing protection. When the Crown failed to protect, the condition was breached.

**Treaty 7 (Blackfoot, 1877):** The Treaty Land Entitlement provisions established a formula (one square mile per family of five). The Crown's failure to calculate the reserve size correctly was a breach of the conditional promise.

### 2.3 What Happens When Conditions Subsequent Are Breached?

From *New York Indians*:

> "Before a forfeiture or reunion with the public domain could take place, a judicial inquiry should be instituted, or, in the technical language of the common law, office found, or its legal equivalent. A legislative act, directing the possession and appropriation of the land, is equivalent to office found."

**For Crown-Indigenous treaties, the consequences of breach of condition subsequent are:**

1. **The Crown may declare a forfeiture**—but only through legislative or judicial action, not executive action alone
2. **The Crown cannot unilaterally re-enter**—it must follow due process
3. **The condition is construed strictly against the Crown**—ambiguity favours the Indigenous party
4. **Waiver may apply**—if the Crown accepts benefits after the breach, it may be taken to have waived the condition

**Critical point:** If the treaties contained conditions subsequent, and the Crown breached those conditions, the **Indigenous party** may argue for reversion of the land—not the Crown. The Crown cannot breach a condition and then claim the benefit of the breach.

### 2.4 The Reverse Application

The doctrine of condition subsequent can work **in favour of the Indigenous party**:

- The Crown's promise to protect land from encroachment was a condition subsequent to the Indigenous party's surrender
- The Crown's failure to protect was a breach of that condition
- The breach gives rise to a right of re-entry—not in the Crown, but in the Indigenous party
- The land reverts to the Indigenous party upon exercise of that right

This is precisely the argument made by the Chippewas of Nawash in *Chippewas of Nawash Unceded First Nation v Canada (Attorney General)*, 2023 ONCA 565, where they argued that the Crown's failure to fulfill Treaty 45½ compelled them to enter into Treaty 72, resulting in the loss of most of their land on the Bruce Peninsula.

## 3. BREACH OF FIDUCIARY DUTY

### 3.1 The Crown's Fiduciary Duty

The Supreme Court of Canada has recognized that the Crown owes a **sui generis fiduciary duty** to Indigenous peoples. This duty arises from:

1. The Crown's discretionary control over Indigenous interests in land
2. The Crown's assumption of control over the use of reserve lands
3. The power imbalance between the Crown and Indigenous peoples

**Key case law:**

*Guerin v The Queen* [1984] 2 SCR 335: The SCC described the fiduciary duty as a *sui generis* relationship arising from the Crown's discretionary power. The Crown must administer reserve lands for the benefit of Indigenous peoples.

*Williams Lake Indian Band v Canada* [2018] SCC 4: The fiduciary duty imposes obligations of **loyalty, good faith, full disclosure, and protection and preservation** of the First Nation's quasi-proprietary interest from exploitation.

*Southwind v Canada* [2024] SCC: A strong fiduciary duty arises where the Crown exercises control over a First Nation's land. The duty requires the Crown to capture the **full potential value** of the land, not merely expropriation-value compensation.

*Wewaykum Indian Band v Canada* [2002] SCC 79: The fiduciary duty expands once a reserve is created to include protection and preservation of the band's quasi-proprietary interest from exploitation. The Crown must use diligence to protect a band's legal interest from exploitative bargaining with third parties or from exploitation by the Crown itself.

### 3.2 What Happens When Fiduciary Duty Is Breached?

The consequences of fiduciary breach are severe:

1. **Equitable compensation** — not limited to expropriation principles, but reflecting the full potential value of the land (*Southwind*)
2. **Constructive trust** — the Crown may be declared to hold land in trust for the Indigenous party
3. **Accounting of profits** — the Crown must account for all benefits derived from the breach
4. **Reversion** — where the fiduciary duty was to protect the land, breach may give rise to a right of reversion

**From *Guerin*:**

> The Crown's fiduciary duty, upon surrender, **crystallized into an express trust** of the land for the specified purpose. When the Crown breached the trust by leasing the land on unfavourable terms, the breach was enforceable in equity.

**From *Southwind*:**

> "Canada's fiduciary obligations required it to ensure the highest compensation possible, including compensation for the land's anticipated use as land for hydroelectricity generation. If negotiations for a surrender of the land were unsuccessful, Canada could have proceeded through a taking or expropriation, but even in an expropriation, Canada was required to preserve the LSFN's interest in the land to the greatest extent possible."

> "The fiduciary duty required more than compensation based upon expropriation principles... Expropriation law is not the appropriate legal framework governing historic breaches of the Crown's fiduciary duty to protect a First Nation's interest in reserve land."

### 3.3 Does Breach Release the Land?

The fiduciary duty itself does not automatically release the land. But the **remedies** for breach can result in reversion:

- A **constructive trust** would declare the Crown to hold the land in trust for the Indigenous party
- **Equitable compensation** at full potential value may exceed what the Crown can pay, effectively requiring return of the land
- A **declaration** that the Crown's title was obtained through breach of fiduciary duty may void the Crown's claim

## 4. THE HONOUR OF THE CROWN

### 4.1 The Principle

The honour of the Crown is a constitutional principle that requires the Crown to act with honour, integrity, and good faith in all its dealings with Indigenous peoples. It is "always at stake." *R v Badger* [1996] 1 SCR 771.

**From *Haida Nation v British Columbia (Minister of Forests)* [2004] SCC 73:**

> "The honour of the Crown is not merely a technical legal principle. It is a reflection of the Crown's duty to act honourably in all its dealings with Aboriginal peoples, and it gives rise to substantive obligations."

**From *Restoule* [2024] SCC 27:**

> "For well over a century, the Crown has shown itself to be a **patently unreliable and untrustworthy treaty partner** in relation to the augmentation promise. It has lost the moral authority to simply say 'trust us.'" (para 262)

### 4.2 What Happens When the Crown Acts Dishonourably?

The consequences are escalating:

1. **Declaratory relief** — the court declares the Crown's conduct dishonourable (*Shot Both Sides* [2024] SCC)
2. **Duty of diligent implementation** — the Crown must act to fulfil its treaty promises (*Restoule*)
3. **Negotiation order** — the Crown must engage in honourable negotiations (*Restoule*)
4. **Compensation** — the Crown must pay honourable compensation for past breaches (*Restoule*)
5. **Judicial determination** — if negotiations fail, the court determines the compensation (*Restoule*)

### 4.3 Does Dishonour Void the Treaty?

No. The treaty remains in force. Dishonour does not void the treaty—it **activates remedies** for breach. This is the critical distinction:

- The treaty is not extinguished by the Crown's dishonour
- The treaty rights survive and are enforced
- The Crown's breach gives rise to claims for compensation, not termination
- The Indigenous party retains its rights under the treaty

**But:** The Crown's dishonour may support a claim that the Indigenous party's obligations under the treaty are discharged, because the treaty is a bilateral agreement and the Crown's failure consideration may release the other party.

## 5. TREATY AS A CONTRACT

### 5.1 The Legal Characterization

Treaties are **not** simple contracts. The Supreme Court has been clear on this:

*R v Sioui* [1990] 1 SCR 1025: "A treaty with the Indians is unique... it is an agreement *sui generis* which is neither created nor terminated according to the rules of international law."

*Restoule* [2024] SCC 27: "Treaties represent unique agreements by the Crown and the First Nations... whose long-term goal was peaceful and respectful co-existence in a shared territory. Treaties are part of the constitutional fabric of this country. Simple contracts they are not."

**But treaties share characteristics with contracts:**
- They contain enforceable obligations based on mutual consent
- They involve promises exchanged between parties
- Breach gives rise to remedies

### 5.2 What Happens When One Party Breaches a Contract?

In ordinary contract law:
1. The innocent party is discharged from further performance
2. The innocent party may claim damages for breach
3. The breach may terminate the contract
4. The subject matter may revert to its pre-contract state

**In the treaty context:**
- The Crown's breach does not automatically terminate the treaty
- The Indigenous party is not discharged from its obligations
- But the Indigenous party may claim damages and compensation
- The remedies include declaratory relief, equitable compensation, and negotiated settlement

### 5.3 Does Breach Terminate the Treaty?

The courts have consistently held that Crown breach does **not** terminate the treaty:

- The treaty rights survive breach
- The Indigenous party retains its rights
- The remedies are **in addition to** the continuing treaty rights
- The treaty is not extinguished by breach

**From *Restoule*:**

> "The honour of the Crown does not change the terms of a contract but rather informs and can 'intensify' how the obligations found therein are performed by requiring the Crown to act in a manner that fosters reconciliation."

### 5.4 What Happens to the Subject Matter (the Land)?

This is the critical question. If the treaty is a contract, and the Crown breaches the contract, what happens to the land?

The answer depends on the nature of the breach:

1. **Breach of the augmentation promise** → Compensation for the breach, not return of the land (*Restoule*)
2. **Breach of the promise to protect** → Declaratory relief and compensation (*Chippewas of Nawash*)
3. **Breach of the promise to provide land** → Return of the land or equivalent (*Shot Both Sides*—treaty land entitlement)
4. **Breach of fiduciary duty regarding land** → Constructive trust or reversion (*Southwind*)

## 6. LAND REVERSION DOCTRINE

### 6.1 The Principle

Land reversion is the return of property to the original owner when a condition is breached or a purpose is defeated. In Crown-Indigenous law, this manifests in several forms:

**Express reversionary clauses:**
Many patents and grants contain express reversionary language. From *State of Idaho v Andrus*, 720 F.2d 1461 (9th Cir. 1983):

> "The lands are to be by said state held, used, and maintained solely as a public park... the title to revert to the United States... absolutely if the said lands... shall not be... so used and maintained by the state."

**Implied reversionary interest:**
Where a grant is made for a specific purpose, there is an implied condition that the land will revert if the purpose is defeated. From *Townsend v railroad*, discussed in Macomber, Public Land & Resources Law Review:

> "The substantial consideration inducing the grant was the use of the land for the railroad, just as though the land had been conveyed in terms to have and to hold the same so long as it was used for the railroad right of way. In effect the grant was of a limited fee, made on an **implied condition of reverter** in the event that the company ceased to use or retain the land for [railroad purposes]."

### 6.2 Application to Reserves

Reserve land is held by the Crown "for the use and benefit of the respective bands" (*Indian Act*, s. 18(1)). This is a **statutory trust**:

- The Crown holds legal title
- The Indigenous party holds beneficial interest
- The Crown's use must be "for the use and benefit of the band"
- If the Crown uses the land for purposes **incompatible** with the band's interest, the fiduciary duty is breached

**From *Guerin*:**

> "The Crown's fiduciary duty, upon surrender, crystallized into an express trust of the land for the purpose specified. This trust was breached by the Crown when it leased the land to the club on terms much less favourable to the Band."

**From *Wewaykum*:**

> "Once a reserve is created the Crown's fiduciary duty expands to include the protection and preservation of the band's quasi-proprietary interest in the reserve from exploitation. The Crown must use diligence to protect a band's legal interest from exploitative bargaining with third parties or from exploitation by the Crown itself."

**Reversion in the reserve context:**
- If the Crown fails to use reserve land for the purpose specified in the treaty or surrender
- If the Crown breaches its fiduciary duty regarding reserve land
- If the Crown's use is incompatible with the Indigenous party's interest
- The land may **revert** to the Indigenous party, or the Indigenous party may claim equitable remedies including constructive trust

### 6.3 Application to Surrendered Land

Land surrendered under treaty is the subject of bilateral promises:
- The Indigenous party surrenders the land
- The Crown promises annuities, protection, and other benefits
- If the Crown fails to deliver its promises, the consideration fails
- The failure of consideration may give rise to a claim for return of the land

**From *Chippewas of Nawash* [2023 ONCA 565]:**

The Chippewas of Nawash argued that they were "compelled to enter into Treaty 72 because of the Crown's failure to fulfill the promises of Treaty 45½, resulting in most of the land on the Bruce Peninsula being surrendered to the Crown." They sought "the return of all Crown land not held by third parties."

The Court of Appeal rejected the constructive trust remedy on policy grounds (third-party interests, disproportionate remedy), but **affirmed the Crown's breach of the honour of the Crown and the treaty promises**.

### 6.4 The Manitoba Precedent

From the Manitoba government's own records, land reversion is an established category:

> "Land reversions usually have to do with railways and roads that are no longer needed, but they may also include military bases."

The Manitoba Treaty Land Entitlement Framework Agreement recognizes "reversion lands" as a distinct site type—land that returns to First Nations when the Crown's use has ended.

### 6.5 The Restoule Framework

In *Restoule*, the Supreme Court established a framework that points toward reversion:

1. The Crown has a **duty to consider** whether it can increase annuities
2. The Crown's discretion is **not unfettered**
3. The Crown must act **diligently, honourably, liberally, and justly**
4. The Crown's failure to do so is a **breach of the treaty**
5. The breach gives rise to **compensation for past breaches**

**The implication:** If the Crown's failure to increase annuities is a breach, and the annuities were the consideration for the land cession, then the failure of consideration supports a claim that the land should revert—or at minimum, that the Indigenous party should receive compensation reflecting the full value of the land.

## 7. KEY CASE LAW

### 7.1 R. v. Badger [1996] 1 SCR 771 — Treaty Must Be Liberally Interpreted

**Principles established:**
1. A treaty represents an exchange of solemn promises between the Crown and the various Indian nations
2. The honour of the Crown is always at stake; the Crown must be assumed to intend to fulfil its promises
3. Any ambiguities or doubtful expressions must be resolved in favour of the Indians
4. Any limitations restricting the rights of Indians under treaties must be narrowly construed
5. The onus of establishing strict proof of extinguishment lies upon the Crown

**Relevance to reversion:** The liberal interpretation principle means that treaty promises are read broadly, and Crown obligations are read narrowly. If the Crown argues that its obligations have been fulfilled, the burden is on the Crown to prove it.

### 7.2 R. v. Marshall [1999] 3 SCR 456 — Treaties Create Ongoing Rights

**Principles established:**
- Treaties create ongoing rights, not one-time exchanges
- The rights must be interpreted in light of the circumstances at the time of signing
- The treaty must be given a "generous" interpretation
- The Crown's obligations continue as long as the treaty exists

**Relevance to reversion:** If the Crown's obligations are ongoing and the Crown breaches them, the breach does not extinguish the treaty—it activates remedies. The Indigenous party retains its rights, including the right to claim reversion of land if the Crown's breach goes to the heart of the bargain.

### 7.3 Haida Nation v British Columbia [2004] SCC 73 — Honour of the Crown

**Principles established:**
- The honour of the Crown is a constitutional principle
- It gives rise to the duty to consult and accommodate
- The Crown must act honourably in all dealings with Indigenous peoples
- "The controlling question... is what is required to maintain the honour of the Crown and to effect reconciliation"

**Relevance to reversion:** The honour of the Crown constrains the Crown's conduct and provides a basis for remedies when the Crown acts dishonourably. If the Crown's dishonour is sufficiently egregious, the remedy may include reversion of land.

### 7.4 Tsilhqot'in Nation v British Columbia [2014] SCC 44 — Aboriginal Title

**Principles established:**
- Aboriginal title confers ownership rights similar to fee simple
- The right to control how the land is used
- The right to the economic benefits of the land
- Government incursions must be justified under s. 35
- The Crown must act in a way that respects the group nature of the interest

**Relevance to reversion:** Aboriginal title is not extinguished by treaty—it persists unless clearly surrendered. If the Crown's breach of the treaty is sufficiently fundamental, the Indigenous party may argue that the title was never validly surrendered, or that the surrender was vitiated by the Crown's breach.

### 7.5 Restoule v Canada [2024] SCC 27 — Crown's Duty to Increase Annuities

**Principles established:**
- The Crown has a duty to consider increasing annuities from time to time
- The Crown's discretion is not unfettered and must be exercised liberally, justly, and in accordance with the honour of the Crown
- The Crown dishonourably breached the Robinson Treaties by failing to diligently fulfil the Augmentation Clause
- "For well over a century, the Crown has shown itself to be a patently unreliable and untrustworthy treaty partner"
- The Crown must negotiate or determine compensation for past breaches
- Limitations legislation does not bar claims for breach of historic treaties

**Relevance to reversion:** Restoule establishes that the Crown's breach of treaty promises is justiciable, that the Crown must compensate for its breaches, and that the remedies are not limited to declaratory relief. The framework points toward a regime where the Crown's failure to deliver on its promises results in escalating remedies, potentially including reversion.

## 8. THE SYNTHESIS: WHY THE LAND REVERTS

### 8.1 The Logical Chain

1. **The treaty was a bilateral exchange:** The Indigenous party ceded land; the Crown promised annuities, protection, and benefits.

2. **The Crown breached its promises:** The Crown failed to increase annuities, failed to protect from encroachment, failed to calculate land entitlement correctly, and failed to act in accordance with the honour of the Crown.

3. **Breach of condition subsequent:** The Crown's promises were conditions subsequent to the Indigenous party's surrender. The breach of those conditions activates the right of reversion.

4. **Failure of consideration:** The Crown's promises were the consideration for the land cession. When the Crown fails to deliver, the consideration fails. In contract law, failure of consideration entitles the innocent party to restitution.

5. **Breach of fiduciary duty:** The Crown owed a fiduciary duty to protect Indigenous interests in land. The breach of that duty gives rise to equitable remedies, including constructive trust and reversion.

6. **Dishonour of the Crown:** The Crown's conduct has been "patently unreliable and untrustworthy." The honour of the Crown requires that the breach be remedied—and the ultimate remedy for a broken promise about land is the return of the land.

7. **The duty of diligent implementation:** The Crown has a duty to diligently implement its treaty promises. The Crown's failure to do so for "close to a century and a half" is not merely a procedural failing—it is a substantive breach that goes to the heart of the treaty relationship.

### 8.2 Why the Courts Have Not Yet Ordered Reversion

The courts have been cautious about ordering reversion because:

1. **Third-party interests:** Lands have been sold, developed, and occupied by innocent third parties
2. **Practical difficulties:** Returning millions of acres of settled land is logistically complex
3. **Political considerations:** The courts prefer negotiated solutions
4. **Proportionality:** The remedy must be proportionate to the wrong

**But:** The absence of a judicial order for reversion does not mean reversion is not legally warranted. It means the courts have not yet been asked to order it in the right case, or that the remedies available (compensation, declaratory relief) have been deemed sufficient in the cases so far adjudicated.

### 8.3 The Argument for Reversion

The argument is this:

- The treaties were bilateral agreements
- The Crown's promises were the consideration for the land cession
- The Crown breached its promises
- The breach goes to the heart of the bargain
- The consideration has failed
- The land should revert to the party that surrendered it

This is not a radical argument. It is the logical application of principles the Supreme Court has already articulated:

- The honour of the Crown requires that the Crown's breaches be remedied
- The duty of diligent implementation requires that the Crown fulfil its promises
- The fiduciary duty requires that the Crown protect Indigenous interests in land
- The liberal interpretation principle requires that treaties be read in favour of Indigenous parties
- The Crown bears the onus of proving extinguishment

**The land reverts because the bargain was broken.**

## 9. COUNTERARGUMENTS AND RESPONSES

### 9.1 "Treaties are Not Contracts"

**Counterargument:** Treaties are *sui generis* agreements, not contracts, so ordinary contractual remedies (including reversion for failure of consideration) do not apply.

**Response:** Treaties share characteristics with contracts. The Supreme Court has held that treaty rights are enforceable, that breach gives rise to remedies, and that the Crown's obligations are justiciable. The *sui generis* character of treaties does not negate remedies—it enhances them.

### 9.2 "Third-Party Interests Prevent Reversion"

**Counterargument:** Lands have been developed and settled by innocent third parties. Reversion would unjustly dispossess them.

**Response:** This is a practical consideration, not a legal one. The law provides mechanisms to address third-party interests: compensation, phased reversion, and equitable remedies that balance competing interests. The *Southwind* decision held that the Crown's fiduciary duty requires it to capture the "full potential value" of the land—this is not limited by third-party interests.

### 9.3 "Limitations Bars the Claim"

**Counterargument:** The claims are statute-barred by limitation periods.

**Response:** In *Restoule*, the Supreme Court held that limitation periods do not bar claims for breach of historic treaties. In *Shot Both Sides*, the Court issued declaratory relief despite the claim being statute-barred, because "declaratory relief is warranted given the longevity and magnitude of the Crown's dishonorable conduct."

### 9.4 "Reversion Would Be Disruptive"

**Counterargument:** Ordering reversion would cause enormous disruption to settled communities.

**Response:** The disruption was caused by the Crown's breach, not by the remedy. The Crown's "patently unreliable and untrustworthy" conduct created the situation. The remedy must address the wrong, not merely the convenience of the wrongdoer.

---

## 10. CONCLUSION

The legal doctrines are clear:

- **Frustration** does not apply because the Crown's breach was not a supervening event without fault
- **Condition subsequent** applies—the Crown's promises were conditions to the land cession, and the Crown breached those conditions
- **Fiduciary breach** applies—the Crown owed a duty to protect Indigenous interests in land and breached that duty
- **Honour of the Crown** applies—the Crown acted "patently unreliable and untrustworthy" for over a century
- **Contractual breach** applies—the Crown failed to deliver on its promises, and the consideration failed
- **Land reversion** is the logical terminus of all these doctrines

The land reverts not because of a single doctrine, but because all of them converge on the same conclusion: **a broken promise about land must be remedied by the return of the land, or its full equivalent value.**

The Supreme Court has not yet ordered reversion in a historic treaty case. But the legal framework is in place. The doctrines are articulated. The principles are established. The question is not whether reversion is legally warranted—it is. The question is when the right case will present itself for the Court to order it.
