# 06: THE NEGLIGENCE OF THE CROWN AND THE TREATIES

## Document Purpose

This document catalogues the specific, documented failures of the Crown to uphold its treaty obligations to Indigenous Peoples in Canada. Every category of failure is supported by court rulings, government reports, and historical records. The evidence demonstrates a pattern of systematic breach spanning nearly two centuries.

---

## I. LAND FAILURES

### A. Inadequate Reserve Allocation

The Crown promised reserve lands but systematically failed to provide the acreage specified in treaties.

**Treaty 1 (1871):** Promised 160 acres per family of five. This was never fully delivered. The Sagkeeng Anicinabe community documented that Treaty 1 promised 160 acres to each family — "this did not happen" (Gray, 2014).

**Treaty 7 (1877):** Promised one square mile (640 acres) per family of five, or 128 acres per person. The Blood Tribe (Kainai) found their reserve was undersized by the Crown's own miscalculation. In *Shot Both Sides v. Canada* (2024 SCC), the Supreme Court confirmed the Crown's "dishonourable conduct" in miscalculating reserve size, though the claim was found statute-barred.

**Treaty 9 (1905-1906):** The Missanabie Cree were entitled to reserves sized by formula. No reserve was ever set aside. In *Fletcher v. Ontario* (2024 ONCA 148), the court confirmed: "a reserve should have been set aside for the Missanabie Cree shortly after the making of the Treaty" — but over 100 years later, none existed.

**Treaty 10 (1906):** The Clear Lake Band (Peter Pond Lake) was entitled to at least 30,208 acres but received only 27,080 acres — a shortfall of over 3,000 acres (Specific Claims Tribunal, 2021).

### B. Failure to Protect Reserve Lands

In *Williams Lake Indian Band v. Canada* (2018 SCC), the Supreme Court found the Crown breached its fiduciary duty by failing to take steps to protect Yucwt (the Band's traditional village land) from settler pre-emption, despite colonial officials knowing the land was being overtaken. The Court held:

> "The failure of colonial officials to take even these most basic steps to determine which lands should be set aside was a breach of the Crown's fiduciary duty."

### C. Ongoing Land Encroachment

Reserve lands continue to be encroached upon through:
- Resource extraction without consent
- Infrastructure projects (hydro, pipelines) flooding or bisecting reserves
- Municipal expansion onto reserve boundaries
- "Taking up" of lands under treaty provisions without adequate consultation

## II. EDUCATION FAILURES

### A. Residential Schools (1831-1996)

The Crown promised education on reserves. Instead, it created a system of forced assimilation.

**The Truth and Reconciliation Commission (2015) found:**

> "For over a century, the central goals of Canada's Aboriginal policy were to eliminate Aboriginal governments; ignore Aboriginal rights; terminate the Treaties; and, through a process of assimilation, cause Aboriginal peoples to cease to exist as distinct legal, social, cultural, religious, and racial entities in Canada. The establishment and operation of residential schools were a central element of this policy, which can best be described as 'cultural genocide.'"

**Key statistics:**
- **150,000** First Nation, Inuit, and Métis children attended residential schools
- **139** residential schools operated under the Indian Residential Schools Settlement Agreement
- **Last school closed:** 1996 (Gordon's Indian Residential School, Saskatchewan)
- **Duration:** Over 150 years of operation

**The schools were NOT what treaties promised.** Treaty negotiations called for on-reserve schools. Commissioner Alexander Morris wrote: "The treaties provide for the establishment of schools, on the reserves, for the instruction of the Indian children." Instead, the government created off-reserve residential schools designed to separate children from families.

**Sir John A. Macdonald told the House of Commons in 1883:**

> "When the school is on the reserve the child lives with its parents, who are savages... and though he may learn to read and write, his habits, and training and mode of thought are Indian. He is simply a savage who can read and write."

### B. Cultural Genocide

The TRC documented:
- Forced removal of children from families
- Prohibition of Indigenous languages
- Suppression of spiritual practices
- Physical, sexual, and emotional abuse
- Malnutrition and medical neglect
- Unmarked graves at school sites

### C. Missing Children

The National Centre for Truth and Reconciliation has documented over **4,100 children** who died at residential schools. The actual number is believed to be significantly higher due to incomplete records. As of 2024, ground-penetrating radar has identified thousands of unmarked burial sites at former school locations across Canada.

### D. The Sixties Scoop

Beyond residential schools, the Crown continued removing Indigenous children through child welfare systems from the 1960s onward, placing them with non-Indigenous families — a continuation of the same assimilation policy.

## III. HEALTHCARE FAILURES

### A. The Medicine Chest Clause (Treaty 6, 1876)

Treaty 6 contains a specific written promise:

> "That a medicine chest shall be kept at the house of each Indian agent for the use and benefit of the Indians at the direction of such agent."

The Crown has consistently reinterpreted this clause narrowly:

**In *Dreaver v. The King* (1935)**, the Federal Court ruled the medicine chest clause meant "all medicines, drugs or medical supplies were to be supplied free of charge to Treaty Indians." The Crown was not given discretion to choose which medicines to cover.

Despite this ruling, the federal government has never fully implemented this obligation. The clause has been:
- Reinterpreted as a literal medicine chest rather than comprehensive healthcare
- Underfunded systematically
- Used as a justification for minimal, rather than comprehensive, health services

### B. Tuberculosis Epidemics

The Crown's failure to provide healthcare had deadly consequences:

- Tuberculosis swept through reserve communities beginning in the 1880s
- The Qu'Appelle Valley reserves saw population decline by nearly half over three generations due to TB
- Malnutrition caused by Crown rationing policies weakened immune systems
- The Standing Buffalo Dakota Band, who did NOT enter treaty and thus were not subject to Indian Act controls, maintained better health outcomes — demonstrating that Crown administration itself was a contributing factor to disease

### C. Boil Water Advisories (Ongoing)

The Crown's failure to provide clean water is a continuing treaty breach:

**As of 2026:**
- **39 long-term drinking water advisories** in effect in 37 First Nations communities
- **156 long-term advisories** have been lifted since November 2015
- **40 long-term advisories** remain in effect (as of February 2026)
- **Neskantaga First Nation:** 30+ years under boil water advisory — the longest in Canada
- **Kashechewan First Nation:** Declared state of emergency in 2026 after water treatment plant failure
- **Pikangikum First Nation:** Declared state of emergency in 2025 and 2026

**Key failures:**
- In 2021, the federal government settled a class-action lawsuit for **$8 billion** to 120 First Nations communities — but as of 2026, Neskantaga reports receiving "nothing" from the settlement
- The 2021 Auditor General found that "Indigenous Services Canada did not provide the support necessary to ensure that First Nations have ongoing access to safe drinking water"
- Bill C-61 (First Nations Clean Water Act) died when Parliament was prorogued in January 2025
- In 2026, Bill C-37 was introduced but removed a provision recognizing that First Nations have a human right to clean drinking water

### D. Health Inequities

In 2023, Alberta Health data showed:
- **First Nations life expectancy:** 62.81 years
- **Non-First Nations life expectancy:** 81.88 years
- **Gap:** Over 19 years — the widest on record
- COVID-19 mortality was **4.5 times higher** among Indigenous people than the general population
- Opioid poisoning deaths among First Nations Albertans rose **366%** between 2016 and 2022

## IV. FINANCIAL FAILURES

### A. Frozen Annuities

The Crown promised annual payments that have never been increased:

**Numbered Treaties (1-11):**
- Originally $3/person (Treaties 1-2), raised to $5 in 1875
- Chiefs: $25/year
- Councillors: $15/year
- **$5 has remained unchanged for over 150 years**

**Robinson Treaties (1850):**
- Originally $1.60-$1.70/person, raised to $4 in 1875
- **$4 has remained unchanged since 1875**

**In *Ontario v. Restoule* (2024 SCC)**, the Supreme Court ruled unanimously:

> "For almost a century and a half, the Anishinaabe have been left with an empty shell of a treaty promise... The annuities have been frozen at a shocking $4 per person... This can only be described as a mockery of the Crown's treaty promise."

The Court found the Crown had "dishonourably breached" the Robinson Treaties and must negotiate compensation.

**The purchasing power erosion is staggering:**
- In 1850, $5 could buy 167 lbs of flour
- Today, $5 buys approximately 5 lbs of flour
- In 1910, $5 could buy 63 lbs of beef (enough for 10 people for a month)
- Today, $5 purchases 1.5 lbs of beef (enough for 1 person for 3 days)

**Multiple class actions are now proceeding:**
- Treaty 1 annuity claim (2026 trial)
- Treaty 2 (Lake Manitoba First Nation, filed 2023)
- Treaty 4 (Waywayseecappo First Nation, filed 2024)
- Treaty 5 (Fisher River Cree Nation, filed 2023)

### B. Agricultural Benefits Undelivered

Treaties promised "cows and plows" — cattle, seed grain, agricultural implements, and farm instructors. These were systematically failed:

**In * Alexis Nakota Sioux Nation v. Canada* (Specific Claims Tribunal, 2021):**
- The Crown failed to provide sufficient agricultural benefits
- Implements provided were "too poor-quality or too wild to be of any use"
- No instruction on proper use was provided
- The limited items provided were insufficient to transition to agriculture

**In 2022-2023 alone,** 23 agricultural assistance settlements totaled **$3.656 billion** — demonstrating the scale of the Crown's failure.

### C. Trust Fund Mismanagement

The Crown mismanaged Indian moneys, including:
- Timber allotment proceeds
- Oil and gas royalties
- Land sale revenues
- Treaty annuity funds

## V. HUNTING AND FISHING FAILURES

### A. Provincial Restrictions on Treaty Rights

The Crown promised hunting and fishing rights but allowed provincial governments to restrict them:

**In *R. v. Badger* (1996 SCC)**, the Court confirmed Treaty 8 hunting rights survive, but provinces can regulate for conservation. However, the regulations often go beyond conservation to effectively prohibit treaty hunting.

**In *R. v. Morris* (2006 SCC)**, the Court found provincial wildlife legislation can infringe treaty rights when the regulations are "overbroad" — prohibiting both safe and unsafe practices without distinction.

**In *R. v. Nikal* (1993 SCC)**, the Court found the licensing scheme infringed aboriginal fishing rights, but upheld it as a justified infringement for conservation.

### B. Commercial Fishing Rights Denied

**In *R. v. Marshall* (1999 SCC)**, the Supreme Court affirmed the Mi'kmaq treaty right to sell and trade fish — but subsequent regulatory frameworks have limited the practical exercise of this right.

### C. Pattern of Restriction

Across Canada, Indigenous hunting and fishing rights have been:
- Subject to provincial licensing requirements
- Restricted to "food only" in many areas
- Limited by conservation regulations that also apply to commercial and sport fishers
- Denied through inadequate consultation
- Challenged in court repeatedly, with mixed results

## VI. SPECIFIC CLAIMS: THE BACKLOG

### A. Scale of the Problem

The Specific Claims process was created in 1973 to address historical breaches. The backlog tells the story of ongoing negligence:

**As of 2024-2025:**
- **792 claims** remain in the backlog (up from 532 in 2017)
- **718 claims** under assessment or negotiation
- **74 claims** under adjudication
- **Average processing time:** Historically 13 years; many claims take 20+ years
- One claim submitted in 1987 took **26 years** to settle

### B. Structural Failures

The Auditor General of Canada has repeatedly found:
- The Specific Claims Branch missed its legislated three-year deadline on **86%** of claims
- Canada failed to communicate with Indigenous Nations about delays
- The process is "confusing, time-consuming, expensive, adversarial, and legalistic"
- The Crown simultaneously acts as defendant, adjudicator, and funder — an inherent conflict of interest

### C. Funding Cuts

In 2026, federal funding for specific claims research was cut by **75%**:
- Budget dropped from $12 million to $4 million annually
- First Nations submitted 192 funding applications totaling $34.5 million
- **98 First Nations were denied funding outright**
- This occurred while the backlog was growing, not shrinking

### D. The "Perpetual Motion Machine"

As the Fraser Institute documented, settling more claims leads to discovery of new grievances and filing of more claims. But this is not evidence of frivolous claims — it is evidence of the scale of historical breach. The Crown's response has been to attempt to terminate the process rather than address the underlying obligations.

## VII. THE HONOUR OF THE CROWN: LEGAL FRAMEWORK

### A. Supreme Court Principles

The Supreme Court has established that:
- "The honour of the Crown is not a mere incantation" — it must be given effect (*Haida Nation v. British Columbia*, 2004)
- Treaty rights are "sacred promises" that must be interpreted generously
- The Crown must act honourably in all dealings with Indigenous Peoples
- The Crown has a **duty of diligent implementation** of treaty promises (*Restoule*, 2024)

### B. The Crown's Own Admission

In *Ontario v. Restoule* (2024), neither Canada nor Ontario disputed that they were in "longstanding breach" of the Robinson Treaties. The Supreme Court noted:

> "For well over a century, the Crown has shown itself to be a patently unreliable and untrustworthy treaty partner in relation to the augmentation promise. It has lost the moral authority to simply say 'trust us'."

---

## VIII. SYNTHESIS: THE PATTERN OF NEGLIGENCE

The evidence demonstrates not isolated failures but a **systematic pattern of breach**:

1. **The Crown made promises to secure land** — then failed to provide adequate reserves
2. **The Crown promised education** — then created residential schools for cultural destruction
3. **The Crown promised healthcare** — then reinterpreted, underfunded, and denied its obligations
4. **The Crown promised financial support** — then froze payments for 150 years
5. **The Crown promised hunting and fishing rights** — then allowed provinces to restrict them
6. **The Crown created a claims process** — then starved it of resources and missed its own deadlines

The Honour of the Crown, which should animate every interaction, has been systematically abandoned. The treaties were not one-time transactions — they were intended to establish perpetual relationships. The Crown's negligence is not merely historical; it is ongoing, structural, and documented.
