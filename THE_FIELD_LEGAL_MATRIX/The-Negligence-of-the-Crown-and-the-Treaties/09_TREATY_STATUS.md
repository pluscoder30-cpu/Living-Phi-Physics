## OVERARCHING LEGAL FRAMEWORK

### Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

> "The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed." — Section 35(1)

**Critical principle:** Section 35 does not create rights — it recognizes and affirms *existing* rights. Rights validly extinguished before April 17, 1982 are not revived. Since 1982, Aboriginal and treaty rights cannot be unilaterally extinguished by Parliament (*Delgamuukw*, *Sparrow*).

**"Existing" does not freeze:** Rights are not frozen at 1982; they may evolve and be interpreted in a contemporary form (*Marshall*, *Desautel*).

### What "Active" Means for a Treaty

A treaty is **active** (in force) if:
1. It has not been validly extinguished by clear and plain legislative intent
2. It has not been superseded by a subsequent agreement that replaces it
3. It has not been voided by a court of competent jurisdiction
4. Its rights continue to be recognized under Section 35

### Can Historic Treaties Be "Voided"?

**No.** Under Canadian law, historic treaties are *sui generis* — unique agreements that are neither created nor terminated according to international treaty law principles (*Simon v. The Queen*, 1985). The Supreme Court has held that Indian treaties cannot be terminated by subsequent hostilities or breach alone. "Extinguishment cannot be lightly implied" (*United States v. Santa Fe Pacific R. Co.*, cited in *Simon*).

## 1. PEACE AND FRIENDSHIP TREATIES (1725–1779)

### Status: **ACTIVE — Never Extinguished**

### Legal Basis
- *R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 SCR 456 — Supreme Court of Canada
- *Simon v. The Queen* [1985] 2 SCR 387 — Supreme Court of Canada
- *R. v. Paul* [1980] — New Brunswick Court of Appeal

### Key Facts

| Treaty | Date | Parties | Status |
|--------|------|---------|--------|
| Treaty of Boston (Dummer's) | 1725 | Wabanaki Confederacy (Penobscot, Abenaki, Passamaquoddy) | Active |
| Mascarene's Treaty | 1726 | Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik | Active |
| 1749 Treaties | 1749 | Mi'kmaq | Active |
| Treaty of 1752 | 1752 | Mi'kmaq | Active — confirmed in *Simon* |
| Treaties of 1760–61 | 1760–61 | Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, Passamaquoddy | Active — confirmed in *Marshall* |
| Fort Howe Treaty | 1778 | Wolastoqiyik, Mi'kmaq | Active |
| 1779 Treaty | 1779 | Mi'kmaq (Cape Tormentine to Chaleur Bay) | Active |

### Why Active?

1. **No land surrender.** These treaties did not involve surrender of land or resources. They guaranteed hunting, fishing, and land-use rights.
2. **Never extinguished.** No legislation or treaty has validly extinguished the rights contained in these agreements.
3. **Constitutionally protected.** Since *Marshall* (1999), these rights are protected under Section 35.
4. **"Sui generis" termination rules apply.** Per *Simon*, Indian treaties are not terminated by subsequent hostilities. The Treaty of 1752 was held to remain in force despite armed conflict in 1753.
5. **Marshall affirmed enforcement.** The SCC held that the Peace and Friendship Treaties created ongoing treaty rights "to obtain necessaries through hunting and fishing by trading the products of…traditional activities."

### Disputes
- **Scope of commercial rights:** *Marshall II* (1999) clarified that treaty rights are not unlimited and can be regulated for conservation or important public objectives.
- **Which communities are parties:** Some scholars argue certain Mi'kmaq communities did not sign treaties in 1760–61, but British documentation treats them as parties.
- **Non-Indigenous fishing communities:** The West Nova Fishermen's Coalition and others disputed the scope of *Marshall*.

### What "Active" Means Here
The treaties remain in force as constitutional obligations. The Crown must honour the hunting, fishing, and trade rights guaranteed to Mi'kmaq, Wolastoqiyik, and Passamaquoddy peoples.

## 2. UPPER CANADA LAND SURRENDERS (1764–1862)

### Status: **SUPERSEDED / PARTIALLY ACTIVE**

### Legal Basis
- Series of 32 land cession treaties negotiated under the Royal Proclamation of 1763
- Most were resolved or superseded by the Williams Treaties (1923)
- Some remain disputed

### Key Facts

| Treaty | Date | Area | Status |
|--------|------|------|--------|
| Niagara River Strip | 1764 | Strip along Niagara River | Historical |
| Various land surrenders | 1781–1862 | Southern Ontario | Most superseded |
| McKee Treaty | 1790 | Southwestern Ontario | Superseded by Williams Treaties |
| Chenail Écarté | 1796 | St. Clair area | Superseded by Williams Treaties |
| Rice Lake Purchase | 1818 | Rice Lake area | Superseded by Williams Treaties |
| Huron Tract | 1827 | Southwestern Ontario | Superseded by Williams Treaties |
| Manitoulin Island | 1862 | Manitoulin Island | Partially active (see §7) |

### Legal Analysis

**The Crown's position:** These treaties extinguished all Aboriginal rights and title to the surrendered lands. Hunting and fishing rights were limited to reserve lands only.

**Indigenous position:** Many communities argued that:
- Outside promises (oral commitments) were not honoured
- Not all lands were properly ceded
- Hunting and fishing rights were broader than the Crown acknowledged

### How Superseded?
The Williams Treaties (1923) were specifically negotiated to resolve outstanding claims arising from the Upper Canada Land Surrenders. The Williams Commission confirmed that many problems existed with the earlier treaties' allotment of lands and that not all lands had been fully ceded.

### What "Active" Means Here
The land surrenders themselves are historical. The underlying Aboriginal title to those specific parcels has been extinguished. However, the Williams Treaties that superseded them contain their own ongoing obligations (see §6).

## 3. ROBINSON TREATIES (1850)

### Status: **ACTIVE — IN BREACH**

### Legal Basis
- *Ontario (Attorney General) v. Restoule* [2024] SCC 27 — Supreme Court of Canada (unanimous)
- *R. v. Badger* [1996] 1 SCR 771
- Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

### Key Facts

| Treaty | Date | Parties | Status |
|--------|------|---------|--------|
| Robinson-Huron Treaty | September 1850 | Anishinaabe of Lake Huron + Crown | **ACTIVE — IN BREACH** |
| Robinson-Superior Treaty | September 1850 | Anishinaabe of Lake Superior + Crown | **ACTIVE — IN BREACH** |

### Why Active?

1. **Never extinguished.** The Robinson Treaties contain land cessions but also ongoing obligations (annuities, augmentation clause).
2. **Constitutionally protected.** Protected under Section 35.
3. **SCC confirmed ongoing force.** In *Restoule* (2024), the Supreme Court confirmed the treaties are in force and the Crown has a mandatory duty to consider increasing annuities.
4. **Neither Canada nor Ontario disputes the treaties are in force.** Both acknowledged longstanding breach.

### The Breach

The Crown has been in **continuous breach** since 1875:

- The Augmentation Clause promised that annuities would be "augmented from time to time" if the ceded lands produced sufficient revenue without the Crown incurring loss.
- Annuities were increased to $4 per person in 1875 and **have been frozen at that amount for nearly 150 years**.
- The SCC described this as "a mockery of the Crown's treaty promise."
- Since 1875, the Crown has **failed to even consider** whether it could increase the annuities.

### Restoule (2024) — Key Holdings

1. **Treaty interpretation is reviewable on correctness** (not deference).
2. **The Crown has a mandatory and reviewable duty** to consider increasing annuities from time to time when economic circumstances permit.
3. **The Crown must increase annuities with respect to the past** given the "longstanding and egregious" nature of the breach.
4. **Breach of treaty claims are NOT statute-barred** by Ontario's limitations legislation.
5. **No fiduciary duty** attaches to the Augmentation Clause, but the **honour of the Crown** requires diligent implementation.
6. **The Crown must negotiate** with the Superior plaintiffs about compensation for past breaches, or face judicial determination.

### What "Active" Means Here
The treaties are fully in force. The Crown owes active, ongoing, mandatory obligations. The treaties have been breached for nearly 150 years but remain constitutionally binding.

## 4. DOUGLAS TREATIES (1850–1854)

### Status: **ACTIVE**

### Legal Basis
- *R. v. White and Bob* [1965] — BC Court of Appeal (affirmed by SCC)
- *R. v. Bartleman* [1984] — BC Court of Appeal
- Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

### Key Facts

| Treaty | Date | Area | Status |
|--------|------|------|--------|
| 14 Vancouver Island Treaties | 1850–1854 | Victoria, Saanich, Sooke, Nanaimo, Port Hardy areas | **ACTIVE** |

### Why Active?

1. **Court-confirmed as treaties.** *R. v. White and Bob* (1965) confirmed the Douglas Treaties are treaties under the Indian Act, with hunting guarantees that take priority over provincial game laws.
2. **Constitutionally protected.** Section 35 protects these treaty rights.
3. **Never extinguished.** Despite decades of government denial, the courts have recognized these as valid treaties.
4. **Exercised in modern courts.** The hunting and fishing rights have been successfully relied upon in multiple court proceedings.

### Key Treaty Terms
- Indigenous signatories agreed to share part of their territories for settlement
- In exchange: small cash payments, blankets, clothing
- **Reserved rights:** village sites and enclosed fields for continued use
- **Hunting rights:** "liberty to hunt over unoccupied lands"
- **Fishing rights:** "carry on their fisheries as formerly"

### Disputes

1. **Blank papers.** James Douglas had leaders sign blank papers; the written text was added later. The language suggests a complete surrender, which Indigenous peoples dispute.
2. **Which communities are parties.** There are 14 recorded treaties, but some communities dispute the terms.
3. **Scope of hunting rights.** *Bartleman* (1984) confirmed the Saanich treaty right to hunt on unoccupied land includes lands anywhere in their traditional hunting territory.
4. **Overlap with modern treaties.** The Te'mexw Treaty Association is negotiating modern treaties that must respect the Douglas Treaties.

### What "Active" Means Here
The treaties are fully in force and constitutionally protected. The Crown must honour the hunting, fishing, and village site rights guaranteed to the signatory nations.

## 5. MANITOULIN ISLAND TREATIES (1862)

### Status: **ACTIVE — PARTIALLY DISPUTED**

### Legal Basis
- Treaty 94 (McDougall Treaty), October 6, 1862
- 1836 Manitoulin Island Treaty (prior agreement)
- 1990 settlement (unsold surrendered lands)
- Wiikwemkoong land claim (1997–2022)

### Key Facts

| Treaty | Date | Parties | Status |
|--------|------|---------|--------|
| Manitoulin Island Treaty (1836) | 1836 | British Crown + all Manitoulin Island peoples | Historical — partially superseded |
| Manitoulin Island Treaty (1862) | 1862 | Ottawa, Chippewa + other bands (excluding Wiikwemkoong) | **ACTIVE** |

### Why Active?

1. **Five reserves created.** M'Chigeeng, Sheguiandah, Sheshegwaning, Aundeck Omni Kaning, and Zhiibaahaasing First Nations were established under this treaty.
2. **Never extinguished.** The treaty remains the legal basis for reserve lands on western Manitoulin.
3. **Ongoing obligations.** The Crown still owes investment fund proceeds and other treaty benefits.
4. **1990 settlement partially resolved.** A settlement addressed unsold surrendered lands for the five participating First Nations.

### Wiikwemkoong Unceded Territory

**Critical distinction:** Wiikwemkoong **refused to sign** the 1862 treaty and was deliberately excluded by the Crown. Their lands remain **unceded** — governed by the 1836 treaty, not the 1862 treaty.

- Wiikwemkoong submitted a specific land claim in 1997 for unsurrendered islands adjacent to the community.
- A settlement was reached by 2022 involving provincial Crown land.

### Disputes

1. **Exclusion of Wiikwemkoong.** The purposeful exclusion raises questions about the nature of the surrender and underlying title.
2. **Boundary disputes.** The boundary between Wiikwemkoong's unceded lands and the rest of the island was never properly surveyed.
3. **Adjacent islands.** Islands adjacent to Wiikwemkoong were not surrendered in 1862.
4. **1836 treaty interaction.** The 1836 treaty placed the entire island into collective ownership — the 1862 treaty carved out only part of it.
5. **Mainland access rights.** People on the mainland accessed many islands seasonally; their use could not be surrendered by resident Chiefs.

### What "Active" Means Here
The 1862 treaty is active for the five western Manitoulin First Nations. Wiikwemkoong's territory remains unceded. The treaty created obligations that the Crown continues to owe.

## 6. WILLIAMS TREATIES (1923)

### Status: **ACTIVE — SUPPLEMENTED BY 2018 SETTLEMENT**

### Legal Basis
- Williams Treaties (October–November 1923)
- Alderville Litigation (1992–2018)
- Williams Treaties First Nations Settlement Agreement (2018)
- Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982

### Key Facts

| Treaty | Date | Parties | Status |
|--------|------|---------|--------|
| Williams Treaty (Chippewa) | October 31, 1923 | Chippewa of Rama, Christian Island, Georgina Island + Crown | **ACTIVE — SUPPLEMENTED** |
| Williams Treaty (Mississauga) | November 21, 1923 | Mississauga of Alderville, Scugog Lake, Mud Lake, Rice Lake + Crown | **ACTIVE — SUPPLEMENTED** |

### Why Active?

1. **Last historic land cession treaties in Canada.** These were the final treaties surrendering Aboriginal lands in southern Ontario.
2. **Never extinguished.** Despite the Crown's failure to honour obligations, the treaties remain in force.
3. **Constitutionally protected.** Protected under Section 35.
4. **2018 Settlement acknowledged ongoing force.** The settlement did not extinguish the treaties but addressed specific breaches.

### The 2018 Settlement

The Williams Treaties First Nations filed the Alderville Litigation in 1992. The litigation was settled out of court in 2018:

- **Financial compensation:** $1.11 billion ($666M from Canada, $444M from Ontario)
- **Recognition of harvesting rights:** Pre-existing treaty harvesting rights acknowledged
- **Land entitlement:** Each First Nation can add up to 11,000 acres to reserve
- **Formal apologies:** Federal and provincial governments apologized for negative impacts

### Disputes

1. **Harvesting rights.** The Crown interpreted the Williams Treaties as surrendering all hunting and fishing rights; the First Nations argued these rights were preserved.
2. **Outside promises.** Oral commitments not included in written texts remain a source of dispute.
3. **Implementation.** The settlement addresses historical breaches but the ongoing treaty relationship requires continued negotiation.

### What "Active" Means Here
The treaties remain in force. The 2018 Settlement addressed specific historical grievances but did not replace or extinguish the treaties. The Crown continues to owe obligations under both the original treaties and the settlement agreement.

## 7. NUMBERED TREATIES 1–11 (1871–1921)

### Status: **ACTIVE — ALL 11 IN FORCE**

### Legal Basis
- Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982
- *R. v. Badger* [1996] 1 SCR 771
- *R. v. Marshall* [1999] 3 SCR 456
- *Ontario (Attorney General) v. Restoule* [2024] SCC 27
- *Shot Both Sides v. Canada* [2024] SCC

### Comprehensive Status Table

| Treaty | Date | Region | Status | Key Issues |
|--------|------|--------|--------|------------|
| **Treaty 1** | August 1871 | Southern Manitoba | **ACTIVE** | Limited hunting/fishing provisions; annuities increased to $5 |
| **Treaty 2** | August 1871 | Manitoba | **ACTIVE** | Same limitations as Treaty 1 |
| **Treaty 3** | October 1873 | NW Ontario | **ACTIVE** | 640 acres/family; guaranteed hunting/fishing; annuity $5 |
| **Treaty 4** | September 1874 | SE Saskatchewan | **ACTIVE** | Similar to Treaty 3; trapping included |
| **Treaty 5** | September 1875 | Northern Manitoba | **ACTIVE** | 160 acres/family; similar terms |
| **Treaty 6** | August 1876 | Central Saskatchewan/Alberta | **ACTIVE** | Medicine chest clause; famine assistance; $1000/year farm supplement |
| **Treaty 7** | September 1877 | Southern Alberta | **ACTIVE** | Ranching focus; cattle instead of implements; teacher salaries |
| **Treaty 8** | June 1899 | NE BC/Alberta/NWT | **ACTIVE** | Largest area; hunting/fishing rights; 160 acres for individuals |
| **Treaty 9** | July 1905–1929 | Northern Ontario | **ACTIVE** | Annuity $4; no ammunition/twine; multiple adhesions |
| **Treaty 10** | 1906–1907 | Northern Saskatchewan | **ACTIVE** | Similar to Treaty 8; lands in severalty |
| **Treaty 11** | 1921 | Great Slave Lake/NWT | **ACTIVE** | Last numbered treaty; oil/gas discovered shortly after |

### Why All Are Active?

1. **Never extinguished.** No numbered treaty has been validly extinguished by legislation or subsequent agreement.
2. **Constitutionally protected.** All are protected under Section 35(1).
3. **Ongoing obligations.** The Crown continues to owe annuities, reserve lands, and other treaty benefits.
4. **"Outside promises" remain enforceable.** Oral commitments made during negotiations are legally enforceable even if not in written texts (*R. v. Badger*).
5. **Shot Both Sides (2024) confirmed.** Treaty rights were enforceable at common law before Section 35 came into force; the Constitution did not create a new cause of action.

### Key Legal Principles

- **Liberal construction.** Treaties must be given "a fair, large and liberal construction in favour of the Indians" (*Simon*, *Badger*).
- **Ambiguities resolved in favour of Indigenous parties.** (*Simon*, *Sioui*, *Badger*, *Restoule*).
- **Honour of the Crown.** The Crown must act honourably in all treaty dealings.
- **Not frozen in time.** Treaty rights may be interpreted in a contemporary form (*Marshall*, *Simon*).
- **Modern activity as logical evolution.** A modern activity represents a "logical evolution" from the traditional activity at the time the treaty was made (*Morris*, *Marshall*).

### Disputes

1. **"Outside promises" — oral commitments.** Many First Nations claim the Crown made oral promises not recorded in treaty texts (e.g., agricultural assistance in Treaties 1 and 2).
2. **Land entitlement shortfalls.** The Crown failed to provide the promised reserve lands in many cases (*Shot Both Sides* — Blood Tribe Treaty 7 claim).
3. **Annuity adequacy.** $4–$5 per year has never been meaningfully increased.
4. **Resource revenue sharing.** Numbered treaties are silent on resource revenue sharing, leading to ongoing disputes.
5. **Treaty 9 adhesions.** Some Cree communities signed adhesions reluctantly; the scope of their consent is disputed.

### What "Active" Means Here
All 11 Numbered Treaties are fully in force and constitutionally protected. The Crown owes ongoing obligations including annuities, reserve lands, hunting/fishing rights, and implementation of oral promises. Breaches are actionable and not statute-barred.

### Why All Are Active?

1. **Constitutional status.** Modern treaties are constitutional documents within Section 35 (*Beckman*, *Moses*).
2. **Paramountcy clauses.** Most modern treaties are paramount over other federal and provincial laws of general application.
3. **Comprehensive governance framework.** They establish detailed legal frameworks for the relationship between Indigenous nations and the Crown.
4. **Self-government provisions.** 20 of 27 include self-government or related provisions.
5. **Ongoing implementation.** Canada's Collaborative Modern Treaty Implementation Policy (2023) commits to timely and effective fulfillment.

### How Modern Treaties Relate to Historic Treaties

**Critical distinction:** Modern treaties do **not** supersede or extinguish historic treaty rights unless explicitly stated. They address Aboriginal rights and title in areas where such rights have **not** been dealt with by historic treaties.

- **No overlap principle.** Modern treaties are negotiated in areas without prior treaty coverage.
- **Historic treaties remain in force.** Modern treaties operate alongside, not instead of, historic treaties.
- **"Today's modern treaty will become tomorrow's historic treaty."** (*Beckman*, per Binnie J.)

### Interpretive Differences from Historic Treaties

| Historic Treaties | Modern Treaties |
|-------------------|-----------------|
| Liberal, purposive interpretation | Greater emphasis on written text |
| Ambiguities resolved in favour of Indigenous parties | Respect for negotiated terms |
| Oral promises enforceable | Detailed written agreements |
| Context-heavy analysis | Deference to parties' expressed intentions |
| Non-technical reading | Product of well-resourced, legally represented parties |

### What "Active" Means Here
All modern treaties are fully in force and constitutionally protected. They represent comprehensive, forward-looking agreements that establish ongoing governance relationships. The Crown must implement all obligations in good faith.

## 9. SELF-GOVERNMENT AGREEMENTS

### Status: **ACTIVE**

### Legal Basis
- Section 35 of the Constitution Act, 1982
- *R. v. Sparrow* [1990] 1 SCR 1075

### Key Facts

|-----------|--------|------|--------|

| Tłı̨chǫ Agreement | Tłı̨chǫ Nation | 2005 | **ACTIVE** |

| + 18 additional with CLCAs | Various | Various | **ACTIVE** |
| + 3 standalone (Champagne and Aishihik, self-gov only) | Various | Various | **ACTIVE** |

### Why Active?

1. **Constitutional protection.** Self-government agreements flow from Section 35 and are constitutionally protected.
2. **Recognition of inherent right.** Self-government agreements recognize the inherent right of self-government as an Aboriginal right.
3. **Jurisdiction transfer.** They transfer law-making authority over specific areas (education, health, land use, citizenship, etc.) to Indigenous governments.
4. **Nation-to-nation relationship.** The Crown and Indigenous nations are recognized as co-equal governments within the agreement framework.

### Disputes

1. **Scope of jurisdiction.** Disputes over what areas of law-making authority are transferred vs. retained.
2. **Fiscal relationships.** Ongoing negotiations about adequate and stable funding.
3. **Relationship to historic treaties.** Self-government agreements must be consistent with and build upon historic treaty rights.
4. **Overlap agreements.** Where multiple Indigenous nations have overlapping territories, overlap agreements must be negotiated.

### What "Active" Means Here
Self-government agreements are fully in force and constitutionally protected. They establish Indigenous governance jurisdiction over specified areas and create ongoing obligations on all parties.

## CROSS-CUTTING LEGAL PRINCIPLES

### Treaty Supersession

**Canadian law does not recognize automatic treaty supersession.** Newer treaties do not automatically replace or extinguish older ones unless explicitly stated. Key principles:

1. **No implied supersession.** A later treaty does not implicitly supersede an earlier one.
2. **Express language required.** To extinguish treaty rights, clear and plain legislative intent is required (*Delgamuukw*, *Sparrow*).
3. **Robinson model.** The Robinson Treaties (1850) provided a model for subsequent Numbered Treaties, but did not supersede the Peace and Friendship Treaties.
4. **Williams Treaties resolved specific claims.** The Williams Treaties addressed problems with the Upper Canada Land Surrenders but did not extinguish all Aboriginal rights.

### Treaty Termination

**Treaty termination is virtually impossible under Canadian law:**

1. **Sui generis nature.** Indian treaties are not international treaties and cannot be terminated by international law principles (*Simon*).
2. **No termination by hostilities.** Armed conflict does not automatically terminate treaties (*Simon* — Treaty of 1752).
3. **No termination by breach.** Breach of treaty terms does not terminate the treaty; it creates actionable obligations.
4. **Constitutional protection.** Since 1982, treaties cannot be terminated by legislation.
5. **Voluntary surrender only.** Since 1982, Aboriginal and treaty rights can only be extinguished by voluntary surrender to the Crown or constitutional amendment.

### Treaty Void

**No historic treaty has been voided by court decision in Canada.** Courts have consistently held that:

1. Treaties are solemn promises that must be honoured.
2. Ambiguities are resolved in favour of Indigenous parties.
3. The honour of the Crown is presumed.
4. "Extinguishment cannot be lightly implied."
5. Treaty rights were enforceable before Section 35 came into force.

### Modern Treaties and Numbered Treaties

**Modern treaties do not supersede Numbered Treaties.** They address different geographic areas and different time periods:

- Numbered Treaties cover the Prairies, northern Ontario, and parts of the NWT (1871–1921)
- Modern treaties cover areas where Aboriginal rights have not been dealt with by historic treaty (1973–present)
- Both are constitutionally protected under Section 35

## KEY TAKEAWAY

**Every category of treaty in Canada remains active.** No historic treaty has been validly extinguished, voided, or superseded. The Crown's obligations under all treaties remain constitutionally protected under Section 35. The primary legal issue is not whether treaties are in force — they all are — but whether the Crown has honoured its obligations. The answer, overwhelmingly, is that it has not.
