# TREATY 9, TREATY 10, AND TREATY 11 (1899–1921)
## The Last Numbered Treaties: Oil, Land, and the Crown's Final Negotiations

---

## OVERVIEW

Treaties 9, 10, and 11 represent the final phase of Canada's Numbered Treaty system, covering the vast northern territories that the Crown had previously ignored. Unlike the southern treaties negotiated for agricultural settlement, these northern treaties were driven by resource extraction — timber, minerals, and ultimately oil. The pattern is consistent: Indigenous nations petitioned for treaties for decades; the Crown refused until economic interests demanded action; and when treaties were finally signed, the written text diverged from oral promises, creating a legacy of broken commitments that persists today.

| Treaty | Date | Territory | Area | Nations | Trigger |
|--------|------|-----------|------|---------|---------|
| **Treaty 9** | 1905–1906 (adhesions 1929–1930) | Northern Ontario (James Bay watershed) | ~330,000 km² (with adhesions) | Ojibway, Cree, Oji-Cree (~49 First Nations) | Mining, railway construction, settlement pressure |
| **Treaty 10** | 1906–1907 | Northern Saskatchewan + small portion of Alberta | ~220,000 km² | Chipewyan, Cree, Dene | Creation of provinces (1905), Métis scrip claims |
| **Treaty 11** | 1921–1922 | Western NWT (Mackenzie District) | ~372,000 km² (over 950,000 km² total with overlaps) | Slave (Sahtu), Dogrib (Tłı̨chǫ), Loucheux (Gwich'in), Hare, Dene | **Oil discovery at Fort Norman (1920)** |

## TREATY 9 — THE JAMES BAY TREATY (1905–1906)

### The Text

Treaty 9 was negotiated in two phases:
- **1905 signings**: July 12 – August 31, 1905, covering communities along the Albany River and Moose River systems
- **1906 signings**: June 7 – July 17, 1906, covering Abitibi, Matachewan, Mattagami, Flying Post, and Chapleau
- **Adhesions**: 1929–1930, extending coverage to communities north of the Albany River

**Full text**: https://www.rcaanc-cirnac.gc.ca/eng/1100100028863/1581293189896
**PDF**: http://iportal.usask.ca/docs/ICC/treaties/9_eng.pdf

### The Parties

**Crown Commissioners**:
- Duncan C. Scott (Deputy Superintendent General of Indian Affairs)
- Samuel Stewart (Indian Affairs)
- Daniel G. MacMartin (Ontario government representative — first time a province participated in treaty negotiations)

**Indigenous Signatories** (1905):
- Chief Missabay (Osnaburgh)
- Chief Katchange, Yesno (Fort Hope)
- Chief Wm. Whitehead (Marten Falls)
- Chief Frederick Mark (Moose Factory)
- Chief Esau Omakess (New Post)
- Chief Louis McDougall Jr. (Abitibi, 1906)
- Chief Michel Baptiste (Matachewan, 1906)
- Chief Andrew Luke (Mattagami, 1906)
- Chief Albert Black Ice (Flying Post, 1906)

**Adhesions signatories (1929–1930)**: Walter Charles Cain and Herbert Nathaniel Awrey

**Approximately 3,000 Indians paid in 1905–06; at least 2,000 added through adhesions.**

### The Date

- **Signed**: November 6, 1905 (formal); signing ceremonies July 12 – August 31, 1905, and June 7 – July 17, 1906
- **Ratified**: Order in Council approving adhesions and reserves (date varies by schedule)
- **Adhesions signed**: 1929–1930

### The Territory

**Over 330,000 km²** — larger than the United Kingdom and Ireland combined. Covers approximately **two-thirds of Northern Ontario**:

- The James Bay and Hudson Bay watersheds in Ontario
- From the height of land south to approximately the Albany River
- East to James Bay coast
- West to the Manitoba border
- North to Hudson Bay

**49 First Nations** currently live within Treaty 9 territory.

### The Promises (Crown's Obligations)

**From the written treaty text:**

1. **Annual annuities in perpetuity**:
   - Chiefs: $25/year
   - Headmen: $15/year
   - Every other Indian: $5/year

2. **One-time gratuity**:
   - Chiefs: $32
   - Headmen: $22
   - Every other Indian: $12

3. **Reserves**: Set aside for each band "in sufficient quantity to provide one square mile for each family of five" (this was NOT in the written text — it was an oral promise)

4. **Right to hunt, trap, and fish** throughout the surrendered territory, "subject to such regulations as may from time to time be made"

5. **Education**: "Provision for the education of Indian children" as "deemed advisable"

6. **Flag and medal**: Each chief to receive a silver medal, a suitable flag, and a copy of the treaty

7. **Tools**: Each chief to receive "a suitable suit of clothing" every third year

**CRITICAL: The oral promises (NOT in the written text):**

The treaty commissioners made numerous oral promises that were never recorded in the written document:
- **No geographic restrictions on hunting**: Chiefs were told their hunting rights would continue throughout their territory, not merely "subject to regulation"
- **Reserves would be chosen by the Indians**: The written text was never explained to the signatories; they were told they would have reserves of adequate size
- **No taking-up of land without consent**: Chiefs understood that land could not be taken from them without consultation
- **Continued way of life**: The oral assurances promised that "the same means of earning a livelihood would continue after the treaty as existed before it"

### The Consideration (What Indigenous Nations Gave)

- **Cession of all rights, titles, and privileges** to the land within the treaty boundaries
- **Agreement to obey Canadian law**
- **Agreement to maintain peace** between tribes and with settlers
- **Agreement not to molest or interfere** with settlers passing through the territory
- **Agreement to assist officers** in bringing to justice Indians who offended against the treaty

### The Crown's Failures

**1. Oral promises vs. written text**: The treaty commissioners deliberately did not explain the written treaty terms to Indigenous signatories. The "taking-up clause" — which allowed the Crown to appropriate land for settlement, mining, lumbering, and other purposes — was never translated or explained. As the Treaty 9 Diaries research documents: "The treaty commissioners came to these meetings with a pre-made written Treaty document. This document was only written in English, which the First Nations representatives could not read."

**2. Inadequate reserves**: Many bands never received the full one-square-mile-per-family-of-five allocation promised in oral negotiations. English River and Canoe Lake First Nations were particularly shortchanged.

**3. Resource extraction without consent**: Ontario has unilaterally permitted mining, logging, and hydroelectric development across Treaty 9 territory without meaningful consultation with First Nations. The Ring of Fire mining development in the James Bay lowlands is the most recent flashpoint.

**4. Failure to provide education**: Schools were promised but many communities went decades without access.

**5. Failure to provide medical care**: The treaty doctor's reports documented severe health crises in the territory.

**6. Flooding of traditional lands**: Hydroelectric dams flooded vast areas of traditional hunting, fishing, and trapping lands, devastating communities like Mishkeegogamang.

### Current Status

**ACTIVE — MAJOR LITIGATION ONGOING**

- **2023**: 10 Treaty 9 First Nations (including Neskantaga, Attawapiskat, Fort Albany, Kashechewan) filed a lawsuit against Ontario and Canada challenging Crown jurisdiction over resource extraction in Treaty 9 territory. They seek a declaration that First Nations retain decision-making governance over lands and resources.

- **2025**: Ontario government filed a motion to strike (dismiss) the Treaty 9 lawsuit. The motion was heard in December 2025.

- **"Breathing Lands" challenge**: A coalition of Treaty 9 nations is seeking to enshrine co-jurisdiction over Treaty 9 territory into law. This case has the potential to change treaty interpretation across Canada.

- **The Narwhal (2023)**: "10 First Nations sue Ontario and Canada over resource extraction and broken Treaty 9 promises"

## TREATY 10 — THE NORTHERN SASKATCHEWAN TREATY (1906–1907)

### The Text

**Full text**: https://www.rcaanc-cirnac.gc.ca/eng/1100100028874/1581292941464
**PDF**: https://otc.ca/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Treaty10.pdf

### The Parties

**Crown Commissioner**:
- James Andrew Joseph McKenna (Winnipeg) — also involved in Treaty 8

**Second Commissioner (1907 adhesions)**:
- Thomas Alexander Borthwick (Indian Agent, Mistawasis)

**Indigenous Signatories** (1906):
- William Apisis, Chief (English River Band — Chipewyans)
- Joseph Gun, Headman (English River)
- Jean Baptiste Estral-Shenen, Headman (English River)
- Raphael Bedshi, Chief (Clear Lake Band — Chipewyans)
- John Iron, Chief (Canoe Lake Band — Cree)
- Baptiste Iron, Headman (Canoe Lake)
- Jerome Couillonneur, Headman (Canoe Lake)

**1907 Adhesions signatories**:
- Petit Casimir, Chief (Barren Land Band)
- Jean Baptiste, Headman (Barren Land)
- Andre Antsanen (Barren Land)
- Thomas Benaouni, Chief (Hatchet Lake Band)
- Pierre Aze, Headman (Hatchet Lake)

**Total paid**: 394 Indians in 1906 ($4,828); additional payments in 1907

### The Date

- **Signed**: August 28, 1906 (Île-à-la-Crosse); September 19, 1906 (Canoe Lake)
- **1907 adhesions**: August 19, 1907 (Lac du Brochet); August 22, 1907 (Hatchet Lake)
- **Ratified**: Order in Council P.C. No. 2490, November 7, 1907

### The Territory

**Nearly 220,000 km²** of:
- **Northern Saskatchewan** (the vast majority)
- **Small portion of eastern Alberta**

Covers territory:
- East of Treaty 8
- North of Treaties 5 and 6 and the addition to Treaty 6
- South of the 60th parallel

**Bands covered**:
- English River (Chipewyan)
- Clear Lake (Chipewyan)
- Canoe Lake (Cree)
- Barren Lands (Dene/Chipewyan)
- Hatchet Lake (Dene/Chipewyan)
- Lac la Ronge (Cree)
- Montreal Lake (Cree)
- Stanley (Cree)

### The Promises (Crown's Obligations)

**From the written treaty text:**

1. **Annual annuities in perpetuity**:
   - Chiefs: $25/year
   - Headmen: $15/year
   - Every other Indian: $5/year

2. **One-time gratuity**:
   - Chiefs: $32
   - Headmen: $22
   - Every other Indian: $12

3. **Reserves**: Not to exceed one square mile per family of five; OR land in severalty (160 acres per individual)

4. **Right to hunt, trap, and fish** throughout the territory, subject to regulation and "taking up" for settlement, mining, lumbering, trading

5. **Education**: "Provision as may from time to time be deemed advisable for the education of Indian children"

6. **Agricultural/farming assistance**: "Such assistance as may be found necessary or advisable to aid and assist the Indians in agriculture or stock-raising or other work"

7. **Twine and ammunition**: Annual distribution "as is usually made to Indians similarly situated"

8. **Medals and flags**: Chiefs receive silver medal and flag; headmen receive bronze medal

9. **Clothing**: Chiefs and headmen receive suits every third year

**Métis scrip**: Separate provisions for Métis: $240 scrip redeemable for Dominion Land or 240 acres of Dominion Land

### The Consideration (What Indigenous Nations Gave)

- **Cession of all rights, titles, and privileges** to the land
- **Agreement to obey the law**
- **Agreement to maintain peace** between tribes and with settlers
- **Agreement not to interfere** with persons passing through

### The Crown's Failures

**1. Inadequate reserves**: Neither Canoe Lake nor English River First Nations received sufficient reserve land. This eventually led to the **Treaty Land Entitlement Framework Agreement (1992)**, under which:
   - English River received over $10 million (used partly to purchase an urban reserve near Saskatoon)
   - Canoe Lake received settlement for 3,000 acres of land never received

**2. Cold Lake Air Weapons Range**: Land was taken from Canoe Lake First Nation for a Cold War-era Royal Canadian Air Force facility that significantly affected the community.

**3. Failure to provide agricultural benefits**: Canada failed to provide ploughs, seeds, livestock, and other farming necessities promised in the treaty. In October 2024, Canada settled Agricultural Benefits specific claims ("Cows and Plows") with multiple Treaty 10 First Nations.

**4. Medical care**: Despite repeated requests from Indigenous leaders for medical assistance, the Crown provided virtually nothing. Commissioner McKenna acknowledged the impossibility of providing a resident doctor due to the vast territory, but even promised medicines were not consistently delivered.

**5. No schools**: Chief John Iron of Canoe Lake specifically requested a day school for 25 children of school age. Commissioner McKenna deflected without commitment.

**6. Threats to traditional livelihoods**: White trappers increasingly encroached on traditional territory, depleting fur-bearing animals. The Crown imposed game laws that further restricted Indigenous harvesting.

### Current Status

**ACTIVE — SPECIFIC CLAIMS SETTLED, BROADER ISSUES ONGOING**

- **2024**: Canada settled Agricultural Benefits specific claims with Treaty 10 First Nations including Alexis Nakota Sioux Nation, Buffalo River Dene Nation, Cumberland House Cree Nation, and others (settlements worth nearly $1 billion total across Treaties 5, 6, and 10).

- **Athabasca Denesuline** have been involved in a long-standing dispute with governments of Canada, NWT, and Nunavut over land use north of the 60th parallel.

- **Treaty Land Entitlement**: English River and Canoe Lake received compensation for inadequate reserves, but the underlying issues of resource sharing and governance remain.

**Crown Commissioner**:
- Henry Anthony Conroy (Ottawa) — died April 27, 1922, before completing all signings

**Key figure**:
- **Bishop Gabriel-Joseph-Elie Breynat, O.M.I.** (Apostolic Vicariate of Mackenzie) — accompanied the treaty party; served as moral authority and interpreter; later claimed oral promises were made that were never recorded

### The Date

- **Liard adhesion**: July 17, 1922
- **Ratified**: Order in Council P.C. 3985, October 22, 1921
- **Liard adhesion ratified**: March 29, 1923

### The Territory

**Approximately 372,000 square miles** (over 950,000 km²) covering:
- **Northwest Territories** (the vast majority)
- **Parts of Yukon**
- **Parts of northern Alberta**
- **Parts of northern British Columbia**
- **Parts of what is now Nunavut**

From the treaty text:
> "Commencing at the northwesterly corner of the territory ceded under the provisions of Treaty Number Eight; thence northeasterly along the height-of-land to the point where it intersects the boundary between the Yukon Territory and the Northwest Territories; thence northwesterly along the said boundary to the shore of the Arctic ocean; thence easterly along the said shore to the mouth of the Coppermine river..."

**This is the largest of all Numbered Treaties.**

### The Promises (Crown's Obligations)

**From the written treaty text:**

1. **Annual annuities in perpetuity**:
   - Chiefs: $25/year
   - Headmen: $15/year
   - Every other Indian: $5/year (paid only to heads of families)

2. **One-time gratuity**:
   - Chiefs: $32
   - Headmen: $22
   - Every other Indian: $12

3. **Reserves**: One square mile per family of five (or proportionate)

4. **Right to hunt, trap, and fish** throughout the territory, subject to regulation and "taking up"

5. **Education**: "Pay the salaries of teachers to instruct the children of said Indians in such manner as His Majesty's Government may deem advisable"

6. **Tools per band**: 10 axes, 5 hand-saws, 5 augers, 1 grindstone, files and whetstones

7. **Hunting/trapping/fishing equipment**: $50 per family (one-time)

8. **Annual supplies**: $3 per head for twine, ammunition, and equipment for those continuing to hunt, fish, and trap

9. **Agricultural assistance**: "In the event of any of the Indians aforesaid being desirous of following agricultural pursuits"

10. **Medals and flags**: Chiefs receive silver medal, flag, and copy of treaty

11. **Clothing**: Chiefs and headmen receive suits every third year

**CRITICAL: The oral promises (NOT in the written text):**

Chief Monfwi of the Tłı̨chǫ (Fort Rae) delivered what became the most famous oral statement associated with any Numbered Treaty:

> **"As long as the sun rises, the river flows, and the land does not move, we will not be restricted from our way of life."**

According to Tłı̨chǫ oral history, Chief Monfwi:
- Drew boundaries on paper circumscribing the territory where his people's freedoms would never be restricted
- Received a personal guarantee from Bishop Breynat that this would be upheld
- Breynat signed his name to the map
- Monfwi kept copies of both the map and the treaty
- **Both items have since disappeared**

Bishop Breynat later maintained that he and Commissioner Conroy had made oral promises regarding the rights of signatories to pursue their traditional way of life, and had expected those would be reflected in the final written version. They were not.

### The Consideration (What Indigenous Nations Gave)

- **Cession of all rights, titles, and privileges** to the land
- **Agreement to obey the law**
- **Agreement to maintain peace** between tribes and with settlers
- **Agreement not to interfere** with persons passing through

### The Crown's Failures

**1. Oil was the real motivation**: The Crown had refused to negotiate treaties with northern Indigenous peoples for decades. The government's chief accountant noted in 1910 that "there are no funds available" for treaty negotiations. It was only after **Imperial Oil struck oil at Fort Norman on August 23, 1920** that the government suddenly found urgency. As Conroy wrote: "Indian title has not yet been extinguished... it would be desirable to take a surrender... as soon as possible in order to avoid complications with respect to the exploitation of the country for oil."

**2. No outside promises allowed**: Commissioner Conroy was explicitly instructed: "You should be guided by the terms set forth therein and…no outside promises should be made by you to the Indians." This was a take-it-or-leave-it offer, not a negotiation.

**3. Inadequate explanation of terms**: The treaty was only in English. The translator at Fort Providence, Victor Lafferty, later claimed he did not "read and translate any piece of paper or writing." Many signatories were illiterate. The concept of land surrender was completely foreign to the Dene understanding of their relationship to the land.

**4. Hasty negotiations**: At Fort Simpson, Chief Johnny Norwegian reportedly did not want to sign but was replaced by Chief Antoine during a lunch break. At Fort Wrigley, Julian Yendo was made chief in a confusing process. At Fort Rae, Chief Monfwi's conditions were agreed to orally but never recorded.

**5. Conroy's death**: Commissioner Conroy died on April 27, 1922, without leaving any record of the oral promises he and Breynat had made. This left Indigenous signatories with no documentary evidence of their understanding of the treaty.

**6. No reserves established**: The Crown failed to establish most reserves promised under the treaty.

**7. No medical care**: Medical treatment remained largely inaccessible. A flu epidemic, along with tuberculosis and pneumonia, devastated Northern communities in the following years.

**8. No schools**: Mission schools remained the only option for education for two decades. The government agreed to "pay the salaries of teachers" but no schools were built.

**9. Encroachment by white trappers**: First Nations continued to face increasing competition from white trappers who depleted fur-bearing animal populations, making further game regulations necessary — which further restricted Indigenous harvesting.

**10. Mineral rights taken without understanding**: The 1959 Nelson Commission found that Indigenous concepts of land ownership had changed very little and that "it is impossible to make the Indians understand that it is possible to separate mineral rights from actual ownership of land."

### Current Status

**ACTIVE — MODERN TREATY PROCESS ONGOING**

Treaty 11 is unique among the Numbered Treaties because it has been substantially superseded by modern comprehensive land claims agreements:

- **1973**: Justice William Morrow of the NWT Supreme Court ruled that the Indian Brotherhood (now Dene Nation) could file a claim covering more than 1 million km², based on elder testimony that the treaty was understood as a peace and friendship agreement, not land surrender.

- **Gwich'in Comprehensive Land Claim Agreement (1992)**: Settled Gwich'in claims in western NWT

- **Tłı̨chǫ Agreement (2003)**: The first combined land claim and self-government agreement in the NWT, covering approximately 39,000 km² with governance, land management, and resource revenue sharing

- **Sahtu Dene and Métis Comprehensive Land Claim Agreement (1994)**: Covers the Sahtu region

- **Délı̨nę Self-Government Agreement (2016)**: Community-level self-government

- **Mikisew Cree First Nation** (Treaty 8 signatory but territory overlaps with Treaty 11): Has filed multiple lawsuits against Alberta and Canada over industrial development impacts, including a 2026 lawsuit alleging failure to protect members from health issues caused by oilsands development

- **Dene Nation** continues to challenge the extinguishment component of Treaty 11

## CROSS-CUTTING THEMES: THE NEGLIGENCE OF THE CROWN

### Pattern 1: Refusal Until Economic Interest

All three treaties share the same pattern: Indigenous nations repeatedly petitioned for treaties; the Crown refused until resource extraction made treaty-making economically necessary.

| Treaty | First request for treaty | Crown response | What changed |
|--------|------------------------|----------------|--------------|
| Treaty 9 | Chief Louis Espagnol, 1884 ("All of my old people who used to hunt near here are in great need") | Ignored for 20 years | Mining, railway construction |
| Treaty 10 | Lac La Ronge, Pelican Narrows, Stanley, 1879 | Refused | Creation of provinces (1905), Métis scrip claims |
| Treaty 11 | Northern peoples petitioned repeatedly from 1900s | "No funds available" | **Oil discovery at Fort Norman, 1920** |

### Pattern 2: Written Text vs. Oral Promises

In all three treaties, the Crown prepared written texts in advance and presented them without full translation or explanation:

- **Treaty 9**: "We carefully guarded against making any promises over and above those written in the treaty" — Treaty Commissioner's Report (while simultaneously making oral promises)
- **Treaty 10**: Commissioner McKenna "guaranteed that the treaty would not lead to any forced interference with their mode of life" — not in the written text
- **Treaty 11**: Commissioner Conroy was instructed to make "no outside promises" — yet oral guarantees were given and later lost

### Pattern 3: Taking-Up Clause

All three treaties contain a "taking-up clause" allowing the Crown to appropriate land for settlement, mining, lumbering, and other purposes. This clause was:
- Never explained to Indigenous signatories
- In direct contradiction to oral promises of continued hunting rights
- Used to justify virtually unlimited resource extraction

The Supreme Court of Canada has ruled (in *Mikisew Cree First Nation v. Canada*, 2005 SCC 69) that the taking-up power does not eliminate the duty to consult when the taking-up adversely affects treaty rights.

### Pattern 4: Inadequate Reserves and Benefits

- Treaty 9: Many bands never received the promised one-square-mile-per-family allocation
- Treaty 10: Canoe Lake and English River received inadequate reserves (partially addressed through Treaty Land Entitlement in 1992)
- Treaty 11: Most reserves were never established; the 1959 Nelson Commission documented widespread failure

### Pattern 5: No Medical Care, No Schools

All three treaties promised education and (implicitly) welfare. In practice:
- Schools were not built for decades
- Medical care was virtually nonexistent
- Epidemics devastated communities (Treaty 11 territory particularly affected by flu, TB, pneumonia in 1920s)

## KEY LEGAL PRINCIPLES

### Oral Promises Are Binding

The Supreme Court of Canada has established that oral promises made during treaty negotiations are legally binding as "oral terms" of the treaty:

- **R. v. Badger (1996)**: "The treaties, as written documents, recorded an agreement that had already been reached orally and they did not always record the full extent of the oral agreement."
- **R. v. Marshall (1999)**: "The oral promises made when the treaty was agreed to are as much a part of the treaty as the written words."
- **R. v. Ermineskin (2009)**: It would be "unconscionable for the Crown to ignore oral terms and rely simply on the written words of a treaty."
- **Guerin v. The Queen (1984)**: The Crown is bound by oral terms even where the Governor in Council was not made aware of them.

### The Honour of the Crown

The Crown must act honourably in all dealings with Indigenous peoples. This principle:
- Requires the Crown to fulfil its treaty promises
- Requires consultation when treaty rights may be adversely affected
- Cannot be avoided by pointing to the written text alone

### Treaty Interpretation

Treaties must be interpreted:
- Liberally, in favour of Indigenous signatories
- As the words would "naturally have been understood by the Indians"
- Not subjected to "rigid modern rules of construction"
- In a way that "best reconciles" the interests of both parties
