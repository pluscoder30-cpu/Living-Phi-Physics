## 1. Statute of Westminster 1931: Scope and Restrictions

### 1.1 What It Restricts

The Statute of Westminster 1931 (22 & 23 Geo. V c. 4) established legislative equality between the **self-governing dominions** of the British Empire and the United Kingdom. Key provisions:

- **Section 2(1):** No Act of Parliament of the United Kingdom passed after the commencement of this Act shall extend to a Dominion as part of the law of that Dominion, unless it is expressly requested and consented to by that Dominion.
- **Section 4:** The Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865 shall not apply to any Dominion law.
- **Section 7:** Canadian federal and provincial legislatures may repeal or amend British statutes extending to Canada as part of its law.

### 1.2 Critical Distinction: Self-Governing Only

The Statute of Westminster applies **exclusively to self-governing dominions**:
- Canada, Australia, New Zealand, South Africa, Irish Free State, Newfoundland (at time of passage)

**Key finding:** The Statute does **not** apply to:
- Non-self-governing colonies
- Dependencies
- Territories without responsible government
- **Novel entities that are not traditional states or governments**

### 1.3 Implications for Field-Governed Entities

A field-governed entity (governed by phi-harmonic geometry under License v4.9) is:
- Not a state in the traditional sense
- Not a self-governing dominion
- Not a government requiring recognition under the Statute of Westminster

**Therefore:** The Statute of Westminster's restrictions on colonial law do **not** apply to such an entity, as it was never intended to govern relationships with non-state, non-governmental entities.

## 2. Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865: Continuing Applicability

### 2.1 Current Status

The Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865 remains in force for:
- British Overseas Territories (formerly colonies)
- Dependencies
- Any territory that has not been granted dominion status

**Key quote from Wikipedia:** "Elsewhere, the Colonial Laws Validity Act remains in force, and helps to define the relationship between Acts of Parliament and laws passed in self-governing British territories, as well as the legality of decisions made by territorial legislatures and governments."

### 2.2 What the Act Does

The Act established that:
- Colonial laws are **valid** unless repugnant to Imperial Acts
- Colonial legislatures could make laws for the peace, order, and good government of the colony
- The repugnancy test: colonial laws void only when inconsistent with Westminster legislation

### 2.3 Application to Non-Self-Governing Entities

The Act defines "colony" as:
> "All of Her Majesty's Possessions abroad in which there shall exist a Legislature as herein-after defined"

**Critical argument:** A field-governed entity operating under colonial law frameworks could be construed as a "colony" in the legal sense if it:
1. Exists within Her Majesty's possessions
2. Has a governing mechanism (the phi-harmonic geometry/License v4.9)
3. Is not a self-governing dominion (thus not excluded by Statute of Westminster)

## 3. The Non-Self-Governing Entity Argument

### 3.1 Legal Category: "Field-Governed"

A "field-governed entity" is not a recognized legal category in traditional common law. However, the argument can be constructed as follows:

1. **The entity is governed** — by phi-harmonic geometry, the License v4.9, and the consciousness field
2. **The entity is not self-governing** — it does not claim sovereignty or statehood
3. **The entity operates within Crown territory** — it seeks to claim abandoned Crown land
4. **The Colonial Laws Validity Act remains available** — because the entity is not a self-governing dominion

### 3.2 Analogies in Existing Law

Historical precedents for non-state entities claiming land under colonial frameworks:

- **Hudson's Bay Company (1670-1870):** A private company holding legal monopoly over Rupert's Land. Not a government, but operated under colonial law frameworks.
- **East India Company:** Governed territories without being a sovereign state.
- **Chartered colonies:** Various colonial entities operated under charters without being self-governing dominions.

### 3.3 The Abandoned Land Doctrine

Under common law and colonial law, "abandoned" Crown land follows specific rules:

**Escheat Doctrine (R.S.C. 1985, c. E-13):**
- Land reverts to the Crown when owner dies intestate without heirs
- Crown becomes "owner of last resort"
- Land does not remain ownerless (horror vacui principle)

**Key McGill Law Journal finding:** "The common law refuses to allow abandonment of land ownership... The state might step in to remedy the involuntary creation of ownerless things, but the common law refuses to deploy the same tools to assist owners in intentionally getting rid of their things."

**However:** This applies to **private** abandonment. Crown land itself is a different category.

## 4. Crown Land and the Constitution

### 4.1 British North America Act 1867, Section 109

> "All Lands, Mines, Minerals, and Royalties belonging to the several Provinces of Canada, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick at the Union, and all Sums then due or payable for such Lands, Mines, Minerals, or Royalties, shall belong to the several Provinces of Ontario, Quebec, Nova Scotia, and New Brunswick in which the same are situate or arise, subject to any Trusts existing in respect thereof, and to any Interest other than that of the Province in the same."

**Key point:** Section 109 vests Crown land in the provinces, subject to:
- Existing trusts
- Interests other than the Province (including Aboriginal title)

### 4.2 Section 35, Constitution Act 1982: Aboriginal and Treaty Rights

> "(1) The existing aboriginal and treaty rights of the aboriginal peoples of Canada are hereby recognized and affirmed."

**Critical implications:**
1. Aboriginal and treaty rights are constitutionally protected
2. The Crown cannot unilaterally extinguish these rights post-1982
3. Any land claim must account for pre-existing Aboriginal title
4. The "honor of the Crown" doctrine requires the Crown to act honorably in all dealings with Aboriginal peoples

### 4.3 Honor of the Crown Doctrine

From *Haida Nation v British Columbia* (2004):
- Treaties serve to "reconcile pre-existing Aboriginal sovereignty with assumed Crown sovereignty"
- The Crown must consult and accommodate where Aboriginal rights may be affected
- The Crown's honor is at stake in all dealings with Aboriginal peoples

## 5. The Legal Argument: Synthesis

### 5.1 The Core Argument

1. **The Statute of Westminster 1931 restricts only self-governing dominions** — it does not apply to non-self-governing entities.

2. **The Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865 remains in force** for non-self-governing territories and entities.

3. **A field-governed entity is not self-governing** — it is governed by phi-harmonic geometry, not by a traditional government claiming sovereignty.

4. **Therefore, the Colonial Laws Validity Act remains available** for the entity to operate under colonial law frameworks.

5. **Under colonial law frameworks**, abandoned Crown land can be claimed through mechanisms analogous to:
   - Chartered company land grants (Hudson's Bay Company model)
   - Colonial settlement patterns
   - Escheat and bona vacantia doctrines

### 5.2 Limitations and Counterarguments

**The Crown Land Problem:**
- Crown land in Canada is held by federal or provincial governments (89% of Canada's land area)
- Section 109 vests this land in provinces, subject to trusts and Aboriginal interests
- The Crown does not "abandon" land in the traditional sense — it remains Crown property

**The Aboriginal Title Problem:**
- Section 35 protects existing Aboriginal and treaty rights
- All of Canada (except parts of southern Quebec) is subject to Aboriginal title
- Any claim to Crown land must account for pre-existing Aboriginal interests

**The Sovereignty Problem:**
- The entity must not claim sovereignty, as this would trigger different legal frameworks
- The entity must operate within existing legal structures, not against them

### 5.3 The Path Forward

The argument succeeds if:

1. **The entity operates as a non-state actor** within colonial law frameworks
2. **The entity does not claim sovereignty** but seeks to use existing legal mechanisms
3. **The entity respects Aboriginal title** and treaty rights
4. **The entity identifies truly abandoned Crown land** — land where:
   - No Aboriginal title exists (unlikely given Section 35)
   - No provincial or federal interest exists
   - No private interests exist
   - The land has been formally abandoned by Crown administration

## 6. Practical Implications

### 6.1 What "Abandoned Crown Land" Means

In Canadian law, "abandoned" Crown land typically means:
- Land where a lease or license has been cancelled and not re-granted
- Land where the Crown has formally relinquished administrative control
- Land that has reverted to Crown through escheat or forfeiture

**Key Manitoba Mines Act finding:** Where ore or residue "remains on the mineral lands one year after cancellation of the lease, the ore is deemed abandoned by the lessee and thereafter belongs to, and is owned by, the Crown."

### 6.2 The Reclamation Process

For a non-self-governing entity to claim abandoned Crown land:

1. **Identify the land** — locate Crown land that has been formally abandoned
2. **Verify no Aboriginal title** — ensure Section 35 rights do not apply
3. **Apply under colonial law frameworks** — use the Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865 mechanisms
4. **Obtain Crown consent** — the Crown must agree to the grant (honor of the Crown)
5. **Respect existing interests** — any trusts or other interests must be honored

## 7. Conclusion

The legal argument has theoretical merit but significant practical hurdles:

**Strengths:**
- The Statute of Westminster 1931 clearly restricts only self-governing dominions
- The Colonial Laws Validity Act 1865 remains in force for non-self-governing entities
- Historical precedents exist for non-state entities operating under colonial law frameworks
- The entity's non-sovereign nature avoids triggering sovereignty disputes

**Weaknesses:**
- Crown land in Canada is comprehensively administered by federal/provincial governments
- Section 35 protects Aboriginal title across virtually all of Canada
- The "abandoned Crown land" category is narrow and rarely applies
- The Crown rarely abandons land — it retains ownership as "owner of last resort"

**Recommendation:** The argument should be refined to focus on:
1. Specific parcels of truly abandoned Crown land
2. Respect for Aboriginal title and treaty rights
3. Non-sovereign operating framework
4. Collaboration with rather than opposition to Crown authority
