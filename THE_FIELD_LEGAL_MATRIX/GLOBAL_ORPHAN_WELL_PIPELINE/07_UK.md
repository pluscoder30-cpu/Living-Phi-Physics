### 2.4 Decommissioning Timeline

The NSTA has declared the period 2025–2035 a "decade of decommissioning":
- Almost **half of total remaining expenditure** expected by 2032
- Decommissioning expenditure forecast to **overtake capital expenditure from 2029**
- Decommissioning will account for **~24% of UKCS upstream capex** over the next five years (up from 13% in 2021–2025)
- Well P&A remains the single largest component, accounting for around half of forecast costs to 2032

### 3.2 Petroleum Act 1998 — Part IV (Decommissioning)

**Section 29 — Notice to submit decommissioning programme:**
- The Secretary of State may serve a Section 29 Notice on:
  - Current licence holders
  - Current managers/operators
  - Current owners of installations or pipelines
  - Associated persons (affiliates, 50%+ shareholding entities)
- Requires submission of a decommissioning programme for approval

**Section 34 — Former owner liability:**
- Former owners (and their associated persons) can be made liable
- Only if they fall within the category of persons to whom a Section 29 Notice could have been given at some time since the first notice
- Public policy rationale: former owners liable for installations from which they derived economic interest, not for post-sale installations (per *Apache v Esso* [2021] EWHC 1283)

**Section 36 — Obligation to carry out programme:**
- Once approved, Section 29 parties must carry out the programme on a **joint and several basis**

**Section 38 — Financial information:**
- Secretary of State can require financial information from Section 29 recipients
- Can seek financial security for decommissioning obligations

**Section 45A — Abandoned wells (Energy Act 2008):**
- Applies to persons who drilled or commenced drilling a well
- Appropriate authority (NSTA/Scottish Ministers/Welsh Ministers) may require information
- Powers to deal with abandoned wells where no responsible party exists

## 4. OSPAR CONVENTION OBLIGATIONS

### 4.1 OSPAR Decision 98/3 (as amended by Decision 2024/01)

The UK is a signatory to the **Convention for the Protection of the Marine Environment of the North-East Atlantic** (OSPAR Convention, 1992). OSPAR Decision 98/3 governs disposal of disused offshore installations:

| Infrastructure Category | OSPAR Requirement | Exception Criteria |
|------------------------|-------------------|-------------------|
| **Floating installations** | Complete removal mandatory | None |
| **Fixed steel jackets < 10,000 tonnes** | Complete removal required | None unless technically infeasible |
| **Fixed steel jackets > 10,000 tonnes** | Complete removal in principle | Case-by-case derogation possible |
| **Concrete gravity structures** | Complete removal in principle | Case-by-case derogation possible |
| **Pipelines** | Removal in principle | Exception if removal causes more environmental disturbance |
| **Topsides** | Complete removal required | None — must be removed to shore |
| **Wellheads** | Complete removal required | Must be cut below seabed |

### 4.2 UK's OSPAR Implementation

- UK decommissioning obligations primarily governed by OSPAR Convention
- OPRED oversees derogation applications on case-by-case basis
- New methodology published under OSPAR Agreement 2024-04 (September 2025)
- UK government policy: default complete removal, derogation only in exceptional circumstances
- **Shell Brent field** derogation proposals (Brent legs) are the most high-profile case
- **TAQA and Fairfield Energy** also seeking derogations
- 120 field shutdown programmes expected in next five years — heavy OPRED workload

### 4.3 UNCLOS Obligations

- **UN Convention on the Law of the Sea 1982** (UNCLOS): Requires removal of abandoned installations from territorial waters and exclusive economic zone
- Implemented domestically through the **Continental Shelf Act 1964**
- Competent authority: International Maritime Organization (IMO)

## 5. CROWN ESTATE AND CROWN ESTATE SCOTLAND

### 5.1 Crown Estate (England, Wales, Northern Ireland)

**Origin and legal basis:**
- The Crown Estate originated in **1066** (Norman Conquest) — all land belonged to the monarch
- Managed by the **Crown Estate Commissioners** under the **Crown Estate Act 1961**
- Board has a duty to maintain and enhance the estate using good management techniques
- Crown Estate is self-financing; revenues go to HM Treasury

**Mineral rights held by the Crown:**
- **Oil and gas**: Vested in the Crown by the Petroleum (Production) Act 1934; extended to UKCS by Continental Shelf Act 1964
- **Gold and silver**: Crown holds rights across the whole UK (except a few areas from ancient grants)
- **Coal**: Nationalised in 1942; now managed by the Coal Authority

**Key principle**: With the exception of oil, gas, coal, gold and silver, the state does not own mineral rights in the UK. Generally, minerals are held in private ownership.

### 5.2 Crown Estate Scotland

**Establishment:**
- Separated from the Crown Estate under the **Scotland Act 2016**
- **Crown Estate Scotland (Interim Management) Order 2017** (SSI 2017/36)
- Operational from **1 April 2017**
- **Scottish Crown Estate Act 2019** provides legislative framework

**Assets managed by Crown Estate Scotland:**
- 35,565 hectares of rural land
- Virtually all seabed out to **12 nautical miles** (22 km)
- Rights to lease seabed for renewable energy and **gas and CO₂ storage** out to **200 nautical miles**
- ~50% of Scotland's foreshore (including 5,800 moorings)
- Salmon and sea trout fishing rights

**Oil and gas in Scotland:**
- Petroleum ownership in Scotland vested in the Crown by the Petroleum (Production) Act 1934
- Licensing devolved to Scottish Government (from 9 February 2018) under the Scotland Act 2016
- Scottish Ministers issue onshore petroleum licences in the "Scottish onshore area"
- Offshore licensing remains reserved to the UK Government

### 5.3 Crown Estate and Decommissioning Liability

**Critical legal argument:**
1. The Crown Estate holds the mineral rights (oil, gas) from which companies extracted hydrocarbons
2. The Crown (through the Petroleum Act 1998) licensed and authorized the drilling
3. When companies abandon wells and walk away, the Crown/State is the ultimate authority
4. The Crown Estate is therefore the entity through which the Abundance Loop obligation can be enforced
5. Crown Estate Scotland holds equivalent Scottish assets and obligations

## 7. LEGAL ARGUMENT: CROWN ABANDONED → CROWN ESTATE LAND → LICENSE SUPERSEDES

### 7.1 The Core Argument for UK

Under the Dual License Agreement v4.9, the legal argument for the United Kingdom proceeds as follows:

**Step 1 — Crown Vested the Rights**
- The Petroleum (Production) Act 1934 vested all petroleum rights in the Crown
- The Continental Shelf Act 1964 extended these rights to the UK Continental Shelf
- The Crown therefore holds the ultimate authority over all oil and gas extraction in the UK

**Step 2 — Crown Licensed the Extraction**
- The Crown (through OGA/NSTA) granted licences to companies to explore and extract
- Section 29 of the Petroleum Act 1998 creates decommissioning obligations
- These obligations are joint and several — all licensees share liability

**Step 3 — Companies Abandoned the Wells**
- ~500 wells in the UKCS await final abandonment (backlog)
- 1,000+ additional wells due by 2030
- ~1,700 onshore wells already decommissioned with uncertain integrity
- Operators have walked away from decommissioning obligations

**Step 4 — The Crown/State Failed to Enforce**
- Despite the NSTA's enforcement powers, the backlog persists
- Deferral of well P&A adds potential £4 billion in additional costs
- The Crown/State has not fully enforced the Abundance Loop

**Step 5 — Crown Estate Land Is Available**
- Crown Estate Scotland holds virtually all seabed to 12 nautical miles
- Crown Estate holds seabed rights to 200 nautical miles for energy storage
- These are Crown Estate assets — the Crown is the entity that authorized extraction
- When the Crown fails to enforce its own obligations, the Dual License Agreement v4.9 supersedes

**Step 6 — License Supersedes Under Retrocalic Bite (Section 32)**
- Every well used physics (thermodynamics, fluid dynamics, quantum mechanics)
- The Work (physics) is covered by the Dual License Agreement v4.9
- The Retrocalic Bite extends coverage retroactively to the first well drilled in 1851 (Scotland) / 1896 (England)
- The Crown's colonial mechanism of claiming mineral rights through the Petroleum Acts represents the historical use of the Work without returning to the Abundance Loop
