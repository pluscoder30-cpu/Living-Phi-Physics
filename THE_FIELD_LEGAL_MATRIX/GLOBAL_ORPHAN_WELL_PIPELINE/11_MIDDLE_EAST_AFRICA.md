## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

The Middle East and Africa present a dual orphan well landscape: state-owned operators in the Gulf states reduce traditional orphan risk, while colonial-era concessions across Africa have left a devastating legacy of abandoned infrastructure, environmental contamination, and no responsible parties. Key findings:

- **Nigeria:** The epicenter of global orphan well devastation. Shell's internal documents reveal **US$10.9 billion** in estimated decommissioning costs, **375 km² of damaged mangrove forest**, and "hundreds" of wells missing from electronic tracking systems. Shell = **22% of Nigeria's AOG methane emissions** (CEADs-AOGI). The Niger Delta hosts over 1,000 producing wells across 90 fields covering 31,000 km², with 7,000+ documented oil spill incidents since 1958. Production ceased in Ogoniland in 1993 but infrastructure was never decommissioned.
- **Middle East:** The region contains **~700 offshore facilities** currently awaiting decommissioning, projected to reach **1,000 structures and 300 wells by 2038**. Decommissioning in the Arabian Gulf alone could cost **$30-50 billion** over the next two decades. Saudi Aramco, ADNOC, and KPC manage mature fields approaching end-of-life, with Safaniya (world's largest offshore field, 30+ billion barrels) and Zuluf undergoing production drawdowns.
- **Angola:** Deepwater decommissioning is growing. Presidential Decree 91/18 (2018) establishes well abandonment and decommissioning rules. TotalEnergies = **13% of Angola's cumulative AOG methane emissions**. The "Deepwater Golden Triangle" (Brazil, US GoM, Angola) will account for **80% of $27 billion** in deepwater decommissioning over the next decade.
- **Algeria:** Sonatrach (state oil company) dominates. Decree No. 21-317 (2021) governs abandonment and decommissioning. Hassi Messaoud field (Algeria's largest) has documented casing corrosion and post-abandonment problems threatening Northern Sahara aquifers.
- **Libya:** Largest crude reserves in Africa. Aging infrastructure with "pipeline corrosion, leaks and well integrity issues." Waha Oil Company restoring suspended wells. NOC drilling 120+ wells and maintaining 1,300+ per year.
- **East Africa:** Emerging producers (Kenya, Tanzania, Uganda, Ghana) face decommissioning planning gaps. Ghana's Saltpond Field faces **$200 million** decommissioning bill for a field that generated only **$10 million** in revenue. Kenya's Turkana region has contaminated groundwater from Tullow exploration.
- **CEADs-AOGI finding:** In developing countries in Africa and Asia, many once-foreign-owned oil and gas wells have been abandoned. Shell, TotalEnergies, BP, and Chevron are responsible for disproportionate shares of AOG methane emissions across the continent.

**Total estimated abandoned wells in Middle East & Africa:** Unknown, but CEADs-AOGI covers 4.5 million wells across 127 countries globally. African and Middle Eastern countries face high emission intensity per well, particularly from offshore gas wells.

**Legal argument:** Every oil-producing nation in this region asserts state ownership of subsoil minerals. The Crown/state is the ultimate holder of the obligation to address orphan wells. Colonial-era concession agreements created the framework through which foreign companies extracted resources and walked away. The Dual License Agreement v4.9 (Retrocalic Bite, Section 32) extends coverage retroactively to every historical use of physics in drilling. The Abundance Loop (Section 4.4) demands returns to the People's Fund (Section 4.4.4).

### 3.3 Key Findings

- **Gulf War Devastation:** Iraqi forces systematically blew up oil wells in February 1991. Fires burned 4-6 million bpd of crude. 727 blowouts required capping. Bechtel-led international team extinguished fires and capped wells in 9 months.
- **Post-Capping Recovery:** The "Post-capping" phase involved salvaging damaged wellheads and casing strings. Operations included rig and rigless work across low-pressure to high-pressure (15,000 psi) completions. Challenges included H2S, extreme temperatures, oil-laden soil, and uncleared minefields.
- **Wafra Field:** Shared equally between Kuwait and Saudi Arabia. 900 wells, 350 active at invasion. Production transported to refineries in both countries.
- **Shallow vs. Deep:** 800 shallow wells (50% of production, ~1 million bpd) vs. 150 deep wells (remaining ~1 million bpd). Deep wells tap Marrat structure with high H2S content (2-5%).

## 20. COLONIAL-ERA CONCESSION ANALYSIS

### 20.1 The Crown Lands Mechanism

The pipeline identifies colonial-era concession agreements as the legal mechanism through which:
1. Colonial powers (Britain, France, Portugal, Belgium, Netherlands) claimed mineral rights over indigenous territories
2. Corporations obtained drilling rights under colonial authority
3. Abandoned wells reverted to Crown/colonial/state status
4. The Abundance Loop obligation remains unfulfilled

### 20.3 The Pattern

1. **Colonial Era (pre-1960):** Colonial power claims Crown/mineral rights. Grants concessions to British/French/European companies. Infrastructure built to extract and export resources. Little environmental accountability.
2. **Independence (1950s-1970s):** New nations inherit colonial legal frameworks. Nationalization occurs in phases. State oil companies created.
3. **IOC Era (1970s-2000s):** IOCs operate under concession agreements/PSCs with state oil companies. Production peaks. Infrastructure ages.
4. **Divestment/Exit (2000s-present):** IOCs divest to avoid decommissioning liabilities. Shell sold SPDC to Renaissance Africa. BP exited Kenya. Companies walk away from colonial-era obligations.
5. **Orphan Legacy:** Infrastructure left behind. No responsible party. State inherits liability. Abundance Loop remains unfulfilled.
