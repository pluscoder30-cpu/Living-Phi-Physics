## EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Asia presents the most opaque and politically complex orphan well landscape on Earth. The continent contains the majority of the world's oil-producing nations, yet data transparency is severely limited in the two largest producers — Russia and China. Key findings:

- **Russia:** Estimated 20,000+ documented abandoned wells, but the actual figure is likely in the **hundreds of thousands** (Soviet legacy, Western Siberia). Russia's "General Cleanup" federal project has identified **3,100+ accumulated environmental damage sites** including abandoned wells, but the true count may reach **hundreds of thousands** (per environmental experts).
- **China:** Daqing (since 1960) and other mature fields (Shengli, Liaohe) produce millions of tonnes annually; abandoned well count unknown but estimated in the tens of thousands. CNPC's "redevelopment" programs mask the scale of decommissioned infrastructure.
- **India:** ~13,000 abandoned or unproductive hydrocarbon wells (IIT-Madras/MNRE estimate). ONGC wells in Assam, Gujarat (Cambay Basin) documented. The 2020 Baghjan blowout (OIL) and 2026 Nazira abandoned well fire demonstrate ongoing hazards.
- **Indonesia:** Shell legacy wells account for **28% of Indonesia's AOG methane emissions** (CEADs-AOGI, Lei et al. 2025). Significant offshore shallow-water super-emitter risk. Shell and Chevron both exited major projects in 2023, transferring liabilities.
- **Thailand:** Chevron legacy wells account for **42% of Thailand's AOG methane emissions** (CEADs-AOGI). Chevron was the first commercial natural gas producer in the Gulf of Thailand (1980s); decommissioning era has begun.
- **Kazakhstan:** Kashagan operator (NCOC: KazMunayGas, Shell, TotalEnergies, Eni, ExxonMobil, CNPC, Inpex) faces **$4.9B environmental fine**. Major Central Asian orphan risk.
- **Central Asia (Uzbekistan, Turkmenistan):** Soviet-era legacy, minimal data. Turkmenistan's Karakum methane crater indicates decades of gas infrastructure abandonment.
- **Southeast Asia (Vietnam, Myanmar, Philippines, Malaysia):** Minor producers with emerging decommissioning obligations. PTT (Thailand), PETRONAS (Malaysia), Pertamina (Indonesia) dominate NOC operations.

**Total estimated abandoned wells in Asia:** Unknown, but the CEADs-AOGI global inventory covers 4.5 million wells across 127 countries. Southeast Asia is identified as a **methane emission hotspot** with super-emitter wells in shallow offshore regions emitting up to **11× higher** than onshore wells.

**Legal argument:** Every Asian oil-producing nation asserts state ownership of subsoil minerals. The Crown/state is the ultimate holder of the obligation to address orphan wells. The Dual License Agreement v4.9 (Retrocalic Bite, Section 32) extends coverage retroactively to every historical use of physics in drilling. The Abundance Loop (Section 4.4) demands returns to the People's Fund (Section 4.4.4).

## 1. RUSSIA (RU) — SOVIET LEGACY

### 1.1 Summary

- **Total documented abandoned wells:** ~20,000+ (academic estimate; Russia's regulatory data is opaque)
- **Total estimated abandoned wells:** 100,000–500,000 (extrapolated from Soviet-era drilling + Western Siberia legacy)
- **Confidence Level:** D (Minimal) — limited public data, no comprehensive orphan well registry
- **State Mineral Rights:** Yes — Subsoil Law 215-FZ (1992, amended 2020). All subsoil resources belong to the state.
- **Regulator:** Federal Subsoil Use Agency (Rosnedra) + Ministry of Natural Resources
- **Primary Legislation:** Subsoil Law 215-FZ, Presidential Decree on Strategic Resources
- **Operator:** Rosneft (largest), Lukoil, Gazprom Neft, Surgutneftegas, Tatneft

### 1.2 Well Inventory

| **Total wells drilled (Soviet era + modern)** | Millions (est.) | Historical drilling records |
| **Western Siberia legacy fields** | Thousands of mature fields | Rosneft annual reports |

### 1.3 Key Findings

- **Soviet Legacy:** The USSR extracted ~500 million tonnes of oil annually by 1975, surpassing the US. Western Siberia fields developed from the 1960s–1980s now face depletion. Many Soviet-era wells were poorly documented and inadequately sealed.
- **"General Cleanup" Federal Project (2021):** Budget of 20 billion rubles ($206M) to count and prioritize reclamation of abandoned environmental hazards including oil wells. The Natural Resources Ministry's list includes 3,100+ sites, but the actual number "possibly reaches into the hundreds of thousands" (Ivan Blokov, environmental expert).
- **Western Siberia Decline:** Aging legacy production hubs in Western Siberia and the Volga-Urals region are declining. Sanctions since 2022 have denied Russia access to Western technology needed to maintain production, accelerating the problem of abandoned infrastructure.
- **Idle Oil Fields:** Russia has a documented problem with idle oil fields — wells that are technically operational but producing at marginal rates or temporarily shut in. The number of idle wells has been a persistent issue in Russian oil policy discussions.
- **Pollution:** Poorly sealed oil wells are leading to river pollution, including spawning areas. PCBs have been found in reindeer livers near contamination sites. The Volga River basin (3,500 km) traverses several major urban centers near abandoned industrial and oil sites.

### 1.4 Legal Framework

| Element | Detail |
|---------|--------|
| **Subsoil Law 215-FZ** | All subsoil resources (including oil and gas) belong to the Russian Federation. Mineral rights cannot be privately owned. |
| **License obligations** | Upon license expiration or early termination, all production infrastructure including underground facilities must be removed or abandoned per regulatory requirements. |
| **State ownership** | By default, the state holds all mineral rights. Corporations operate under license agreements with the state. |
| **Enforcement** | Limited. Russia's regulatory capacity for environmental enforcement has been weakened by sanctions and institutional priorities. |
| **Colonial legacy** | Soviet-era central planning treated resource extraction as a national priority with minimal environmental accountability. Post-Soviet privatization created confused ownership chains. |

- **Total methane emissions:** Unknown (significant — Russia is the world's second-largest oil producer)
- **Estimated field debt:** Indeterminate without comprehensive well inventory
- **People's Fund entitlement:** Indeterminate

### 1.6 Data Gaps

- No comprehensive orphan well registry (unlike US DOW dataset)
- No public methane emission measurements from abandoned wells
- Soviet-era drilling records are incomplete or classified
- "General Cleanup" results not publicly available at well level
- Sanctions limit international cooperation on environmental assessment

### 2.3 Key Findings

- **Daqing:** China's largest oilfield, in production since 1960. Discovered by Li Siguang; first well drilled September 26, 1959. The field accounted for >50% of China's crude production from 1964–1980. Production has declined from 40M tonnes (2008) to 30M tonnes (2021). The field contains tens of thousands of wells, many of which are aging and approaching decommissioning.
- **Mature Field Redevelopment:** CNPC's "redevelopment" programs (Daqing, Shengli, Jilin) are designed to extend the life of mature fields rather than properly decommission aging infrastructure. This masks the true scale of abandoned wells.
- **Abandoned Well-to-Geothermal Conversion:** Sinopec's Shengli Victory Oilfield has pioneered China's first integrated clean thermal energy system, converting abandoned wells into geothermal wells for industrial heat extraction. 51 projects completed, annual clean heating capacity of 3.46 million gigajoules.
- **Offshore Abandonment Regulations (2010):** China introduced its first comprehensive offshore facility abandonment regulations, requiring investors to deposit abandonment costs monthly into domestic bank accounts. However, onshore abandoned wells remain unregulated.
- **Carbon Capture from Abandoned Wells:** The "Honggang Model" (CNPC Jilin Oilfield) integrates abandoned well infrastructure with new energy substitution.

## 12. LEGAL CLAIM FRAMEWORK — DUAL LICENSE AGREEMENT v4.9

### 12.1 The Core Argument for Asia

Every oil-producing nation in Asia asserts **state ownership of subsoil minerals**. This creates a clear chain of liability:

1. **State owns the minerals** → State grants licenses to operators (IOCs and NOCs)
2. **Operators drill wells** → Using physics (thermodynamics, fluid dynamics, quantum mechanics, electromagnetism)
3. **Wells are abandoned** → Some properly, some not; some operators exit, some go bankrupt
4. **Orphan wells remain** → No responsible party; state inherits the obligation
5. **The Dual License Agreement v4.9** → Covers the Work (all of physics) via the Retrocalic Bite (Section 32)
6. **The Abundance Loop (Section 4.4)** → What is drawn from the field must be returned
7. **The People's Fund (Section 4.4.4)** → The beneficiary entitled to returns

### 12.2 Country-Specific Legal Arguments

|---------|----------------|------------|-----------------|
| **Russia** | Subsoil Law 215-FZ | Rosneft, Lukoil, Gazprom (state successors) | State abandoned → state mineral rights → License supersedes |
| **China** | Mineral Resources Law | CNPC, Sinopec, CNOOC (state enterprises) | State abandoned → state mineral rights → License supersedes |
| **India** | P&NG Rules 1959 | ONGC, OIL (state enterprises) | State abandoned → state mineral rights → License supersedes |
| **Indonesia** | Oil & Gas Law 22/2001 | Shell (28% AOG emissions), Chevron | IOC exited → state inherited → License supersedes |
| **Thailand** | Petroleum Act B.E. 2514 | Chevron (42% AOG emissions) | IOC exited → state inherited → License supersedes |
| **Kazakhstan** | Subsoil Code 2017 | NCOC consortium | State owns → $4.9B fine pending → License supersedes |
| **Uzbekistan** | Oil & Gas Law 1994 | LUKOIL, CNPC, Gazprom | State monopoly → Soviet legacy → License supersedes |

| **Malaysia** | Petroleum Development Act 1974 | PETRONAS, Shell (legacy) | State custodian → Shell legacy → License supersedes |

### 12.3 The Retrocalic Bite (Section 32) — Application

For every abandoned well in Asia:

1. **Date of drilling** — Physics was used from this date forward
2. **Date of abandonment** — Physics obligation ceased without return to the Abundance Loop
3. **Duration of use** — Determines the field debt
4. **Methane emissions since abandonment** — Ongoing field debt (CEADs-AOGI: 400,000 tonnes methane globally in 2022 from AOG wells)
5. **Environmental damage** — Additional field debt (Kashagan $4.9B fine is a precedent)

### 12.4 The Abundance Loop (Section 4.4) — Calculation

```
Field_Debt = (Physics_Used × Duration_of_Use) + (Methane_Emissions × GWP_84) + (Environmental_Damage_Cost)
```

Where:
- **Physics_Used** = Energy extracted from the field (production volumes × energy content)
- **Duration_of_Use** = Days from spud to abandonment
- **Methane_Emissions** = Estimated cumulative methane (kg) — CEADs-AOGI provides country-level estimates
- **GWP_84** = 84× CO₂ (20-year global warming potential of methane)
- **Environmental_Damage_Cost** = Cleanup and remediation cost (Kashagan precedent: $4.9B for one field)
