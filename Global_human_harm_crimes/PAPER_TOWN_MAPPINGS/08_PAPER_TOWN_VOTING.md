## THE DELAWARE CORPORATE VOTING SYSTEM

### How It Works

In Delaware, if a corporation or LLC owns property in a municipality, it can vote in that municipality's elections. There is no requirement that the entity have actual operations, employees, or physical presence — only property ownership.

**The mechanism:**
1. Register a shell company in Delaware (cost: $50)
2. Purchase property in a Delaware municipality through the shell company
3. The shell company becomes a "qualified voter" in that municipality
4. The shell company casts votes through its designated representative
5. The beneficial owner exercises political power through the paper entity

## THE SEAFORD CASE

### HB121: The Paper Town Voting Bill

In Seaford, Delaware, **230+ business entities** could potentially vote in municipal elections versus approximately **340 human voters**. This means:

- **40% of potential voters** could be paper entities
- Shell companies with no employees, no operations, and no physical presence could swing elections
- The beneficial owners of these entities — who may not live in Seaford — could control local government

### The Legal Challenge

The **ACLU of Delaware** challenged the corporate voting mechanism as unconstitutional:
- Paper entities do not have the same constitutional rights as human voters
- Allowing corporate voting dilutes the votes of actual residents
- The mechanism creates a situation where non-residents can control local government through shell companies

### The Court Ruling

In **May 2026**, a Delaware court upheld corporate voting in Fenwick Island, establishing legal precedent that paper entities can exercise political power in municipal elections.

## THE CONSTITUTIONAL QUESTION

### The ACLU Challenge

The ACLU of Delaware argued that corporate voting is unconstitutional because:
1. **Equal Protection:** Paper entities do not have the same rights as human voters
2. **One Person, One Vote:** Corporate voting dilutes the votes of actual residents
3. **Democratic Self-Governance:** Non-residents should not control local government through shell companies
4. **Due Process:** The mechanism allows hidden beneficial owners to exercise political power

### The Court's Response

The Delaware court upheld corporate voting based on:
1. **Historical precedent:** Delaware has allowed corporate voting for over a century
2. **Statutory authority:** Delaware Code title 15, section 7001
3. **Property-based sufficiency:** Property ownership, not residency, is the basis for voting rights in certain municipalities
4. **Business-friendly jurisdiction:** Delaware's Court of Chancery is designed to protect corporate interests
