## Findings

### Finding 1: Jones Day — R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Defense (1953–Present)

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Jones Day has represented R.J. Reynolds Tobacco Company since the 1950s as national coordinating counsel in smoking and health litigation. The firm has defended R.J. Reynolds in every jury trial and major appellate litigation from the tobacco industry's many challenges.

**Key facts:**
- Jones Day defended R.J. Reynolds in the landmark Galbraith case where Melvin Belli, the "king of torts," took the tobacco industry to trial for the first time in fifteen years
- The jury refused (9-3) to find that smoking caused Galbraith's death
- Jones Day followed up with a victory in Tennessee where the judge held that the required federal statutory warning label preempted any other duty to warn
- The judge ruled that harms from cigarette smoking were "common knowledge"
- Jones Day has represented R.J. Reynolds in the "Engle progeny" tobacco litigation in Florida state court, winning numerous defense verdicts
- Jones Day achieved dismissal of a PFAS class action involving nearly 12 million Ohioans (Source 18)

**Harm caused:** tobacco-related illness and death — over 480,000 deaths per year in the US from smoking (CDC data)

**Source:** Jones Day website (Source 1), CVN BigLaw Spotlight (Source 3), Legal 500 (Source 18)

### Finding 2: Jones Day — Purdue Pharma Opioid Defense

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Jones Day serves as lead counsel advising Purdue Pharma on opioid litigation and bankruptcy proceedings. Purdue Pharma pleaded guilty to fraud and kickback conspiracies and was sentenced to pay criminal penalties of over $5 billion (Source 8).

**Key facts:**
- Purdue Pharma put profits over patient health and safety (DOJ statement)
- The company willfully rejected the law and ignored the diversion of their highly addictive prescription drugs
- Their actions contributed to the opioid crisis that claimed countless lives
- Purdue defrauded the DEA by misrepresenting the effectiveness of its programs designed to prevent illegal diversion
- The company paid kickbacks to prescribers through its doctor speaker program
- Criminal fine: $3.544 billion; criminal forfeiture: $2 billion

**Harm caused:** Over 500,000 opioid-related deaths in the US since 1999 (CDC data)

**Source:** DOJ press release (Source 8), Ben Byers Law (Source 12)

### Finding 4: Kirkland & Ellis — Brown & Williamson Tobacco Defense

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

In 1995, Kirkland & Ellis made legal threats against CBS to prevent it from airing a 60 Minutes interview with tobacco industry whistleblower Jeffrey Wigand. Wigand was a former vice president of research and development at Brown & Williamson who exposed the company's manipulation of nicotine.

**Key facts:**
- Wigand exposed that Brown & Williamson intentionally manipulated its tobacco blend with chemicals such as ammonia to increase the effect of nicotine
- Brown & Williamson sued Wigand for breach of contract
- The company paid the firm Investigative Group International to dig up a 500-page dossier on Wigand's private life
- The dossier was leaked to friendly outlets
- Wigand received two recorded death threats, one traced back inside Brown & Williamson itself
- An ex-FBI agent on the company's payroll trailed him around Louisville
- Callers phoned bomb threats to the high school where he taught
- Brown & Williamson threatened to cut the medical benefits his daughter needed (she had spina bifida)
- CBS spiked his 60 Minutes interview once before it eventually aired
- The Tobacco Master Settlement Agreement of 1998 required tobacco companies to pay at least $206 billion over 25 years

**Harm caused:** Facilitated continued tobacco deception, delayed public accountability for nicotine addiction, harmed whistleblowers

**Source:** Wikipedia - Kirkland & Ellis (Source 4), National Whistleblower Center (Source 5), Wikipedia - Jeffrey Wigand (Source 6), WeMoral (Source 15)

The pattern is clear: major law firms systematically defend industries that cause mass harm — tobacco, opioids, firearms, fossil fuels — for substantial fees. These firms enable continued harm by providing legal shields that delay accountability, suppress whistleblowers, and dismiss victim claims.
