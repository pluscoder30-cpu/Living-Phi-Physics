## 1. THE COMPLETE MORTGAGE PROCESS

### 1.1 What a Mortgage Is

A mortgage is a loan from a bank, credit union, or financial institution used to buy or refinance a home. The borrower repays the principal plus interest over 15 or 30 years. The property serves as **collateral** -- if the borrower defaults, the lender can seize and sell the property. [Source 3]

Key definition: "A mortgage is a secured loan, which means the lender maintains a lien on your home until you pay the mortgage in full." [Source 3]

### 1.2 The 8-Step Origination Process

**Step 1: Pre-qualification / Pre-approval**
- Borrower provides income, debts, credit score to lender
- Pre-qualification: quick estimate based on self-reported information
- Pre-approval: lender pulls credit, reviews documents, issues letter stating approved amount
- "Preapproval isn't a firm commitment to lend, but it does show how much you're likely to get" [Source 2]

**Step 2: Mortgage Application**
- Formal application with detailed financial information
- Documents: pay stubs (2 years), W-2s, tax returns, bank statements, employment history, photo ID
- Lender issues Loan Estimate within 3 business days [Source 4]
- Six key pieces: name, income, SSN, property address, estimated value, loan amount [Source 4]

**Step 3: Loan Processing**
- Loan processor reviews file, verifies documents, ensures completeness
- Orders title search, appraisal, credit report

**Step 4: Home Appraisal**
- Independent third-party appraiser evaluates property
- Considers location, condition, recent comparable sales
- Lender will not approve loan for more than appraised value

**Step 5: Underwriting**
- Underwriter reviews full application for risk assessment
- Evaluates: credit score, DTI ratio, income consistency, appraisal results, employment verification
- May approve, deny, or request additional documentation
- "The underwriter reviews the full picture, not to find faults, but to confirm the loan meets the required guidelines" [Source 1]

**Step 6: Clear to Close**
- All conditions met, final approval issued
- Closing Disclosure provided at least 3 days before closing

**Step 7: Closing**
- Sign loan documents, pay down payment and closing costs
- Typical closing costs: 2-5% of purchase price [Source 3]
- Includes: origination fee (0.5-1% of loan), underwriting fee, title fees, legal fees

**Step 8: Servicing**
- Monthly payments begin
- Payment split: interest (front-loaded) + principal (increases over time)
- Amortization schedule shows payment breakdown

### 1.3 Key Financial Metrics

**Debt-to-Income Ratio (DTI):** Monthly gross income going to debts. Most loans require DTI < 36%.

**Interest Rate Determination:** Credit score, down payment size (20% avoids PMI), loan type, loan term, points paid upfront.

### 1.4 The Origination Fee

"Lenders typically charge an origination fee -- usually between 0.5 percent and 1 percent of the loan amount" [Source 2]. Riskier borrowers may face higher fees (up to 2%). Higher-risk borrowers get higher fees, compounding the burden on those least able to afford it.

## 2. HOW MORTGAGE-BACKED SECURITIES WORK

### 2.1 The Securitization Chain

`
BORROWER -> ORIGINATOR -> ARRANGER -> GSE/ISSUER -> INVESTOR
   |          |           |           |            |
Pays         Creates     Bundles     Guarantees    Receives
mortgage     loan        loans into  & sells       principal
payments     + fees      pools       MBS           + interest
`

1. **Origination:** Bank or nonbank lender creates mortgage, collects origination fees (0.5-1% of loan) [Source 5]
2. **Pooling:** Loans grouped by similar characteristics (rate, term, LTV, geography)
3. **Securitization:** Pooled loans converted into tradable securities
4. **Guarantee:** GSE (Fannie Mae/Freddie Mac) or government (Ginnie Mae) guarantees timely payment
5. **Sale:** MBS sold to investors in secondary market

### 2.2 Types of MBS

**Pass-Through Securities:** All cash flows paid pro-rata. Servicer fee deducted first (25bp for GSE, 44bp for Ginnie Mae). Guarantee fee: 40-50bp for GSE, 6bp for Ginnie Mae. [Source 5]

**Collateralized Mortgage Obligations (CMOs):** Pass-throughs resecuritized into tranches with different prepayment risk and duration. "Sequential pay" structure. .3 trillion outstanding as of 2021. [Source 5]

**Stripped MBS (SMBS):** Separate cash flows into interest-only (IO) and principal-only (PO) tranches.

### 2.3 The TBA Market

"To-Be-Announced" forward market where most agency MBS trading occurs. Buyer/seller agree on: agency, coupon, maturity, price, face value, settlement month. Exact pools not specified at trade time. "The TBA market is among the most liquid markets for securities" [Source 8].

### 2.4 Scale

~2/3 of all residential mortgages securitized as MBS. Nearly all as agency MBS. Agency MBS: ~-9 trillion outstanding.

### 2.5 The Profit Engine

Fees extracted at every step:
1. **Originator:** origination fee (0.5-1%) + gain on sale (1-3%)
2. **Arranger:** structuring fee + underwriting spread
3. **GSE:** guarantee fee (40-50bp annually on entire pool)
4. **Servicer:** servicing fee (25bp annually)
5. **Rating agencies:** rating fee per tranche

"The originator might sell a portfolio of loans with an initial principal balance of \ million for \ million, corresponding to a gain on sale of \ million." [Source 24]

## 3. HOW CDOs WORK

### 3.1 Definition

"A CDO issues debt and equity and uses the money it raises to invest in a portfolio of financial assets such as corporate loans or mortgage-backed securities. It distributes the cash flows from its asset portfolio to the holders of its various liabilities in prescribed ways that take into account the relative seniority of those liabilities." [Source 9]

### 3.2 The Tranche Structure

`
AAA Senior Tranche (70-80% of pool)
  Lowest risk, lowest yield (2-4%)
  First claim on cash flows, last to absorb losses

Mezzanine Tranche (10-20% of pool)
  Medium risk, medium yield (5-10%)
  Paid after senior, absorbs losses after equity

Equity/Junior Tranche (5-10% of pool)
  Highest risk, highest yield (15-20%+)
  Paid last, first to absorb losses, often unrated
`

### 3.3 Turning Junk into Gold

IMF documented the core mechanism: "To move subprime mortgage debt through the market, Wall Street used the collateralized debt obligation... The key was to divide up the risk, creating low-risk investment-grade segments and higher-risk segments."

**The math:** "80 percent of the subprime debt can be resold to institutional investors as senior-tranche, investment-grade assets." [Source 18]

Subprime mortgages (junk quality) -> pooled into CDO -> senior tranche rated AAA -> pension funds and insurance companies (mandated to hold only investment-grade) could buy subprime exposure.

### 3.4 Credit Enhancement

1. Overcollateralization
2. Subordination
3. Excess spread
4. Reserve accounts
5. Cash flow diversion triggers

"To get an AAA rating, a general rule is that the credit enhancement must equal five times the expected loss level." [Source 10]

### 3.5 Synthetic CDOs

"A synthetic CDO is economically similar to a cash CDO in most respects. The principal difference is that rather than there being an underlying portfolio of corporate bonds... the underlying portfolio is actually a basket of credit default swap contracts." [Source 10]

Synthetic CDOs can create UNLIMITED exposure to the same underlying assets. No natural limit.

"Goldman Sachs alone packaged and sold \ billion in synthetic CDOs from July 1, 2004, to May 31, 2007. Synthetic CDOs created by Goldman referenced more than 3,400 mortgage securities, and 610 of them were referenced at least twice." [Source 25]

### 3.6 The Seven Frictions (NY Fed) [Source 24]

1. Borrower <-> Originator: Predatory lending
2. Originator <-> Arranger: Adverse selection
3. Arranger <-> Third Parties: Information asymmetry
4. Servicer <-> Mortgagor: Moral hazard
5. Servicer <-> Third Parties: Moral hazard
6. Investors <-> Rating Agencies: Cannot evaluate model efficacy
7. Rating Agencies <-> Market: Ratings assigned with significant error

## 4. HOW CREDIT DEFAULT SWAPS WORK

### 4.1 Definition

"A CDS is a contract where a 'protection buyer' agrees to make periodic payments (the CDS 'spread' or premium) over a predetermined number of years... to a 'protection seller' in exchange for a payment from the protection seller in the event of default by a 'reference entity.'" [Source 11]

### 4.2 Mechanics

**Example from Federal Reserve:**
- Notional amount: \ million
- CDS spread: 40 basis points
- Term: 5 years
- Protection buyer pays: \,000/quarter (\,000/year)
- If default occurs: Protection seller pays difference between face value and recovery value
- If bonds recover 20 cents on dollar: Seller pays \ million [Source 11]

### 4.3 Settlement Methods

**Physical Settlement:** Protection buyer delivers defaulted bond to seller. Seller pays face value.

**Cash Settlement:** Credit event auction determines recovery price. Seller pays difference between face value and auction price. Became standard after 2009. [Source 11]

### 4.4 Credit Events (Triggers)

Standard credit events: Bankruptcy, failure to pay, debt restructuring, obligation acceleration, obligation default, government intervention (sovereign), repudiation/moratorium (sovereign). [Source 29]

### 4.5 The Critical Problem: Naked CDS

"A CDS can be bought by a person who does not actually hold the underlying asset (a bond). (Imagine trying to buy insurance for a house that one does not own!) Consequently, the total value of CDS contracts can (and frequently does) exceed the amount of outstanding debt being insured." [Source 13]

This means:
- Investors could bet against mortgages they didn't own
- Total "insurance" written could vastly exceed the actual mortgage pool
- This MULTIPLIED the losses when defaults occurred

### 4.6 Scale

- CDS market: >\ trillion gross notional exposure [Source 29]
- AIG Financial Products: >\ billion in CDS on mortgage securities [Source 25]
- CDS are OTC instruments -- no centralized exchange, no transparency
- "Pricing is not transparent for contracts that trade infrequently" [Source 13]

### 4.7 AIG: The Node of Systemic Risk

AIG's Financial Products unit wrote CDS on mortgage-backed CDOs without:
- Setting aside capital reserves
- Understanding concentration risk in subprime
- Having sufficient liquidity for simultaneous triggers

When housing prices fell:
1. Subprime mortgages defaulted
2. MBS values collapsed
3. CDOs backed by those MBS were downgraded
4. AIG's CDS contracts triggered
5. AIG owed \+ billion but had no reserves
6. Government committed \+ billion to prevent cascading failures [Source 25]

## 6. THE FRAUD MECHANICS

### 6.1 Predatory Lending Practices

**HUD/Treasury Task Force findings:** [Source 23]

**Loan Flipping:** "Some mortgage originators refinanced borrowers' loans repeatedly in a short period of time. With each successive refinancing, these originators charged high fees that reduced borrowers' equity in their homes. Borrowers in some instances were charged upwards of \,000 for each refinancing, sometimes as much as 10 percent of the loan amount."

**Lending Without Regard to Ability to Repay:** "Some lenders may have sought to recover the loan principal by foreclosing on the borrower's home. In particularly egregious cases, loans were made to elderly people living on fixed incomes where the monthly payments equaled or exceeded their monthly income."

**Asset Flipping:** "An unscrupulous investor buys a dilapidated house, makes superficial repairs, and conspires with an appraiser and a mortgage broker or other originator to inflate the appraised value of the property."

**Targeting:** "Subprime lending is most heavily concentrated in lower-income and predominantly minority neighborhoods."

### 6.2 The Underwriting Collapse

Documentation standards deteriorated dramatically:
- 2001: ~70% of subprime loans had full documentation
- 2006: ~50% had low or no documentation [Source 20]

"Lax, if not fraudulent, underwriting practices in subprime mortgage lending largely explain the rise in the rate of seriously delinquent loans from 6 percent to 9 percent between the second quarter of 2006 and the second quarter of 2007." [Source 18]

### 6.3 Rating Agency Fraud

"Credit rating agencies catered to investment banks by issuing increasingly inflated ratings on both RMBS and collateralized debt obligations (CDOs)." [Source 22]

- In 2003, Fitch director warned: "One of the things we will be watching closely for is a loosening in underwriting guidelines"
- By 2005, rating agencies were fielding complaints that ratings were too high
- Fannie Mae's chief risk officer wrote a 2005 memo warning that subprime loans would lose value if housing prices dropped
- Freddie Mac's chief risk officer advised in 2004 that subprime loans "would likely pose an enormous financial and reputational risk"

### 6.4 The "Originate to Distribute" Model

"This 'originate to distribute' model led to a rise in predatory lending that targeted a wide spectrum of consumers who might not have understood the embedded risks." [Source 19]

**The perverse incentives:**
- Mortgage brokers: collect fees at closing, bear no credit risk
- Lenders: earn upfront fees, pass loans to investors
- Investment banks: make money from securitizing, had incentive to maximize volume
- Rating agencies: paid by issuers (conflict of interest), not investors
- Investors: relied on ratings, didn't do independent due diligence

"saga of subprime mortgage lending was a game of hot potato, and few of the players can legitimately deny that they knew the potato was hot" [Source 21]

## 8. THE GEOMETRY OF DEBT: pattern ratio PATTERNS

### 8.1 Compound Interest as Exponential Growth

The fundamental equation of compound interest:

A = P(1 + r/n)^{nt}

Where:
- A = final amount
- P = principal
- r = annual interest rate
- n = compounding periods per year
- t = years

As n approaches infinity, this converges to continuous compounding:

A = Pe^{rt}

Where e = 2.71828... (Euler's number) [Source 39, 40]

**The mathematical insight:** "Your effective interest rate is about r^2/2 more than the nominal one" for small r [Source 39].

### 8.2 The Effective Rate Trap

Credit cards illustrate the gap between stated and effective rates:

- Stated: 1.5% per month = 18% per year (intuitively)
- Actual (with monthly compounding): 1.015^{12} - 1 = 19.56\%$

"Because compounding occurs more than once per year, the true annual rate is higher than appears." [Source 38]

For daily compounding (standard credit card):
- Stated APR: 20%
- Effective Annual Rate: ^{0.20} - 1 = 22.14\%$

### 8.3 The Amortization Trap

Mortgage amortization front-loads interest payments. Example from Plus Maths [Source 40]:

Loan: 100,000 at 7% over 25 years. Monthly payment: 706.78

Payment 1: 583.33 interest, 123.45 principal (only 17.5% goes to principal)
After 20 years: Only ~2/3 of principal repaid, even though 80% of time has elapsed.

"The interest component falls and the capital repayment rises as the payments are made." [Source 40]

### 8.4 The Golden Ratio and Financial Spirals

The golden ratio (phi = 1.618...) appears in nature as the optimal growth pattern. In debt systems, the inverse of phi (0.618...) represents the "????" of destruction:

**The pattern ratio in interest rates:**
- Federal funds rate (2003-2006): 1% (artificially low)
- Subprime mortgage rates: 8-15% (1.25-1.5x above prime)
- Credit card rates: 18-29% (approximately phi^3 = 4.23x above prime)
- Payday loan rates: 300-400% (phi^6 = 17.9x above prime)

The progression follows a rough phi-power series:
- Prime rate: base
- Subprime: prime + phi^1
- Credit cards: prime + phi^3
- Payday: prime + phi^6

**The compounding doubling time:**
At 7% interest (roughly the average mortgage rate), the Rule of 72 says debt doubles every ~10.3 years. At 20% (credit cards), debt doubles every ~3.6 years. The ratio of doubling times: 10.3/3.6 = 2.86, which is approximately phi^2 = 2.618.

### 8.6 The Self-Similar Structure of Debt Instruments

The debt system exhibits self-similarity (a fractal property):

Each level extracts fees and amplifies the underlying structure. The self-similarity means the same dynamics that cause individual borrowers to struggle cause systemic crises.
