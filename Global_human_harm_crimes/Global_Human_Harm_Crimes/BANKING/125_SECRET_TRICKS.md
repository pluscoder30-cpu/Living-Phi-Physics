## Part I: Overdraft Tricks (Tricks 1-25)

### Trick 1: Biggest-First Transaction Reordering
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Banks process debits from largest to smallest, maximizing the number of overdraft fees from a single days transactions. A customer making 5 small purchases that are individually affordable can incur 5 overdraft fees when one large transaction depletes the account, because the bank posts the large one first.

**Source:** Wikipedia, citing USA Today and U.S. Bank $55M settlement (2014)
**Harm:** Artificially triggers multiple $35 overdraft fees per day

### Trick 2: Courtesy Overdraft as Profit Center
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Banks present overdraft protection as a customer service, but the program is a massive profit center. The CFPB found banks charged $15 billion in overdraft and NSF fees in 2022 alone. Consumers who opt into overdraft programs pay more than 7x as much in fees, averaging approximately $260/year.

**Source:** CFPB (Sources 3, 6); Wikipedia citing CFPB data
**Harm:** Low-income consumers subsidize banking operations

### Trick 3: Opt-In Deception
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

After the 2010 Federal Reserve opt-in regulation, banks were required to get affirmative consent for overdraft coverage on debit/ATM transactions. Despite this, approximately 90% of customers opted in, often through confusing disclosures or at account opening when the opt-in was bundled with other paperwork.

**Source:** Wikipedia citing Moebs Services (2011)
**Harm:** Consumers unknowingly consent to $35 per-overdraft fees

### Trick 4: Float Exploitation
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Banks exploit the time lag between when a check is deposited and when it clears. They may make funds available immediately but take days to verify the check, creating a window where the customer believes they have money they do not.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft article)
**Harm:** Customers spend funds that are later clawed back, triggering overdrafts

### Trick 5: Authorization Hold Manipulation
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

When a customer makes a debit card purchase without PIN (credit mode), funds are placed on hold. The merchant does not collect for days. The bank may release the hold early, but when the merchant finally collects, the account overdraws if the customer spent the available funds.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft article)
**Harm:** Creates phantom available balance, triggers overdrafts

### Trick 6: Deposit Hold Timing
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Banks use Regulation CC (which governs check holds) to justify holding deposits longer than necessary. Even when funds appear available, the bank may not have actually received the money. Early spending triggers overdrafts.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft article)
**Harm:** Misleads customers about actual available balance

### Trick 7: Returned Check Fee Stacking
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

When a deposited check bounces, the bank charges a fee for the returned check AND may charge an overdraft fee if the reversal creates a negative balance. The original depositor is victimized twice.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft article)
**Harm:** Double-fee victimization

### Trick 8: Intraday Overdraft Gaming
**Confidence:** [PV]

Some banks charge overdraft fees even for intraday overdrafts that are corrected by end of day. The fee is assessed at the moment of negative balance, even if a deposit arrives hours later.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft article)
**Harm:** Punishes customers for temporary negative balances

### Trick 9: NSF Fee Stacking
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Each rejected transaction generates its own NSF fee ($25-35). A single direct debit rejection can cascade into multiple returned items on the same day, each generating a separate fee.

**Source:** Wikipedia citing OFT; CFPB data
**Harm:** Cascading fees from a single event

### Trick 10: Overdraft Fee on the Overdraft Fee
**Confidence:** [PV]

When an overdraft fee itself pushes the account more negative, some banks charge an additional overdraft fee for that fee. Halifax in the UK had a no fees on fees policy, implying this was widespread.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft article)
**Harm:** Recursive fee extraction

### Trick 11: Monthly Overdraft Limits as False Protection
**Confidence:** [PV]

Some banks cap overdraft fees per month (e.g., 10 per month), presenting this as consumer-friendly. In reality, 10 x $35 = $350/month in fees for a customer who may have overdrafted by $1.

**Source:** Wikipedia (UK overdraft section)
**Harm:** Creates illusion of consumer protection while maintaining profit extraction

### Trick 12: Cross-Account Overdraft Sweeping
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Banks automatically transfer funds from savings to checking to cover overdrafts, charging a transfer fee ($10-12 per transfer) and depleting the customers savings without explicit permission.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Linked accounts section)
**Harm:** Depletes savings, charges fee for the service

### Trick 13: Overdraft Line of Credit Predation
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Banks offer overdraft lines of credit that charge interest on the negative balance plus a per-overdraft fee. The line of credit is invisible in the available balance, so customers do not realize they are borrowing.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Overdraft lines of credit section)
**Harm:** Hidden debt accumulation

### Trick 14: Bounce Protection as Unregulated Loan
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Bounce protection advances funds like a loan but is not regulated as one under the Truth in Lending Act, because the bank is not contractually obligated to cover the overdraft. This means no APR disclosure, no cooling-off period, and no consumer protections.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Bounce protection section)
**Harm:** Eliminates consumer protections applicable to loans

### Trick 15: Bounce Protection Added Without Permission
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Historically, bounce protection could be added to a consumers account without their permission or knowledge.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Bounce protection section)
**Harm:** Unauthorized enrollment in fee-generating program

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

**Source:** Wikipedia (Bounce protection section)

### Trick 17: Recurring Daily Negative Balance Fees
**Confidence:** [PV]

**Source:** Wikipedia (UK overdraft section)

**Source:** Wikipedia (UK section)
**Harm:** Daily fee extraction maximizes revenue from brief overdrafts

### Trick 21: Refused Payment Fee Taking Account Overdrawn
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

In the UK, banks charged a fee for refusing a payment (direct debit, cheque). That fee itself pushed accounts into unauthorized overdraft, triggering additional fees.

**Source:** Wikipedia (UK section)
**Harm:** Fee-on-fee cascade

### Trick 22: ChexSystems Blacklisting
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Without checking accounts, consumers are forced into payday lenders, check-cashing services, and money orders, each extracting significantly higher fees. The FDIC found black and Hispanic families, recent immigrants, and single parents are disproportionately affected.

### Trick 24: Wage Garnishment Overdraft Trigger
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

When a wage garnishment, tax levy, or offset claim hits an account, it can trigger an overdraft. The consumer has no control over the timing and is penalized for involuntary debits.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Unexpected electronic withdrawals section)

### Trick 25: Bank Error Overdraft
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

When a bank posts a cheque for an incorrect amount due to error, the customer bears the overdraft cost. Some bank errors work to the customers detriment without adequate remediation.

## Part II: Credit Card Tricks (Tricks 26-55)

### Trick 26: Penalty APR (29.99%+)
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

One late payment can trigger a penalty APR of 20-30%+ on the ENTIRE balance. First Premier Bank once offered a card with 79.9% APR. The penalty rate applies retroactively to existing balances.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - High interest section)
**Harm:** Retroactive rate increase on existing debt

### Trick 27: Universal Default
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Missing a payment on ANY account with the same issuer (e.g., a store card) can trigger a penalty rate on your primary credit card, even if the primary card payments were on time.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - High interest section)
**Harm:** Cross-product penalty application

### Trick 28: Arbitrary Rate Increases
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Most cardholder agreements enable the issuer to arbitrarily raise the interest rate for any reason. No specific trigger is required.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - High interest section)
**Harm:** Unpredictable rate increases without cause

### Trick 29: Introductory Rate Bait-and-Switch
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Low introductory rates (0-2%) are limited to 6-12 months, then jump to 20%+. Research shows approximately 40% of consumers choose suboptimal agreements, incurring hundreds in avoidable interest.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - High interest section)
**Harm:** Deceptive rate structure leads to debt accumulation

### Trick 30: Minimum Payment Trap (Negative Amortization)
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

When minimum payments are less than monthly interest charges, the balance GROWS despite payments. This was banned in the US in 2003, but the practice existed for decades.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - Minimum payment section)
**Harm:** Debt grows despite regular payments

### Trick 31: Payment Allocation Manipulation
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

When multiple balance segments exist (transfers, purchases, cash advances), payments are allocated to the LOWEST rate balance first. Higher-rate balances accrue interest longer. The bank controls allocation.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - Interest charges section)
**Harm:** Maximizes total interest paid

### Trick 32: Cash Advance Trap
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Cash advances incur 3-5% upfront fees PLUS higher interest rates PLUS no grace period (interest starts accruing immediately). Purchases deemed cash (money orders, prepaid cards, lottery tickets) are processed as cash advances without clear disclosure.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - Cash advance section)
**Harm:** Hidden classification triggers highest fee tier

### Trick 33: Residual Retail Finance Charge (RRFC)
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

After paying the balance in full, customers still receive interest charges on subsequent statements because interest is calculated back to the original transaction date. The residual charge persists even after the balance is paid.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - Interest charges section)
**Harm:** Lingering charges after full payment

### Trick 34: Grace Period Elimination
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

If ANY balance remains from the previous cycle, the grace period is eliminated and interest is charged on ALL new purchases from the transaction date, even if the previous balance is small.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Credit card - Grace period section)
**Harm:** New purchases immediately accrue interest

### Trick 35: Credit Card Dropping (Historical)
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Until 1970, banks mass-mailed unsolicited credit cards to unemployable people, drunks, narcotics addicts, and compulsive debtors (Life magazine). Approximately 100 million cards were distributed before the practice was outlawed.

**Harm:** Predatory distribution to vulnerable populations

## Part III: Mortgage and Lending Tricks (Tricks 56-80)

### Trick 56: Loan Flipping
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Frequent refinancings that generate fees and prepayment penalties for the lender with little or no benefit to the borrower. Each refinance adds origination fees, title insurance, and other closing costs.

**Source:** OCC Advisory Letter AL 2003-2; Wikipedia (Predatory lending)
**Harm:** Erodes home equity through repeated fees

### Trick 57: Packing (Hidden Fees in Amount Financed)
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Excessive and sometimes hidden fees are packed into the amount financed, increasing the loan principal without the borrowers knowledge.

**Source:** OCC Advisory Letter AL 2003-2
**Harm:** Borrower unknowingly finances fees as debt

### Trick 58: Negative Amortization Loans
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Loan structures where payments do not cover interest, causing the principal to INCREASE over time. The borrower owes more than they originally borrowed.

**Source:** OCC Advisory Letter AL 2003-2
**Harm:** Debt grows despite regular payments

### Trick 59: Balloon Payment Concealment
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Low initial payments hide a large balloon payment due at term end, forcing borrowers into costly refinancing or foreclosure.

**Source:** OCC Advisory Letter AL 2003-2
**Harm:** Forces refinancing or loss of home

### Trick 60: Single-Premium Credit Insurance
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Insurance that pays off the loan if the borrower dies is financed into the loan (increasing principal) and is more expensive than term life insurance. Lenders are not licensed to sell other insurance forms, so borrowers are not shown alternatives.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Predatory lending - Abusive practices)
**Harm:** Overpriced insurance hidden in loan principal

### Trick 61: Failure to Disclose Negotiability
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Many lenders can negotiate interest rates and terms, but do not inform borrowers, especially unsophisticated ones, of this ability. Borrowers do not realize the initial offer is not final.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Predatory lending)
**Harm:** Information asymmetry benefits lender

### Trick 62: Targeting Vulnerable Populations
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Predatory lenders disproportionately target less educated, poor, racial minorities, and elderly borrowers. These groups are most likely to accept unfair terms.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Predatory lending); FDIC OIG
**Harm:** Systematic exploitation of vulnerable communities

### Trick 63: Risk-Based Pricing as Discrimination
**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

Risk-based pricing charges higher rates to borrowers identified as greater credit risks. Studies show minorities are disproportionately placed in subprime products even when creditworthy, because lenders call minority neighborhoods never-never land.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Predatory lending); HUD archives
**Harm:** Racial discrimination disguised as risk management

**Source:** Wikipedia (Payday loan - Pricing section); Federal Reserve Bank of Kansas City

Payday lenders have made effective use of the sovereign status of Native American reservations, forming partnerships with tribes to offer loans over the Internet that evade state law. Evidence shows many are rent-a-tribe schemes where non-Native companies set up operations on tribal land.

### Trick 79: Refund Anticipation Loans
**Confidence:** [PV]

Tax refund anticipation loans are technically not payday loans (repayable upon receipt of refund, not next payday) but have similar credit and cost characteristics. They extract fees from the working poor on money the government already owes them.

Auto title loans use vehicle equity as collateral. If the borrower defaults, the lender repossesses and resells the car. These loans are available only to borrowers with clear title, and the maximum amount is a fraction of the vehicles resale value.

**Source:** Wikipedia (Payday loan - Alternatives section)

## Part IV: Investment and Financial Advisor Tricks (Tricks 81-100)

### Trick 81: Front-Loaded Mutual Fund Commissions
**Confidence:** [PV]

Mutual funds sold through brokers carry front-end loads of 3-5.75%, meaning only 94-97 cents of every dollar invested actually enters the fund. The commission goes to the broker, not the investor.

**Source:** General financial knowledge; SEC Investor.gov
**Harm:** Immediate erosion of investment capital

### Trick 82: 12b-1 Fee Hidden in Returns
**Confidence:** [PV]

Annual marketing/distribution fees (12b-1 fees) of 0.25-1.00% are deducted from fund assets, reducing returns. These fees are not separately disclosed in dollar terms on statements.

**Source:** SEC Investor.gov; general financial knowledge
**Harm:** Ongoing invisible drag on investment returns

### Trick 83: Revenue Sharing Kickbacks
**Confidence:** [PV]

Brokerage firms receive payments from mutual fund companies for preferential placement on recommended lists. The funds with the best broker compensation, not the best investor returns, get recommended.
