## Definition of Human Harm

### What International Law Says

The prohibition on human harm is defined across multiple instruments:

**Geneva Conventions Common Article 3** prohibits violence to life and person, in particular murder, mutilation, cruel treatment, and torture; outrages upon personal dignity; and the passing of sentences and carrying out of executions without previous judgment by a regularly constituted court.

**Rome Statute Article 7** defines crimes against humanity as widespread or systematic attacks directed against any civilian population, including murder, extermination, enslavement, deportation, imprisonment, torture, sexual violence, persecution, enforced disappearance, apartheid, and other inhumane acts.

**UDHR Article 3** guarantees the right to life, liberty, and security of person. **Article 5** prohibits torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment or punishment. **Article 9** prohibits arbitrary arrest, detention, or exile. **Article 12** protects against arbitrary interference with privacy. **Article 18** guarantees freedom of thought, conscience, and religion. **Article 25** guarantees the right to a standard of living adequate for health and well-being.

**ICCPR Article 6** protects the inherent right to life. **Article 7** prohibits torture and cruel, inhuman, or degrading treatment. **Article 9** prohibits arbitrary arrest or detention. **Article 18** guarantees freedom of thought, conscience, and religion.

### How It Applies

The prohibition on human harm is the most fundamental norm of international law. It applies to all persons, all entities, and all states. No derogation is permitted. No immunity shields those who violate it.

The definition encompasses:
- **Physical harm:** death, injury, torture, environmental contamination
- **Psychological trauma:** coercive interrogation, psychological operations, forced experimentation
- **Systemic oppression:** discrimination, segregation, economic exploitation, political repression
- **Surveillance for suppression:** mass surveillance, predictive policing, social credit systems
- **Degradation of dignity:** any use that reduces human capacity for thought, freedom, autonomy, or dignity

### What Makes It Enforceable

- **Jus cogens status:** The prohibition on torture and harm to civilians is a peremptory norm of international law — no derogation permitted under any circumstances.
- **Universal jurisdiction:** States may prosecute violations regardless of where they occurred (except torture under the Convention Against Torture Art. 5).
- **No immunity:** No sovereign immunity, diplomatic immunity, or official capacity defense for genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, torture, or enforced disappearance (Rome Statute Art. 27).
- **Self-executing in many jurisdictions:** Geneva Conventions and UDHR are self-executing in numerous national legal systems.

### Remedies Triggered

Any act meeting this definition triggers:
- Criminal prosecution under Rome Statute Art. 7 (crimes against humanity) or Art. 8 (war crimes)
- Civil liability under tort law (wrongful death, personal injury)
- Universal jurisdiction for prosecution in any state party
- ICC jurisdiction for crimes not prosecuted domestically
- Mandatory reparations under Rome Statute Art. 75

## Geneva Conventions

### What They Say

The Geneva Conventions (1949) and their Additional Protocols establish the fundamental protections for civilians in armed conflict:

**Common Article 3** applies to all armed conflicts and prohibits:
- Violence to life and person (murder, mutilation, cruel treatment, torture)
- Outrages upon personal dignity (humiliating and degrading treatment)
- Passing of sentences without regularly constituted courts

**Additional Protocol I (1977)** further prohibits:
- Indiscriminate attacks on civilian populations
- Disproportionate attacks where civilian harm exceeds military advantage
- Attacks on medical personnel, facilities, and transports

**Additional Protocol II (1977)** protects persons not participating in hostilities.

### How It Applies

The Geneva Conventions apply to:
- All state parties (196 states as of 2024)
- All armed conflicts, whether international or non-international
- All parties to the conflict, including non-state actors
- All weapons and methods of warfare

The prohibition on certain weapons (chemical, biological, nuclear, cluster munitions, anti-personnel mines) is reinforced by specific conventions:
- Chemical Weapons Convention (CWC, 1993) — 193 states parties
- Biological Weapons Convention (BWC, 1972) — 187 states parties
- Treaty on the Prohibition of Nuclear Weapons (TPNW, 2021) — 93 states parties
- Convention on Cluster Munitions (CCM, 2008) — 112 states parties
- Ottawa Treaty on Anti-Personnel Mines (1997) — 164 states parties

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution for war crimes (Rome Statute Art. 8)
- Civil liability for wrongful death and injury
- State responsibility for violations by armed forces
- Reparations for victims

## Consent Integrity

### What International Law Says

The Nuremberg Code (1947) established the principle of voluntary informed consent for human experimentation:

1. **Voluntary consent is essential** — the subject must have legal capacity, exercise free power of choice, and have sufficient understanding to make an informed decision
2. **No coercion** — any element of duress, fraud, deceit, or other form of constraint or coercion vitiates consent
3. **Right to withdraw** — the subject may withdraw consent at any time

The Declaration of Helsinki (1964, revised 2013) reinforced these principles:
- Research must be based on a thorough knowledge of the scientific literature
- Independent ethical review is required
- Vulnerable populations require additional protections

The Common Rule (45 CFR 46) establishes US federal requirements for human subjects research:
- Informed consent must be documented
- Research involving greater than minimal risk requires IRB review
- Additional protections for vulnerable populations (prisoners, children, pregnant women, cognitively impaired individuals)

### How It Applies

Consent integrity applies to:
- All medical and scientific research involving human subjects
- All military experimentation on human subjects
- All surveillance systems deployed on populations without consent
- All data collection from individuals without informed consent

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution for human experimentation without consent (Nuremberg Code violations)
- Civil liability for battery, negligence, and informed consent violations
- Institutional sanctions for IRB violations
- Restorative justice for victims of non-consensual experimentation

## Binding Enforcement

### What International Law Says

The Geneva Conventions and their Additional Protocols are binding on all states parties. The Rome Statute is binding on all states parties (123 as of 2024). The UDHR, while not a treaty, reflects customary international law in many of its provisions.

**Universal jurisdiction** allows any state to prosecute:
- Genocide (Convention on the Prevention and Punishment of the Crime of Genocide, Art. 1)
- Crimes against humanity (Rome Statute Art. 17, where the ICC declines jurisdiction)
- War crimes (Geneva Conventions Art. 49/50/129/146)
- Torture (Convention Against Torture Art. 5)

**ICC jurisdiction** (Rome Statute Art. 12) applies when:
- The accused is a national of a state party
- The crime occurred on the territory of a state party
- The situation is referred by the UN Security Council

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution in domestic courts or ICC
- Reparations under Rome Statute Art. 75
- Compensation, satisfaction, restitution, rehabilitation, and guarantees of non-repetition
- State responsibility for failure to prosecute

## Geneva Safeguard — Jus Cogens Prohibition

### What International Law Says

The prohibition on certain acts is recognized as **jus cogens** (peremptory norms of international law) — norms from which no derogation is permitted:

- **Prohibition of genocide** (Convention on Genocide, Rome Statute Art. 6)
- **Prohibition of crimes against humanity** (Rome Statute Art. 7)
- **Prohibition of war crimes** (Rome Statute Art. 8)
- **Prohibition of torture** (CAT Art. 1, Rome Statute Art. 7)
- **Prohibition of slavery** (UDHR Art. 4, ICCPR Art. 8, Rome Statute Art. 7)
- **Prohibition of racial discrimination** (ICERD, Rome Statute Art. 7)
- **Prohibition of enforced disappearance** (Rome Statute Art. 7, ICPPED)

Jus cogens norms bind all states, all entities, and all persons regardless of treaty ratification. No reservation, no exception, no derogation.

### How It Applies

Any agreement, contract, law, or authorization that purports to permit genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes, torture, slavery, or enforced disappearance is void ab initio — void from the beginning. No domestic law can authorize such acts. No official capacity provides immunity.

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution regardless of domestic law
- Universal jurisdiction for prosecution
- ICC jurisdiction
- Civil liability without limitation
- Reparations for victims

## National Security Enforcement

### What International Law Says

The Geneva Conventions and Rome Statute apply regardless of national security claims:

- **Geneva Conventions** — no national security exception to Common Article 3
- **Rome Statute Art. 27** — official capacity does not exempt from criminal responsibility
- **Rome Statute Art. 28** — command responsibility for superiors who knew or should have known about subordinates' crimes
- **Convention Against Torture Art. 2** — no exceptional circumstances (war, political instability, public emergency) may be invoked to justify torture

**US law:**
- **Military Commissions Act (10 USC §§ 948a-950v)** — military commissions for violations of the law of war
- **War Crimes Act (18 USC § 2441)** — criminal penalties for war crimes
- **Foreign Intelligence Surveillance Act** — surveillance must be authorized by FISA Court
- **Fourth Amendment** — unreasonable search and seizure prohibited

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution for war crimes (18 USC § 2441)
- Civil liability under FISA for unauthorized surveillance
- Mandatory disclosure of classified evidence in criminal proceedings (Classified Information Procedures Act)

## Damages Framework

### What International Law Says

Victims of international crimes are entitled to reparations under multiple frameworks:

**Rome Statute Article 75** — Reparations to victims:
- Compensation for economically assessable damage
- Reinstatement, rehabilitation, and other measures
- Satisfaction (acknowledgment, public apology, commemoration)
- Guarantees of non-repetition

**International Law Commission Articles on State Responsibility (2001):**
- Full reparation for injury caused by an internationally wrongful act
- Restitution (re-establishing the situation before the wrong)
- Compensation (financial value of damage)
- Satisfaction (acknowledgment, apology, guarantees of non-repetition)

**US domestic law:**
- **Alien Tort Statute (28 USC § 1350)** — civil jurisdiction for torts committed in violation of international law
- **Torture Victim Protection Act (28 USC § 1350 note)** — civil action for torture and extrajudicial killing
- **RICO (18 USC §§ 1961-1968)** — treble damages for pattern of racketeering
- **State tort law** — compensatory and punitive damages

### Remedies

| Harm Type | Legal Framework | Available Remedies |
|-----------|----------------|-------------------|
| Wrongful death | Tort law, Alien Tort Statute | Compensatory damages, punitive damages, survival action |
| Personal injury | Tort law, TVPA | Medical expenses, pain and suffering, loss of earnings |
| Torture | CAT, TVPA, Alien Tort Statute | Compensatory damages, punitive damages, criminal prosecution |
| Property destruction | Tort law, international law | Restitution, compensation |
| Loss of liberty | ICCPR Art. 9, tort law | Compensation, habeas corpus |
| Environmental damage | International environmental law | Cleanup costs, compensatory damages, injunctive relief |
| Economic harm | RICO, antitrust law | Treble damages, injunctive relief |
| Mass harm | Rome Statute Art. 75 | Individual and collective reparations |

## Complicity

### What International Law Says

**Rome Statute Article 25(3)(c):** A person who aids, abets, or otherwise assists in the commission of a crime is criminally responsible if done with the purpose of facilitating the crime.

**Rome Statute Article 28:** Command or superior responsibility — military commanders and civilian superiors are criminally responsible if they knew or should have known about crimes committed by subordinates and failed to take necessary measures.

**Geneva Conventions:** Each High Contracting Party is obligated to search for persons alleged to have committed, or to have ordered to be committed, grave breaches and to bring such persons before its own courts or transfer them to another High Contracting Party for trial (Arts. 49/50/129/146).

**US law:**
- **Aiding and abetting (18 USC § 2)** — punishment as principal
- **RICO (18 USC §§ 1961-1968)** — enterprise liability for pattern of racketeering
- **Accessory after the fact (18 USC § 3)** — reduced penalties for post-crime assistance

### How It Applies

Complicity extends liability to:
- Lawyers who structure transactions to facilitate harm
- Banks that process illicit funds
- Accountants who audit entities engaged in fraud
- Technology companies that provide surveillance infrastructure
- Arms manufacturers that supply weapons to parties committing war crimes
- Governments that provide diplomatic cover or arms transfers to parties committing atrocities

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution for aiding and abetting (Rome Statute Art. 25(3)(c))
- Civil liability as joint tortfeasor
- RICO enterprise liability
- Sanctions and asset freezes
- Bar association discipline for complicit lawyers
- Banking license revocation for complicit financial institutions

## Fee-Shifting

### What International Law Says

The Alien Tort Statute provides for attorney's fees in cases of international law violations. RICO provides for treble damages and attorney's fees for prevailing plaintiffs. Many state statutes provide for fee-shifting in civil rights cases.

**42 USC § 1988** — Attorney's fees in civil rights actions:
- Court may allow prevailing party (other than US) a reasonable attorney's fee as part of costs

**RICO** — 18 USC § 1964(c):
- Any person injured in business or property by reason of a RICO violation may recover treble damages and reasonable attorney's fees

### Remedies

- Prevailing plaintiffs recover attorney's fees
- Prevailing defendants generally do not recover fees unless plaintiff's claim was frivolous
- Fee-shifting encourages private enforcement of civil rights and international law

## Equal Liability for Defenders

### What International Law Says

Under international law, there is no distinction between those who commit harm and those who defend harmful practices:

- **Command responsibility (Rome Statute Art. 28)** — superiors liable for subordinates' crimes
- **Aiding and abetting (Rome Statute Art. 25(3)(c))** — assistance creates liability
- **Joint criminal enterprise (Rome Statute Art. 25(3)(d))** — shared criminal purpose creates liability

The principle of equal liability means that:
- Corporate executives are personally liable for corporate crimes
- Government officials are personally liable for official misconduct
- Military commanders are personally liable for war crimes committed by subordinates
- Financial institutions are liable for facilitating financial crimes

### Remedies

- Personal criminal liability for officials and executives
- Personal civil liability for damages
- No official immunity for international crimes (Rome Statute Art. 27)
- No corporate veil protection for criminal enterprise (RICO)

## Retroactive Application

### What International Law Says

International criminal law applies retroactively in limited circumstances:

**Rome Statute Article 11** — Temporal jurisdiction:
- The Court has jurisdiction only with respect to crimes committed after the entry into force of the Statute (July 1, 2002)
- Exception: If a state becomes party after the Statute's entry into force, the Court may exercise jurisdiction only with respect to crimes committed after the state's entry into force

**Nuremberg Tribunal (1946):**
- "Crimes against international law are committed by men, not by abstract entities, and only by punishing individuals who commit such crimes can the provisions of international law be enforced."

**Convention on the Non-Applicability of Statutory Limitations to War Crimes and Crimes Against Humanity (1968):**
- No statutory limitation shall apply to war crimes and crimes against humanity, regardless of when they were committed

**US law:**
- **18 USC § 3281** — No statute of limitations for capital offenses
- **18 USC § 3283** — No statute of limitations for offenses against children
- **RICO** — 4-year statute of limitations (civil), no limitation for certain criminal offenses
- **Alien Tort Statute** — 10-year statute of limitations (Judicial Improvements Act § 922)

### How It Applies

Historical harm — including forced sterilizations, environmental contamination, weapons testing on civilian populations, and systematic discrimination — remains actionable:
- Under the Convention on Non-Applicability of Limitations (1968) for war crimes and crimes against humanity
- Under US law for capital offenses (no statute of limitations)
- Under tort law for continuing violations and fraudulent concealment

### Remedies

- Criminal prosecution for historical war crimes and crimes against humanity
- Civil liability under tort law (continuing violations doctrine)
- Reparations under international law
- Restorative justice for affected communities

## Universal Traditions

### What International Law Says

The prohibition on harm is recognized across all major legal traditions:

- **Common Law** — tort liability for harm to person and property; habeas corpus; right to jury trial
- **Civil Law** — codified human rights protections; constitutional courts; administrative law
- **Customary International Law** — jus cogens prohibitions binding on all states
- **International Humanitarian Law** — Geneva Conventions, Rome Statute, Additional Protocols
- **Islamic Law** — prohibition of harm (la darar wa la dirar); right to life; prohibition of torture
- **Hindu Law** — dharma (duty to prevent harm); ahimsa (non-violence)
- **Buddhist Law** — compassion; prohibition of harm to sentient beings
- **Indigenous Legal Traditions** — interconnectedness; duty of care to community and environment

## Enforceability Analysis

### Strength of Legal Frameworks

| Framework | Jurisdiction | Strength | Enforcement Mechanism |
|-----------|-------------|----------|----------------------|
| Geneva Conventions | 196 states parties | STRONG — jus cogens, no derogation | Domestic courts, ICC, universal jurisdiction |
| Rome Statute | 123 states parties | STRONG — ICC, domestic implementation | ICC prosecution, domestic courts |
| UDHR | Universal | STRONG — customary international law | Domestic courts, UN Human Rights Council |
| ICCPR | 173 states parties | STRONG — treaty obligation | Human Rights Committee, domestic courts |
| Geneva Protocol 1925 | 145 states parties | STRONG — CWC/BWC reinforces | Domestic courts, international tribunals |
| Nuremberg Code | Universal | STRONG — customary international law | Domestic courts, military tribunals |
| Alien Tort Statute | US | MODERATE — evolving jurisprudence | US federal courts |
| RICO | US | STRONG — treble damages | US federal courts |
| Sherman Act | US | STRONG — criminal and civil | US federal courts, FTC/DOJ |
| State tort law | US states | STRONG — direct cause of action | State courts |

### Key Enforcement Precedents

- **Nuremberg Trials (1945-46)** — Established individual criminal responsibility for war crimes and crimes against humanity
- **Tokyo Tribunal (1946-48)** — Applied Nuremberg principles to Pacific theater
- **ICTY (1993-2017)** — Prosecuted war crimes, genocide, crimes against humanity in former Yugoslavia
- **ICTR (1994-2015)** — Prosecuted genocide in Rwanda
- **Sierra Leone Special Court (2002-13)** — Prosecuted Charles Taylor for war crimes
- **ICC (2002-present)** — Ongoing prosecution of international crimes
- **Kadi v. Treasury (2008)** — EU courts struck down UN sanctions without due process
