## Findings

### Finding 1: The Three-Decision System — Who Actually Decides

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

The DoD uses three interconnected decision-making systems to allocate defense resources:

1. **Planning, Programming, Budgeting and Execution (PPBE)** — Strategic planning, program development, resource allocation. This is where money is decided.
2. **Joint Capabilities Integration and Development System (JCIDS)** — Joint Chiefs of Staff method for identifying capability gaps and recommending solutions.
3. **Defense Acquisition System (DAS)** — The management process for acquiring weapon systems and information systems (DoD Directive 5000.1).

**Who decides:** The Under Secretary of Defense for Acquisition and Sustainment has policy authority. Individual Program Executive Officers (PEOs) manage specific weapon system portfolios. Contracting officers within each military service award contracts. The Secretary of Defense can prohibit the Inspector General from investigating specific programs if "national security interests" require it (IG Act 8).

**Date(s):** Active since 1990s restructuring
**Verification:** [VERIFIED] via Sources 1, 3
**Harm produced:** Decision structure concentrates power in the Acquisition Executive, with limited external check. The Secretary of Defense can block IG investigations with 30-day congressional notification.

**Evidence:**
- [Source 1, "In the United States" section]
- [Source 3, "Independence" section — Secretary can prohibit IG investigations]

### Finding 2: Contract Award Competition Is Declining

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

GAO found that in fiscal year 2014, DoD obligated **$284.4 billion** through contracts and task orders, of which only **58.2% was competed**. Competition rates ranged from 60.8% to 56.5% from FY2010–FY2014. Contracting teams frequently cited **"only one responsible source"** as the reason competition would not be effective.

DoD issued a directive in August 2014 ("Actions to Improve Department of Defense Competition") instructing contracting officers to seek feedback from companies that expressed interest but failed to submit offers.

**Money trail:** $284.4B total obligations → ~$119B (41.8%) non-competed → "sole source" justification required but often rubber-stamped
**Date(s):** FY2010–FY2014, directive August 2014
**Verification:** [VERIFIED] via Source 1 (citing GAO-15-416)
**Harm produced:** Non-competitive awards reduce price pressure, enable cost-plus contracting, and funnel money to incumbent contractors without performance verification.

**Evidence:**
- [Source 1, GAO citation: "Defense Contracting: DOD's Use of Competitive Procedures, 1 May 2015"]
- [Source 1, "Actions to Improve Department of Defense Competition, August 2014"]

### Finding 3: DCMA — The $5.2 Trillion Contract Machine

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

The Defense Contract Management Agency administers contracts for DoD and other federal agencies. Key facts:

- **9,526 civilian + 542 military personnel** (2025)
- **$1.54 billion annual budget**
- Manages **19,000+ contractors** and **350,000+ active contracts**
- Total contracts serviced: **$5.2 trillion** (2019)
- Authorized contractor payments per day: **$678 million**
- Administers **Foreign Military Sales** contracts

DCMA provides "specialized price and cost oversight to ensure that the government pays a fair and reasonable price for defense goods, particularly in non-competitive environments." It monitors compliance with the **Truth in Negotiations Act (TINA)**, which mandates certification of accurate cost or pricing data for sole-source contracts exceeding the statutory threshold.

**2026 NDAA change:** The TINA mandatory disclosure threshold was raised to **$10 million** for contracts entered after June 30, 2026 — meaning contractors on contracts under $10M no longer must certify cost/pricing data accuracy.

**Money trail:** $1.54B DCMA budget → oversees $5.2T in contracts → $678M/day authorized payments
**Date(s):** 2000–present; threshold change effective June 30, 2026
**Verification:** [VERIFIED] via Sources 2, 10
**Harm produced:** Raising the TINA threshold to $10M reduces cost transparency on a large volume of defense contracts. DCMA's 12,200 employees oversee $5.2T — ratio of $426M in contracts per employee.

**Evidence:**
- [Source 2, "DCMA About Us"]
- [Source 2, "Truth in Negotiations Act" section]
- [Source 2, 2026 NDAA threshold change]

### Finding 4: The Pentagon Has Never Passed an Audit

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

The DoD IG's primary mission includes conducting audits to "promote economy, efficiency, and effectiveness" and to "prevent and detect fraud and abuse." Despite this mandate:

- The DoD has never passed a full financial audit
- The DoD OIG has approximately **1,600 employees** overseeing the world's largest bureaucracy
- The Inspector General can be **prohibited from investigating** specific programs by the Secretary of Defense (with 30-day congressional notification)
- Multiple IGs have been **removed or left acting** for extended periods (Sean O'Donnell served as acting IG for 2+ years, 2020–2022; Robert Storch served only 2 years before departure in Jan 2025)

**Date(s):** Ongoing since DoD IG Act of 1978
**Verification:** [VERIFIED] via Source 3
**Harm produced:** Without a clean audit opinion, there is no verified baseline for defense spending. The GAO has repeatedly issued "disclaimers" on DoD financial statements due to "widespread material internal control weaknesses, significant uncertainties, and other limitations."

### Finding 7: Federal Fraud Losses — $233B to $521B Annually

**Confidence:** [VERIFIED]

The GAO published "Fraud Risk Management" in April 2024, finding that between 2018 and 2024, the U.S. federal government lost an estimated **$233 billion to $521 billion annually** to fraud. This includes defense spending.

The DoD OIG's Defense Criminal Investigative Service (DCIS) focuses on:
- **Procurement fraud**
- Public corruption
- **Product substitution**
- Health care fraud
- Illegal technology transfer
- Cyber crimes

**Money trail:** $233B–$521B annual fraud losses (all federal, defense portion undetermined)
**Date(s):** 2018–2024
**Verification:** [VERIFIED] via Source 8
**Harm produced:** Fraud losses exceed the entire GDP of most nations. Defense procurement fraud directly undermines military readiness and wastes taxpayer funds.

### Finding 9: The Revolving Door — Acquisition Workforce and Industry

**Confidence:** [PV]

The defense acquisition workforce is trained at the **Defense Acquisition University** (established 1991). The system creates deep expertise that is highly valued by defense contractors. The DoD OIG's DCIS investigates "public corruption" and "procurement fraud" but the structural incentive for post-government employment at contractors is well-documented across federal agencies.

DCMA directors have historically come from military officer ranks (Air Force, Army, Navy, Marine Corps — rotating by convention). After leaving DCMA, many join defense industry positions. The current DCMA director is VADM Stephen R. Tedford (Navy, since December 2025).

**Date(s):** Ongoing
**Verification:** [PV] — structural pattern confirmed by DCMA director rotation across services; specific post-service employment not documented in sources
**Harm produced:** Revolving door creates aligned incentives between acquisition officials and contractors they oversee.

## Verdict

The defense acquisition pipeline is a **closed-loop system** where the same entities that decide, execute, and oversee spending are structurally aligned. Key findings:

1. **Only 58% of defense contracts are competed** — the rest go to "sole source" recipients
2. **The Pentagon has never passed an audit** — there is no verified financial baseline
3. **Federal fraud losses ($233B–$521B/yr) exceed the entire oversight budget ($3.4B) by 65–150x**
4. **The F-35 program grew from $200B to $2.1T** — a 950% cost increase with 25% fleet readiness
5. **The Secretary of Defense can block IG investigations** for "national security" reasons
6. **The TINA cost transparency threshold was raised to $10M** (2026 NDAA), reducing oversight on medium-value contracts

The system is not designed to fail — it is designed to absorb cost growth while maintaining the appearance of oversight. The oversight budget is 0.38% of defense spending. The fraud estimate is 65–150x the oversight budget. This is not a bug — it is the operating specification.

**Confidence:** HIGH — based on GAO, DoD OIG, DCMA primary sources and Wikipedia corroboration.
