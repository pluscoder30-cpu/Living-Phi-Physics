# THE AIR POISONING CHAIN

**Industrial Air Pollution, Health Effects, Who Profits, Who Pays**

Research compiled: 2026-08-21 | Investigation Agent 18

---

## Executive Summary

Air pollution is the second leading risk factor for early death globally, responsible for **7.9 million deaths in 2023** -- roughly 1 in 8 deaths worldwide. The economic cost reaches **$5.7-8.1 trillion annually** (4.8-6.1% of global GDP). A small number of industrial facilities and corporations generate the vast majority of toxic air emissions, while marginalized communities -- particularly Black, Indigenous, and people of color (BIPOC) -- bear disproportionate health burdens. The Clean Air Act framework provides enforcement tools, but penalties remain far below the damage inflicted, and polluters routinely externalize costs onto taxpayers and vulnerable populations.

- Respiratory diseases (asthma, COPD, lung cancer)
- Cardiovascular diseases (ischemic heart disease, stroke)
- Type 2 diabetes
- Adverse reproductive outcomes
- Neurodegeneration and brain development problems
- Dementia
- Skin aging

### Cummins: Largest Clean Air Act Penalty in History

The Cummins settlement (January 2024) represents:
- **$1.642 billion** in Clean Air Act penalties (largest ever)
- **$33 million** in California state law penalties
- Nearly **1 million vehicles** affected (2013-2023 RAM 2500/3500 diesels)
- Software designed to **cheat emission tests** while increasing NOx during normal driving
- **630,000 vehicles** with defeat device software; 330,000 with undisclosed software

*Source: EPA FAQ, Cummins Violation of CAA (2024)*

### Clean Air Act Penalty Framework

| Violation Type | Max Civil Penalty (per day/violation) | Criminal Penalty |
|---------------|--------------------------------------|-----------------|
| Stationary source violations | $124,426/day | Up to 5 years imprisonment |
| Mobile source violations | $45,268/vehicle | Up to 5 years imprisonment |
| Tampering/defeat devices | $4,527/event | Up to 2 years imprisonment |
| Knowing endangerment | -- | Up to **15 years** imprisonment |
| Negligent endangerment | -- | Up to 1 year imprisonment |

*Source: EPA Criminal Provisions of the Clean Air Act; 42 U.S.C. Sec. 7413*

### The Penalty Gap

Despite these figures, penalties are **trivially small** compared to:
- The **$5.7 trillion** annual global health cost of PM2.5
- Corporate revenues (e.g., Cummins 2023 revenue: ~$34 billion; penalty = ~5% of one year's revenue)
- The **$886 billion** in estimated annual U.S. damages from anthropogenic PM

EPA enforcement factors include: size of business, economic impact, compliance history, duration of violation, gravity of violation, and economic benefit of noncompliance. Penalties rarely approach statutory maximums.

*Source: EPA Penalty Framework; Hunton Andrews Kurth (2021); PNAS (2019)*

## IV. ENVIRONMENTAL JUSTICE: Air Pollution Inequality

### The Core Finding

**People of color in the United States are exposed to disproportionately high levels of PM2.5 from nearly all major emission sources.** This is systemic, not localized.

### Exposure Disparities by Race/Ethnicity (U.S.)

| Group | Avg PM2.5 Exposure | Relative to Population Average | Sources Causing Disproportionate Exposure |
|-------|-------------------|-------------------------------|------------------------------------------|
| Population average | 6.5 ug/m3 | -- | -- |
| People of Color (all) | 7.4 ug/m3 | **+14%** | 75% of source types |
| Black Americans | 7.9 ug/m3 | **+21%** | 78% of source types |
| Hispanic Americans | 7.2 ug/m3 | **+11%** | 87% of source types |
| Asian Americans | 7.7 ug/m3 | **+18%** | 73% of source types |
| White Americans | 5.9 ug/m3 | **-8%** | 40% of source types |

*Source: Tessum et al., Science Advances (2021)*

### Most Inequitable Emission Sectors

Four sectors consistently drive the largest racial-ethnic exposure disparities:
1. **Industry** -- largest contributor to both exposure and disparity
2. **Light-duty gasoline vehicles**
3. **Construction**
4. **Heavy-duty diesel vehicles**

Additional high-disparity sectors:
- **Residential gas combustion** -- 41% relative disparity for POC
- **Commercial cooking** -- 35% relative disparity for POC

*Source: Tessum et al. (2021)*

### Race is a Stronger Predictor Than Income

Key findings from peer-reviewed research:
- Racial disparities in PM2.5 exposure are **NOT simply a proxy for economic-based disparities**
- POC at **every income level** are disproportionately exposed by the majority of sources
- The difference in average exposure between POC and Whites is **2.4x larger** than the range in average POC exposure among income levels
- Black higher-income persons may benefit **more** from lower PM2.5 than White higher-income persons
- **Structural racism** (not individual behavior) drives the disparity

*Source: NEJM (2023); Science Advances (2021)*

### Historical Roots: Redlining and Pollution

- 1930s HOLC "redlining" graded neighborhoods; Black/immigrant areas marked "hazardous"
- 2022 study found **consistent, nearly monotonic association** between HOLC grade and current pollution levels
- NO2 levels showed **>50% increments** between highest (Grade A) and lowest (Grade D) neighborhoods
- Intraurban disparities by historical HOLC grade were **substantially larger** than by race/ethnicity alone

*Source: Annual Reviews (2026); Lane et al. (2022)*

### State-Level Structural Racism and Air Pollution

- Tracts in states with **higher levels of state-level Black-white gaps** report greater environmental health risk from outdoor air toxics
- The state racism index explains **4-10% of county- and state-level variation** in carcinogenic and respiratory risk from air toxics
- Systematic inequalities in environmental regulation, housing, and incarceration drive placement of pollution sources

*Source: Journal of Racial and Ethnic Health Disparities (2022)*

### Geographic Concentration of Disparities

| Region | Key Finding |
|--------|-------------|
| 45 of 48 U.S. states | Disproportionately exposing sources cause majority of POC exposure |
| Average urban area | 73% of POC exposure from sources that disproportionately expose POC |
| Average rural area | 62% of POC exposure from disproportionate sources |
| Industrial sector | Largest contributor to remaining disparities in nearly all states (2019) |
| Agriculture | Large contributor to disparities, historically under-regulated |
| Residential energy | Major contributor, especially in Pacific NW and New England |

*Source: Science Advances (2024); Tessum et al. (2021)*

### Disparate Siting: Who Breathes What

- **Black Americans produce proportionally less air pollution than White Americans, but breathe more of it**
- Roadways and pollution-emitting facilities are **disproportionately built in Black or low-income communities**
- This pattern holds in both urban and rural areas
- Legacy of racist housing policy + current facility siting = persistent inequality

*Source: NEJM (2023); Annual Reviews (2026)*

### Taxpayer Cleanup Costs

- U.S. Superfund program: **$3.5 billion** allocated from Infrastructure Investment and Jobs Act (2021)
- FY2022: $1.026 billion obligated, 49 sites cleared
- FY2023: $1.032 billion obligated, 22 new cleanup projects
- FY2024: $1+ billion for cleanup, 25 new projects
- Superfund chemical taxes reinstated in 2022 at **doubled rates** (expire 2031)

*Source: EPA Superfund Tax Operating Plan (2024)*

### Healthcare Costs from Air Pollution

| Country | Cost | Period |
|---------|------|--------|
| United Kingdom | **5.56 billion GBP** | 2017-2025 |
| Leicester, UK (one city) | **873,000+ GBP/year** per standard deviation PM10 increase | 2024 study |
| France | **13 billion EUR** savings possible if PM2.5 kept below 3 ug/m3 | 2018 data |
| United States | **13% increase** in asthma/COPD spending from higher PM2.5 | 2019 study |
| China | **8.09% decline** in health expenditure from 2015-2017 clean air action | 98 cities |

*Source: State of Global Air/2025*

### Cost-Benefit of Air Pollution Control

- **69% of studies** report benefits of control strategies exceed costs
- Source reduction is most commonly employed (n=42 studies)
- Benefits framework: environmental, ecological, social + health
- Transport regulations are the exception: only 3 of 13 studies found positive outcomes

*Source: Global Health Research and Policy (2024)*

### Marginal Damage Variability

- The **top 1% most harmful primary PM2.5 emissions** cause damages of **>$900,000/ton** -- every 5 tons causes one premature death
- The **least harmful 1%** cause **$4,200/ton** -- a **400-fold difference**
- Within a single county, damages can vary **56-127 fold** depending on emission location
- **10% of emissions** account for **54% of total primary PM2.5 damages**

*Source: PNAS (2019)*

## VI. THE PROFIT CALCULUS: Who Benefits, Who Bears the Cost

### The Asymmetry

The air poisoning chain follows a consistent pattern:

1. **Corporations profit** from polluting activities (energy, manufacturing, agriculture, transportation)
2. **Health costs are externalized** to communities, taxpayers, and the healthcare system
3. **Enforcement penalties** are small relative to profits and damages
4. **Marginalized communities** bear the heaviest health burden
5. **Taxpayers** fund cleanup, healthcare, and regulatory enforcement

### Who Profits

| Sector | Mechanism | Estimated Annual Revenue/Value |
|--------|-----------|-------------------------------|
| Fossil fuel power generation | Coal/gas combustion | Hundreds of billions |
| Petroleum refining | Oil processing | Hundreds of billions |
| Chemical manufacturing | Industrial processes | Hundreds of billions |
| Agriculture (livestock/fertilizer) | Ammonia emissions | Hundreds of billions |
| Transportation | Vehicle emissions | Trillions |

### Who Pays

| Category | Who Pays | Mechanism |
|----------|---------|-----------|
| Health costs | Individuals, families, NHS/Medicare | Medical bills, lost productivity, premature death |
| Environmental cleanup | Taxpayers | Superfund, state/local cleanup programs |
| Regulatory enforcement | Taxpayers | EPA budget, enforcement operations |
| Ecosystem damage | Society at large | Crop losses, biodiversity loss, infrastructure damage |
| Climate costs | Current and future generations | Adaptation, extreme weather, sea level rise |

### The Mathematical Structure of the Chain

```
Corporate Profit = Revenue - Private Costs
Social Cost = Health Damages + Environmental Cleanup + Climate Impacts + Lost Productivity
Externalized Cost = Social Cost - Corporate Penalties

True Cost to Society = Corporate Profit + Externalized Cost
True Cost to Vulnerable Communities = Disproportionate Exposure x Higher Susceptibility x Lower Resources
```

The system is designed so that:
- **Profits are private** (shareholders, executives)
- **Costs are socialized** (taxpayers, communities, future generations)
- **Penalties are a cost of doing business** (often less than the economic benefit of noncompliance)
- **Vulnerable populations** absorb the worst effects with the fewest resources
