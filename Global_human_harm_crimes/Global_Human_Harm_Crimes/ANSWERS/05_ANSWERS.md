### Q1: What is the total market size of the K-12 science textbook market (not just higher ed)?

The US K-12 science instructional materials market is approximately **$1.2 billion** (Simba Information, 2022). The total US PreK-12 instructional materials market is **$10.6-12.9 billion** (Simba/Freedonia Group, 2024), with science representing roughly 9-11% of the total. The broader US K-12 instruction material market is estimated at **$7.4 billion** (GII Research, 2024).

Sources: PRNewswire 2022-11-28 (Simba K-12 Science Market); Freedonia Group/Simba 2024; GII Research 2024; Simba Publishing for PreK-12 2024-2025.

### Q6: Are there any textbook publishers that explicitly include alternative physics frameworks?

**No major publisher** includes alternative physics frameworks (systemic behavior physics, patterned, adaptive response, aether dynamics, etc.). All major K-12 and college physics textbooks follow the Standard Model curriculum. Some notable approaches exist but are pedagogical alternatives, not theoretical alternatives:

- ISLE Physics (Pearson, by Etkina et al.): Standard physics through inquiry-based methods
- Novare Science: Christian faith-based publisher, standard physics with theological overlay
- OpenSciEd: NGSS-aligned inquiry-based curriculum, standard physics content

The textbook oligopoly has no financial incentive to publish alternative physics frameworks.

Sources: ISLE Physics Network (islephysics.net); Novare Science (novarescienceandmath.com); Activate Learning OpenSciEd.

### Q7: What is the relationship between AAPT and state teacher certification boards?

AAPT influences but does not directly control state certification. The relationship operates through several channels:

1. **PhysTEC (Physics Teacher Education Coalition):** Joint effort of APS, AAPT, and AIP working with ~300 institutions to improve physics teacher preparation.

2. **AAPT Community on Teacher Preparation:** Influences policy within AAPT to foster improvement in physics teacher recruitment, preparation, and retention.

3. **State-level influence:** AAPT sections work on regional or state efforts related to certification/licensure, program requirements, and physics content tests.

4. **National Task Force on Teacher Education in Physics:** Recommended states replace general science certification with subject-specific endorsements � but this remains a recommendation, not implemented.

5. **Certification varies by state:** Each state has its own certification process. Some offer single-subject physics certification (e.g., Tennessee), others only general science certification (e.g., Colorado).

Sources: AAPT K12 Programs (aapt.org); AAPT Teacher Preparation Community; AAPT National Task Force Report; PhysTEC Conference 2018; NCTQ 2017.

### Q8: How does the AP Physics curriculum (College Board) differ from NGSS, and who controls AP content?

**Who controls AP content:** The **College Board** controls AP Physics curriculum. Course frameworks are developed by committees of college faculty and expert AP teachers.

**Key differences:**

| Aspect | AP Physics | NGSS |
|--------|-----------|------|
| Control | College Board (private nonprofit) | Achieve Inc. / National Research Council |
| Purpose | College-level course for credit/placement | K-12 standards for all students |
| Content depth | College-equivalent, deeper | High school level, broader |
| AP Physics 1 topics | Kinematics, Forces, Energy, Momentum, Rotation, Oscillations, Fluids | NGSS covers same plus chemistry, biology, earth science |
| AP Physics 2 topics | Thermodynamics, E&M, Circuits, Magnetism, Optics, Waves, Modern Physics | NGSS integrates across disciplines |
| Exam | Standardized national exam, scored 1-5 | No national exam; state/local assessments |
| Laboratory | 25% minimum hands-on time required | Three-dimensional learning integrated |
| Alignment | NGSS comparison charts exist but AP is independent | Framework developed separately |

The College Board periodically revises AP Physics to align with college courses. AP Physics 1&2 were revised for 2025-26 to align content, language, and skills across courses. NGSS comparison charts exist (nextgenscience.org) but AP does not follow NGSS � it follows college course equivalency.

Sources: College Board AP Physics 1 CED; College Board AP Physics 2; Next Generation Science Standards AP comparison; AP Central Course Audit; All Access College Board 2024-03-01.

### Q9: What is the total value of US agricultural subsidies flowing to the top 10 food companies (combining USDA procurement, crop insurance, disaster assistance, and state/local subsidies)?

Direct quantification of total subsidies to the top 10 food companies is not available in a single consolidated figure, but components include:

**USDA Food Procurement (FY2024):** $4.8 billion total, with top 25 vendors receiving 45% (~$2.1B). Tyson Foods alone received $238M (5% of total). The top 10 vendors received approximately $1.1-1.3B.

**Federal Crop Insurance (FY2024):** Total premium subsidies ~$9.9B. The top 10,000 livestock feeding operations received $2.4B from crop insurance (2018-2023).

**Disaster Assistance (2018-2023):** Top 10,000 livestock operations received $5.5B from CFAP, $1.8B from Dairy Margin Coverage, $1.2B from Market Facilitation Program.

**State/Local Tax Subsidies:** Over $500M in tax breaks to meat processing companies over 20 years. Cargill alone received $170M in direct subsidies plus $5.34B in loan/bailout value.

**Estimated total for top 10 food companies:** When combining USDA procurement (~$1.1-1.3B/yr to top 10), crop insurance subsidies (proportional share of ~$9.9B/yr), disaster assistance, and state/local subsidies, the annual total likely exceeds **$3-5 billion** flowing to the top 10 food companies across all programs. However, no single source aggregates this figure.

**Sources:** Food and Water Watch 2025-11-19 (USDA procurement); GAO-25-106347 (livestock subsidies); Good Jobs First Subsidy Tracker (state/local); OpenSubsidies 2024; USDA ERS Food Price Outlook.

### Q10: How do BlackRock, Vanguard, and State Street proxy votes affect corporate governance decisions at concentrated food companies?

The Big Three (BlackRock, Vanguard, State Street) constitute the largest shareholder in **88% of S&P 500 firms** and hold significant stakes in every major food company (5-10% ownership each).

**Key findings:**

1. **Coordinated voting:** The Big Three show remarkably high internal agreement in proxy voting. BlackRock has only 18 mixed votes per 100,000 proposals; Vanguard has only 6 per 100,000. This evidences centralized voting strategies.

2. **Management-friendly but with teeth:** They generally vote with management, but vote against management at director (re-)elections ~45-50% of the time when dissatisfied � suggesting they use voting power to ensure management attention.

3. **Private engagements:** BlackRock performed over 1,500 private engagements with companies in a single year. "Meetings behind closed doors can go further than votes against management" (Larry Fink, BlackRock CEO).

4. **ESG/climate activism antitrust case:** Texas and 12 other states sued BlackRock, State Street, and Vanguard alleging they used collective shareholdings to pressure coal companies to reduce output through climate initiatives (Net Zero Asset Managers Initiative, Climate Action 100+). Vanguard settled in February 2026 for $29.5M, agreeing to "strict passivity commitments" � not using shareholdings to dictate business strategy or push shareholder proposals.

5. **Anticompetitive risk:** Research shows common ownership by the Big Three may contribute to 3-11% increases in airline ticket prices (Azar et al.). The Texas v. BlackRock court found the states "plausibly alleged that defendants coordinated with one another" through shared participation in climate initiatives.

6. **Food company proxy votes:** BlackRock and Vanguard supported shareholder proposals for human rights due diligence at Tyson Foods (82% of independent shares voted in favor). They also voted for lobbying disclosure and climate reporting proposals at various food companies.

**Sources:** Harvard Law School Forum on Corporate Governance 2024-12-03; Reuters 2026-02-26 (Vanguard settlement); Cleary Antitrust Watch 2025-08-06; Cambridge Core "Hidden power of the Big Three" (Fichtner et al.); Kelley Drye 2026-03-04; Morningstar 2024-03-12; IASJ 2021-02-19 (Tyson Foods).

### Q11: What is the breakdown of USDA food procurement by foreign-owned vs domestic-owned companies?

**USDA does not report sufficient data to assess this breakdown.** The Congressional Research Service (CRS) explicitly states: "Data are not readily available to assess the possible implications of foreign-owned companies that produce and process food in the United States that may be USDA vendors."

Key known facts:
- JBS International (Brazilian-owned) was among USDA's Top 100 Contractors in FY2023 with $70.7M in foods and services
- USDA purchases must be of 100% domestic origin with limited exceptions
- The Buy American provision requires domestic sourcing, but does not require domestic ownership
- Foreign-owned companies operating in the US (JBS, which owns National Beef; Smithfield, owned by WH Group of China) can and do receive USDA contracts

The CRS notes this as a gap in available data: "USDA does not report sufficient data to assess the implications of foreign-owned companies."

**Sources:** CRS R48141 "Trends in USDA Procurement of U.S. Food and Agricultural Products" (2024-07-25); GAO-24-106602; AMS Fiscal Year Purchases by Vendor; Food and Water Watch 2025-11-19.

### Q12: How do commodity trader financial derivatives operations (74-76% of ADM/Bunge revenues) interact with physical grain markets?

The ABCD traders operate simultaneously in physical grain markets and financial derivatives markets, creating complex interactions:

**Scale of financial operations:** For ADM and Bunge, financial/derivatives operations account for approximately 74-76% of reported revenues. This reflects the financialization of commodity trading where price risk management and speculative activity dominate revenue figures.

**Key interactions documented:**

1. **Price discovery and volatility:** The EU Parliament (2024) found that "the financialisation of agricultural commodity markets has become a significant factor in price volatility." Financial players' increased activity in derivatives markets has raised concerns about excessive speculation contributing to price spikes.

2. **Disconnect from physical supply:** During the 2020-2022 food crisis, global wheat production remained relatively constant, yet wheat prices rose drastically. The volume of agricultural derivative trading increased dramatically. "There is a clear disconnect between the real world of wheat production and consumption" (Food and Water Watch 2026).

3. **Profits from volatility:** ADM's nominal gross profits rose 61% and Bunge's 87% in the five years following the 2020 crisis. Profit margins expanded (ADM from 6.07% to 7.23%, Bunge from 4.39% to 6.48%), suggesting these firms profited beyond cost pass-through.

4. **Hedging vs. speculation blur:** An LDC executive stated: "I don't consider that speculating at all. It's what's normally done in the norm of our business. It is our business." It is "nearly impossible to differentiate between hedging and speculating."

5. **Storage as leverage:** ADM and Bunge's combined self-reported storage capacity equals the combined annual wheat production of Canada. This physical storage position combined with derivatives trading creates potential for strategic supply management.

6. **Regulatory gap:** "Physical commodities traders are, in principle, not subject to any oversight" (Swiss government report, cited by Blas 2013). The EU Parliament recommended new MiFID II/MiFIR reporting obligations to disclose how much derivatives trading is hedging vs. speculative.

7. **Index fund contagion:** Research shows ADM stock responds to financial market movements by >30%. The MOO agribusiness ETF shows cross-volatility spillovers of up to 72% with ADM shares. Financial speculation via ETFs has intensified price volatility in commodity futures.

**Sources:** EU Parliament Study IPOL_STU(2024)747276; MDPI Journal of Risk and Financial Management 2024-12-03; Food and Water Watch 2026-08-01; Canadian Food Studies article on ABCD firms; Taylor & Francis "Taming Transaction Costs" 2025-09-15.

### Q13: What is the total cost to US consumers of limited competition in beef, pork, and poultry markets?

**Direct quantification is difficult, but evidence of consumer harm is substantial:**

1. **Beef prices at record highs:** Average retail beef price set a record in April 2026 at $9.64/lb, up 13% year-over-year. Beef and veal prices predicted to increase 10.7% in 2026 (USDA ERS). The US cattle herd is at its lowest level in 75 years.

2. **Price transmission asymmetry:** Research shows that when farm prices rise, retail prices increase, but when farm prices fall, retail prices do not decrease proportionally � a classic sign of market power in the supply chain.

3. **Meatpacking margins:** The Big Four beef packers (85% concentration) have maintained high margins even as cattle prices surged. The Agri Stats antitrust case (settled 2026) revealed that data-sharing allowed meat processors to inflate prices � turkey processor margins tripled between 2013-2016.

4. **Consumer food prices overall:** Food prices are roughly 26% higher than five years ago (CoBank 2026). While inflation is a major driver, the FTC found food retailers hiked prices beyond inflation during COVID, with grocery prices rising 21% in three years while major chain revenue spiked to 36%.

5. **Farmer share declining:** Farmers' share of each dollar spent on food has dropped to 15.9% (Minnesota Farmers Union), suggesting market power concentrates margins at the processing/retail level.

6. **Estimated consumer cost:** A definitive aggregate figure for the "cost of limited competition" in US meat markets is not published. However, the combination of:
   - Record beef prices ($9.64/lb average, up 13% YoY)
   - Big Four beef packers controlling 85% of slaughter
   - Agri Stats data-sharing inflating processor margins
   - Farm-to-retail price spreads widening
   
   suggests consumers pay a significant premium � potentially **hundreds of dollars per household annually** � due to concentrated market power. The USDA ERS Meat Price Spreads data tracks these margins but does not publish a single "cost of concentration" figure.

**Sources:** USDA ERS Food Price Outlook (2026-07-24); AP News 2026-05-08 (Agri Stats settlement); Minnesota Farmers Union/MinnpPost 2026-06-02; CoBank 2026-08-13; American Farm Bureau Federation 2026; USDA ERS Meat Price Spreads.
