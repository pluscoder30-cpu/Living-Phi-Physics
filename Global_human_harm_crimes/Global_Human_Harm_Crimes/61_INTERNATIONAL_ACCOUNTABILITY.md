## Executive Summary

The international accountability architecture consists of **eight primary mechanisms** operating across roughly **200+ participating jurisdictions**. These mechanisms coordinate through overlapping treaty obligations, peer review, and mutual recognition — but face structural enforcement gaps where powerful states decline participation or impose counter-pressure.

---

## Part 1: International Criminal Court (ICC)

### Structure
- **Treaty:** Rome Statute (1998, entered into force 2002)
- **Seat:** The Hague, Netherlands
- **Member States:** 124 States Parties (as of 2025)
- **Non-member major powers:** US, Russia, China, India, Israel

### Key Facts
- **32 outstanding arrest warrants** known to public (as of OTP Annual Report 2025)
- **17 warrants issued 2023-2025** — meaningful increase in output
- Operating investigations in **16 countries** worldwide
- Budget mismatch: workload vs. resources "severely hampered" ability to deliver (HRW, 2024)

### Member State Coordination
- **Assembly of States Parties (ASP)**: Annual governing body, sets budget, elects officials
- **79 member states** issued joint statement (Feb 2025) reaffirming "unwavering support" in response to US sanctions
- **93 member states** expressed support (Dec 2024) against US congressional sanctions efforts
- States Parties obligated under Rome Statute to **cooperate with Court** and ensure national laws enable fulfillment

### US Opposition
- **Executive Order (Feb 6, 2025):** Trump authorized sanctions against ICC officials (asset freezes, travel restrictions)
- Targeted court's investigation of Israeli PM Netanyahu and former Defense Minister Yoav Gallant
- **American Servicemembers' Protection Act (2002):** US considers protected persons under this act
- US demanded ICC drop Palestine and Afghanistan investigations as condition for lifting sanctions
- US also demanded Rome Statute amendment to prohibit prosecution of non-signatory state nationals — ASP rejected this

### Key Countries in ICC Accountability

## Part 2: Universal Jurisdiction (UJ)

### Overview
- **UJAR 2026:** 34 new cases opened/made public in 2025; 23 convictions secured
- **UJAR 2025:** 36 new cases in 2024; 27 convictions (nearly double 2023)
- **95 extraterritorial cases** prosecuted across **16 countries** (2024)
- **16 countries** actively exercising universal jurisdiction

### Coordination Mechanisms
- **Joint Investigation Teams (JITs):** Coordinated through Eurojust; MH17 model replicated for Ukraine
- **Atrocity Crimes Advisory Group (ACA):** US, UK, EU established for Ukraine (May 2022)
- **Ljubljana-Hague Convention (2023):** 68 states agreed on text for international cooperation on investigation/prosecution of genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes

### Structural Limitations
- **Diplomatic immunity:** Heads of state/diplomats protected unless immunity waived
- **State reluctance:** UJ can harm state-to-state relations
- **Resource dependence:** Effectiveness depends on specialized units and adequate resources
- **Power dynamics:** "Effectiveness is shaped by power dynamics" (UJAR 2026)
- **Double standards:** US sanctions against ICC "risk entrenching double standards" (ECCHR)

## Part 3: UNCAC Implementation Review Mechanism

### Structure
- **Treaty:** UN Convention against Corruption (2003, entered into force 2005)
- **Parties:** 191 States Parties — near-global coverage
- **Secretariat:** UNODC (UN Office on Drugs and Crime)
- **Review body:** Implementation Review Group (all States Parties)

### How It Works
1. **Self-assessment:** State under review completes comprehensive checklist
2. **Peer review:** Two reviewing states (one from same regional group) conduct assessment
3. **Country visit:** Direct dialogue during on-site visit
4. **Output:** Executive summary + full country review report
5. **Follow-up:** Identifies good practices, gaps, technical assistance needs

### Review Cycles

| Cycle | Period | Chapters Covered | Focus |
|-------|--------|-----------------|-------|
| First | 2010-2015 | III & IV | Criminalization, law enforcement, international cooperation |
| Second | 2015-present | II & V | Preventive measures, asset recovery |
| Third | In preparation | TBD | New phase being designed |

### Key Statistics
- 370 organizations from 111 countries urged strengthening of IRM (June 2025)
- Mechanism has reviewed implementation across all 191 parties
- Changed "the landscape of anti-corruption efforts" — UNODC assessment

### Coordination Principles
- **Non-adversarial and non-punitive:** No form of ranking
- **Peer-driven:** States learn from and help each other
- **Technical nature:** Focus on implementation, not enforcement
- **Complementary:** Works alongside OECD, GRECO, and other mechanisms

### Countries Reviewed (Examples)

| Country | Review Cycle | Reviewing States | Year |
|---------|-------------|------------------|------|
| Sweden | 1st cycle | France, Canada | 2012-2014 |
| Sweden | 2nd cycle | Belgium, Lesotho | 2019-2022 |
| US | 1st cycle | (DOJ Public Integrity Section lead) | 2012 |

### How Countries Get Greylisted

4. Country placed on grey list with **action plan**
5. Regular progress reviews at plenary sessions
6. **On-site verification** before removal

## Part 7: Mutual Legal Assistance Treaties (MLATs)

### Structure
- **Purpose:** Enable law enforcement to obtain evidence, information, testimony from abroad
- **Format:** Bilateral or multilateral treaties
- **Mechanism:** "Central Authority" in each country (for US: DOJ Office of International Affairs)
- **Scope:** Criminal investigations and prosecutions

### US MLAT Network (as of April 2022)

| Category | Examples |
|----------|---------|
| Bilateral MLATs | ~70 treaties in force |
| Multilateral conventions | Budapest Convention (cybercrime), OAS MLAT, OECD conventions |
| Executive agreements | Additional binding instruments |

### Key MLAT Partners

| Country | Treaty Type | Notes |
|---------|------------|-------|
| Switzerland | Bilateral (1977) | First modern US MLAT — pierced Swiss bank secrecy |
| Germany | Bilateral (2003) | Broad scope: "any other form of assistance not prohibited" |
| UK | Bilateral | Extensive cooperation |
| Canada | Bilateral | Close cooperation via Five Eyes |
| Japan | Bilateral | — |
| Brazil | Bilateral (2001) | — |
| Ireland | Bilateral (2001) | — |
| India | Bilateral | 42 MLATs total (as of 2019) |

### MLAT Process
1. Requesting state's Central Authority sends formal request
2. Requested state's Central Authority reviews for treaty compliance
3. Evidence gathered according to **requested state's law** (not requesting state's)
4. Evidence returned in form admissible in requesting state's courts
5. Constitutional protections of requested state apply

### Limitations
- **Extremely slow:** Can take months or years
- **Resource intensive:** Significant government lawyer time
- **Dual criminality** often required
- **Political exceptions** may apply
- **Informal channels** often used alongside or instead of formal MLATs

### Informal Cooperation
- "Significant amount of criminal investigation-related information is exchanged through informal channels"
- Investigator-to-investigator, prosecutor-to-prosecutor
- Can be "so informal and 'off the grid' that law enforcement agencies, courts, and defendants may only learn of them by accident"

### Modern Alternatives
- **CLOUD Act (US, 2018):** Allows US law enforcement to compel US-based tech companies for data stored abroad
- **EU E-Evidence Regulation:** Cross-border electronic evidence framework
- **Second Additional Protocol to Budapest Convention:** Enhanced cybercrime cooperation

## Part 8: Extraterritorial Jurisdiction

### US Extraterritorial Reach

| Statute | Scope | Key Feature |
|---------|-------|-------------|
| FCPA (Foreign Corrupt Practices Act) | Anti-bribery + accounting | Applies to US persons, issuers, anyone acting in US territory |
| Alien Tort Statute | Human rights violations | Civil claims for international law violations |
| Torture statute (18 USC §2340) | Torture abroad | Extraterritorial jurisdiction; 2nd conviction (Roggio) |
| RICO | Organized crime | Can apply to foreign conduct with US nexus |
| Money laundering statutes | Financial crimes | Broad extraterritorial reach |
| Sanctions (OFAC) | Sanctions evasion | Secondary sanctions on non-US persons |

### FCPA Enforcement Trends
- **50% of corporate defendants** (2015-2024) were foreign companies
- **62% of individual defendants** were foreign nationals
- **Executive Order (Feb 10, 2025):** Trump paused FCPA enforcement for 180 days
- New investigations require "specific authorization by the Attorney General"
- Review of past FCPA actions for potential "remedial measures"

### EU Extraterritorial Mechanisms

| Mechanism | Scope | Year |
|-----------|-------|------|
| EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive | Supply chain human rights/environmental due diligence | 2024, amended 2025-2026 |
| EU Blocking Statute | Shields EU operators from extraterritorial US sanctions | Active |
| EU Autonomous Sanctions | Russia, Syria, others — not replicated at UN level | Ongoing |

### How Extraterritorial Jurisdiction Interacts with UJ
- UJ: Prosecution of intl crimes regardless of where committed or nationality of perpetrator/victim
- Extraterritorial jurisdiction: Prosecution based on statute's reach beyond national borders
- Overlap: FCPA, torture statute, and war crimes statutes can create parallel jurisdiction
- Coordination: JITs, MLATs, and informal cooperation manage competing claims

## Accountability Gap Analysis

### Where Accountability Exists

| Mechanism | Coverage | Enforcement | Effectiveness |
|-----------|----------|-------------|---------------|
| ICC | 124 states | Moderate | Growing — 32 warrants, 17 in 2023-2025 |
| Universal Jurisdiction | 16+ countries | Active | 27 convictions in 2024 (double 2023) |

## Verdict

**[VERIFIED]** The international accountability architecture is extensive in design but uneven in execution. The ICC has 124 members and is growing operational capacity (32 warrants, 16 situations). Universal jurisdiction is expanding — 27 convictions in 2024 across 16 countries, with new jurisdictions like Portugal joining. UNCAC reaches 191 states through peer review. However, the most powerful states (US, Russia, China) remain outside ICC jurisdiction, US sanctions actively undermine the court, OECD enforcement has declined to only 2 active enforcers, and UNSC vetoes block accountability for allies. The system functions through overlapping mechanisms that compensate for each other's gaps, but power dynamics fundamentally shape who is held accountable and who is not.
