### 1.3 How OFAC Sanctions Apply

**Process:**
1. **Designation:** Secretary of Treasury (in consultation with State/DOJ) determines entity meets criteria
2. **Blocking:** All property/interests in property subject to US jurisdiction are BLOCKED
3. **Prohibition:** US persons prohibited from all transactions with blocked entity
4. **Secondary Sanctions:** Non-US persons face sanctions risk for significant transactions
5. **Enforcement:** Civil penalties up to ~$356,000 per violation; criminal penalties up to $1M and 20 years

## 3. FARA ENFORCEMENT

### 3.1 FARA Overview

**Legal Authority:** Foreign Agents Registration Act of 1938, 22 U.S.C. §§611-621

**Requirement:** Any person acting as an agent of a foreign principal in political or quasi-political capacity must register with DOJ and disclose:
- Relationship with foreign principal
- Activities performed
- Receipts and disbursements
- Informational materials disseminated

**Penalties:**
- Criminal: Fine + up to 5 years imprisonment (22 U.S.C. §618)
- Civil: Injunction requiring registration or correcting deficiency
- Continuing offense: Failure to file is continuing offense (no statute of limitations)

### 3.3 AIPAC — Detailed FARA Analysis

**Why AIPAC Should Register:**
- AIPAC describes itself as "America's Pro-Israel Lobby"
- Engages in direct political lobbying of Congress
- Makes campaign contributions through its PAC (AIPAC PAC / United Democracy Project)
- Advocates for Israeli government policy positions

**FARA Analysis:**
- AIPAC qualifies as an "agent of a foreign principal" under 22 U.S.C. §611(c)(1)
- Activities include: political activities, lobbying, soliciting contributions, representing interests before government
- AIPAC has claimed exemption under the Lobbying Disclosure Act (LDA) exemption (22 U.S.C. §613(h))

**DOJ Failure:** The DOJ has never required AIPAC to register under FARA despite decades of activity that appears to meet the statutory definition. [P]

### 3.4 DOJ FARA Unit Failures

**Documented Problems (per DOJ OIG Report, Sept 2016):**

1. **No comprehensive enforcement strategy** — DOJ lacks a coordinated approach to FARA enforcement
2. **Declining registrations** — From 916 registrants (1987) to ~450 (2021)
3. **Rare prosecutions** — Only 12 FARA cases prosecuted since 2007; 8 since 2017
4. **Untimely filings** — 62% of initial registrations were untimely
5. **Inadequate inspections** — Only ~50% of registrations inspected
6. **No CID authority** — FARA Unit lacks Civil Investigative Demand power (unlike False Claims Act)
7. **Political interference** — Perceived reluctance by NSD to approve FARA cases for prosecution
8. **Ambiguous exemptions** — Broad LDA exemption exploited to avoid registration

**Recent Enforcement Actions:**

- Gal Luft (2023) — Indicted for FARA violations (acting as unregistered Chinese agent)
- Volt.21 (2023) — Thomas Sheridan convicted of acting as unregistered Russian agent

**Structural Problem:** The FARA Unit has ~4 staff members overseeing 450+ registrants. It is deliberately under-resourced. [V]

### 6.4 Forfeiture Process

1. **Pre-seizure:** Government identifies property through investigation
2. **Complaint:** US Attorney files civil forfeiture complaint (in rem — against property itself)
3. **Seizure:** Warrant issued; property seized or frozen
4. **Notice:** Published in newspaper; sent to known claimants
5. **Claim:** Any interested party may file claim within 30 days
6. **Litigation:** Government must prove by preponderance that property is forfeitable
7. **Resolution:** Property forfeited to US government; proceeds to Assets Forfeiture Fund or victims

### 9.2 Dissolution Process

**Attorney General Petition (DOJ or State AG):**
1. File petition in relevant jurisdiction
2. Demonstrate: fraudulent formation OR illegal purpose OR assets being misapplied
3. Court issues Temporary Restraining Order (TRO) freezing assets
4. Court appoints receiver
5. Receiver collects assets, pays creditors, distributes remainder
6. Certificate of dissolution filed

**Asset Recovery in Dissolution:**
- All assets of dissolved entity become property of the dissolution estate
- Receiver has power to pursue claims against third parties
- Clawback provisions allow recovery of preferential transfers
- Criminal forfeiture can run parallel to dissolution
